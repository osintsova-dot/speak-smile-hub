🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use eight verbs of social media (post, tag, like, follow, share, comment on, take down, block) accurately and in the right form, and read a magazine article for opinion, purpose and inference — Cambridge Reading Part 3.
What is genuinely hard
The vocabulary is not hard to understand and is very hard to produce correctly, because the class knows all eight words as Russian borrowings with Russian grammar: запостить, лайкнуть, тегнуть. The English patterns are different in three places — comment ON something, take something DOWN (separable), block SOMEBODY — and none of them survive translation. The reading is hard for another reason: four of the five questions ask what the writer THINKS or is DOING, not what happened.
Typical error to expect
«in a fact», «in the general», «on advance» — the phrases with in are fixed chunks with no logic to reason from, and they are among the commonest distractors in the lexical section. They are learned whole or they are lost.
The verbs arrive with the wrong preposition: «comment my photo», «block from me», «take down it». Write the three patterns on the board before the exercise, not after it.
Why the lesson is built this way
The topic arrives as evidence, not as opinion: two 'before and after' photographs where the class has to find the changes themselves, then a teenager on the recording who tells the whole story of one photo using all eight verbs in order. That is why the vocabulary is heard inside a narrative before it is listed — the order of the verbs IS the story. The reading then does the thing this unit exists for: two fifteen-year-olds say out loud what airbrushed images do to them, one of them a boy, which is the sentence that changes the room. The exam questions are taken with a proof rule — underline the words in the TEXT that prove your letter — because at B1 the difference between a good and a poor reader is not vocabulary, it is whether the answer is checked against the line.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two photographs of the same person, taken thirty seconds apart. One of them is real. Today: the English for everything you do online — and an article where two fifteen-year-olds say what perfect pictures actually do to them.”

WHAT WE DO: About you + ex.1–2: find the changes, then hear the photo's story on track 50

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. About you in pairs, 90 seconds: Where and when do you take selfies? Describe your favourite selfie. Which photo apps do you use? Have you ever used #NoFilter with one of your photos — and what does the hashtag actually mean? Take two answers and let the last one land — most of them have used the hashtag and few can define it.
  2. ex.1, in pairs, 90 seconds: the two 'before and after' pairs. What exactly was changed? Push for specifics, not for «she looks better».
  3. Feed back with the words on the board: the two 'before and after' pairs: the girl is put in a different position, standing on a red carpet, and her skin tone is changed to match an outdoor location; the boy has his facial blemishes removed and his skin tone made darker. Useful words on the board: facial, position, situation.
  4. ex.2, track 50, played whole: which 'after' picture is she describing? Answer: A2. Ask what words decided it — and give no feedback yet, the Teacher's Book is right that it kills the next exercise.
  5. Thirty seconds of background, no more: photo manipulation is older than the class expects — it starts in the nineteenth century, within twenty years of the first photograph, with retouching in ink and paint. The famous case is National Geographic in 1982, which moved the pyramids closer together to fit a cover, and spent years answering the question of whether the magazine could still be trusted. Today the industry that manipulates hardest is advertising: cosmetics that improve a face, supplements that deliver a body

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4: the eight gaps, then the meanings, and the pattern column is filled

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Before playing: pairs look at the box of eight words and decide one thing only — nouns or verbs? (Verbs.) Then unpack the first line together: the red carpet is the carpet put down for the people arriving at an official event.
  2. ex.3, track 50 again with pauses, four minutes: eight gaps, in the order the events happened. Play a second time straight through for checking.

  6. Fast finishers write when they last did each of the eight things; collect two of these during the check.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 + the first read: the article taken whole, for its argument

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 first, books open at the photo, 90 seconds in pairs: does this woman look like this in real life? possible answer: no, she does not look like that in real life — and the message it sends readers is that people have to look perfect to be acceptable
  2. Six words on the board with pronunciation before they read — digital editing · retouch · manipulate · photoshop · reality · false — and leave them up.
  3. Five minutes, silent, whole text, one job only: what does the writer WANT? Not what happened — what does she want to change?
  4. Take the answers, then the two voices that matter, read aloud from the page: Shelly, fifteen, on forgetting that the legs and the skin are not real; and Corey, fifteen, on the muscles boys are supposed to have. Ask the room which of the two surprised them — it is usually the boy.
  5. Stronger students, while the others finish: work out from context body image, campaigning, scale and spreading messages, and be ready to teach them at the check.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2, Reading Part 3: five four-option questions, answered with evidence

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Name the format before they start: Five questions, four options, and they follow the order of the text. They ask what the writer thinks and what a paragraph is FOR — not only what it says.
  2. Do question 1 as a class, exactly as the Teacher's Book suggests: underline what separates the four options (A — not good results, B — celebrities, C — almost all photos, D — the equipment). Read paragraph one. Nominate one student for the letter AND for the line that proves it.
  3. Questions 2–5 individually, seven minutes, clock visible. The rule holds: underline the proof in the TEXT.
  4. Pairs compare — underlinings first, letters second. If the underlinings differ, that is the conversation, not the letters.
  5. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Reading Part 3, the five questions on «Don't airbrush me!»: 1 C (it is difficult to find a photo that has not been improved) · 2 B (research has shown the effect on body image) · 3 D (unfortunately … a long way to go) · 4 B (perhaps one day … again) · 5 A (the Girl Guides' own words about the badge). For each one, the proof line is read aloud before the letter is confirmed.
  6. ex.3 in pairs, two minutes: the five highlighted words. Then the stronger students teach the four they worked out during the reading.
📺 On screen: the five answers with their proof lines written up as they are confirmed — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: work alone with an underlined proof for every answer, then read the proof aloud before the letter

WHAT WE DO: the two discussion questions from the page, with the language on the wall

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set both questions and give the useful language: Should there be limits on how photos are changed in advertising? Why, or why not? Why do companies use models, celebrities or sportspeople to advertise their products? — pairs first, then the floor
  2. Kagan RallyRobin first, two minutes: partners alternate single reasons, no speeches.

  4. Bring it to the floor for three minutes and take the disagreement rather than the consensus — ask for the strongest argument AGAINST the room's majority view.

WHAT WE DO: a productive task that cannot be done without today's eight verbs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The task, written on the board: You have posted a photograph. Tell the story of what happened next — in six sentences, using six different verbs from the wall. It can be true or invented; nobody is asked which.
  2. Four minutes to write six sentences, alone, silent. Minimum six different verbs, correct patterns, and the pattern column is on the board for anyone who needs it.
  3. Kagan Timed Pair Share: 90 seconds each way, listener's job is to count the verbs on their fingers and to catch one wrong pattern.
  4. Change partners once and repeat — the second telling is always the better English, which is the point of doing it twice.
  5. Four stories to the floor. Then the closing question, thirty seconds each in pairs: If every airbrushed photo had a label on it, would anything change?
  6. Correction is deferred: collect five errors from the monitoring on the board, unattributed, and let the class repair them in the last minute.
📺 On screen: the six errors written up anonymously at the end — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: write six sentences, tell them twice to two different partners, and repair the class's errors together

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can describe what people do on social media with eight precise verbs in the correct pattern, and answer five opinion-and-purpose questions on a 400-word article, proving each answer from the text.
  • Subsidiary. Listening to a monologue for the sequence of events (track 50); guessing meaning from context (body image, campaigning, spreading messages); the weak forms of was and were, which is where the passive hides in tomorrow's lesson.
  • Personal. Open the block's WORD WALL in two columns — WHAT I DO (post, share, comment on, follow, tag) and WHAT I CAN DO ABOUT IT (block, take down, report). Insist on the pattern being written with the verb: comment ON, take something DOWN, block SOMEBODY.
By the end, students can…
  • tell the story of one photograph using eight social-media verbs
  • say comment on a post, take a photo down, block somebody — with the right pattern
  • answer a Reading Part 3 question and point at the line that proves it
  • say what airbrushing is and give one argument about it

Structure
I posted it and tagged Pippa. · People commented on it and asked me to take it down. · I block people like that. · In the first paragraph the writer says that…
Form
comment ON something · take something DOWN (separable: take it down, not take down it) · block SOMEBODY · tag SOMEBODY (in a photo) · follow SOMEBODY · share SOMETHING (with somebody).
Pron. watch
the eight social-media verbs are easy to say and hard to stress in a sentence: the class will put the accent on the pronoun (I TAGGED her) where English puts it on the new information · the phrases with in run together and the vowel of in reduces to a schwa: /ɪnfækt/, /ɪnəˈvɑːns/, /ɪnpəˈtɪkjələ/ — say them as one word or they sound like a list · in the passive, was and were are weak forms: /wəzˈteɪkən/, /wəˈʃəʊn/, which is why students do not hear the passive in the listening
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«She took the photo down.» Is the photo still online? → no. Now say it with it: took IT down — never took down it.
«I follow her» — do I know her? → not necessarily. «I blocked her» — can she see my posts now? → no.
Reading Part 3, question 2 asks about the PURPOSE of the paragraph. Is that the same as what it says? → no: what does the writer want this paragraph to do to me?

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Everyone knows the eight words already — in RussianThat is the point of the pattern column. Nobody is asked what post means; they are asked to say block SOMEBODY, comment ON something, take something DOWN. Mark the pattern on the wall in a different colour and check it in every answer.
The topic slides into a general chat about InstagramEvery opinion has to be attached to a line of the text or to a word from the wall. Thirty seconds of free reaction is planned for; anything longer is stopped with the timer, not with your voice.
Reading Part 3 answered from memory of the paragraphThe proof rule is not optional: no letter is accepted without the underlined words in the TEXT. When you check, the student reads the proof line aloud first and the letter second.
Weaker readers stall on the fourth paragraph (campaigners)Give them the four names before they read — Meghan Markle, Paloma Faith, the Girl Guides, the 'Free being me' badge — as a list on the board. The paragraph then becomes a matching job rather than a wall of new names.
Someone in the room is genuinely uncomfortable with the topicThe task is never personal: nobody is asked about their own body or their own photos. Every question in this lesson is about the pictures in the magazine, not about the people in the room. Keep it that way even if the class opens up.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 12–18 (multiple choice on a long text — the Cambridge Reading Part 3 done today is the same task with one option fewer) and tasks 32–38 (lexical choice).

Reading Part 3 asks for attitude, purpose and inference rather than facts, and that is exactly the half of the Russian reading section where marks are lost: the candidate finds the paragraph and then answers from general sense instead of from the line. The rule trained today — underline the words in the TEXT that prove the letter — transfers without any change. The social-media verbs are exam currency too: post, share, tag, follow and block print in the lexical section and are needed for the speaking card about the internet, which comes round every year.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
