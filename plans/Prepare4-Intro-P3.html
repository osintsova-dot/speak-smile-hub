🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Choose correctly between the present simple, present continuous, past simple, past continuous and present perfect — and between must / have to in the negative — in context, and say why in one sentence each time.
What is genuinely hard
Not the forms: all six were taught and tested last year. What is hard is the choice under pressure, because Russian marks these differences in different places — I have seen it last week feels fine to a Russian ear, and mustn't versus don't have to has no Russian trigger at all. Those two are the price of the lesson.
Typical error to expect
I have seen that film last week · You mustn't do the extra task (meaning «you don't have to») · Did you ever eat…? for an open experience question · we was watching. The first two will still be there in April if they are not named today.
Why the lesson is built this way
The grammar half is twenty-eight minutes and runs the short way round: one story with all six structures inside it, shelves the class fills itself, then an auction, then a mingle — no rule is presented, no table copied, because a reason you formulated yourself is the only kind usable under pressure. The second half is the Cambridge entry diagnostic for LEVEL 4 — a different paper from last year's, so nobody has seen a question of it. Its hundred questions walk the coursebook in order, exactly five per unit, so the finished paper is literally a map of the year with the student's own dots on it. That is why it is framed as a timetable and not a test, why nothing is marked, and why the papers go into an envelope that comes back out in the mock-exam week of May.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Six shelves of grammar. You built all six last year — today they go back on the wall in half an hour. Then something different: a hundred questions from Cambridge. Most of them are things nobody has taught you yet — and that is the point. Every question you cannot answer today is a lesson on our timetable between now and May. No mark, nothing in the register, and I keep the paper until the last week of May so that you can see what happened to you.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, the second error feedback of the year, and the bridge into the six shelves

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — feedback from lesson 2. Four sentences from your correction sheet, no names: I have visited Kazan last August. · You mustn't do the bonus task if you are tired. · We was watching a film at ten. · Did you ever try sushi?
  3. Kagan RallyCoach, two minutes: one pen between two, one writes the repair, the other says why. Then all four aloud, fast.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the lesson: Two of those four are today's whole lesson. By the end you will not just fix them — you will say WHY in one sentence.
📺 On screen: the four sentences in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair four sentences in pairs. Expect we was found instantly and you mustn't to be defended as correct — it IS correct in form, wrong in meaning, and that argument is the perfect doorway into today. Typical problem: repairs without reasons; make them say the reason

Lesson frame

Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write six shelf labels across the top of the board with space under each: ALWAYS · NOW · THEN · WAS HAPPENING · UP TO NOW · RULES.
  2. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: six shelves, all built last year — and the aim is not the forms, it is saying WHY, in one sentence, every time.
  3. Announce the second half honestly, in the words used all lesson: After the break there are a hundred questions. It is not a test, it is a map of the year — and I keep it until May.
  4. Set the bar: Every answer today comes with the word «because».
📺 On screen: the six shelf labels in chalk — they stay up all lesson and every example gets written under one of them, so the board is the medium and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: read the six labels and start guessing. Expect «UP TO NOW» to be identified fast (they drilled the present perfect all spring) and «RULES» to puzzle them until must/have to lands on it. Typical problem: Russian tense names called out; accept once, then insist on the six English labels

8–18′
Six shelves · the grammar of last year on its feet
class → pairs
Why: One story on the board with six structures inside it, and shelves the class fills itself. This is the fastest honest way back into the grammar of Prepare 3 — and the shelves stay on the wall of the year: the book will add to them unit by unit.

WHAT WE DO: one story on the board, six structures inside it, shelves filled by the class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Tell a forty-second true story about your own summer with all six inside, writing only the verbs as you go: I usually spend August in Norilsk. This year I went to Kazan. On the third day I was walking along the river when it started to rain. I have been to Kazan three times now — but I had to come home early. Right now I am looking at the photos and planning the next trip.
  2. Pairs, three minutes: put each verb on one of the six shelves and write the reason next to it. Nothing else.
  3. Take the reasons, not the answers, with Kagan Numbered Heads Together. The reasons are the lesson.
  4. Draw the two pictures that fix the hard pairs for the year: the timeline (a long line for was walking, a cross for started) and the arrow to now (have been three times — a bracket from then to today).
  5. For your own reference, the six in one table:
    ShelfWhat it is for · what gives it away
    Present simple — what is always or usually trueI get up at seven. She plays tennis on Fridays.
    usually · every day · on Mondays · never
    Present continuous — happening now, around now, or a fixed planI am doing my homework. We're playing in the final on Saturday.
    now · at the moment · this term · tonight
    Past simple — a finished action at a finished timeWe went to Kazan. She didn't like the film.
    yesterday · last summer · in 2024 · two days ago
    Past continuous — what was already going on when something else happenedI was reading when the phone rang.
    while · when · at six o'clock yesterday
    Present perfect — an experience or a state that reaches up to nowI have never broken a bone. We've lived here since 2019. She's just gone out.
    ever · never · just · already · yet · for · since
    must / have to — obligation — and the trap in the negativeYou must wear a helmet. I had to help at home. You don't have to come (= not necessary). You mustn't be late (= prohibited).
    rules · school · sport · parents
📺 On screen: the verbs, the timeline and the to-now bracket in chalk under the six labels — the board is the medium here and the drawings have to happen live, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: sort seven verbs and write a reason for each. Expect the two present shelves instant, the past pair fast, and the argument to live between THEN and UP TO NOW — exactly where it should. Typical error: have been explained as «it is more polite»; push to «it counts up to now»

18–27′
Grammar auction
teams of 3–4
Why: Correcting other people's sentences is faster and much safer than producing your own, and an auction makes the class argue about grammar with actual heat. Every sentence on the list is either real Prepare 3 grammar or a real Russian-speaker error — mostly both.

WHAT WE DO: ten sentences, some right and some wrong, bought with a budget

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Each team gets 100 points, bids in tens. Ten sentences go up one at a time; teams bid on the ones they believe are correct. Points come back — with interest — only if the sentence really is correct.
  2. The ten sentences, with what each is actually testing:
    • I have seen that film last week. → WRONG — last week is a finished time, so it is past simple: I saw that film last week. The single most common present perfect error in a Russian-speaking group.
    • She's just gone out — she'll be back at six. → RIGHT — just + present perfect, and gone (not been) because she is still out.
    • My brother has gone to London twice. → WRONG — twice means he came back: has been to London twice. The been/gone pair is Prepare 3 Unit 13.
    • We have lived in Norilsk since 2019. → RIGHT — a state that started then and reaches now: present perfect with since.
    • I must to wear a helmet at the skate park. → WRONG — must takes the bare infinitive: I must wear. Accept have to wear as an alternative repair.
    • You mustn't do the extra task — it's optional. → WRONG in meaning, right in form — mustn't = prohibited. Optional = you don't have to do it. This is the distinction the whole auction is fishing for.
    • While I was walking home, it started to rain. → RIGHT — a long action interrupted by a short one, and the two past tenses are in the right places.
    • Did you ever eat sushi? → WRONG for an open experience question — Have you ever eaten sushi? Accept the past simple only if the student adds a finished time (Did you eat sushi in Tokyo last year?).
    • Every day after school we are playing basketball. → WRONG — a routine takes the present simple: we play. Every day is the giveaway on the board.
    • I don't have to get up early on Sundays, so I usually sleep till ten. → RIGHT — both halves: don't have to = not necessary, and the routine is present simple.
  3. Kagan Team Statements before every bid: the team must agree one sentence of reason before anybody may raise a hand, and you may demand to hear it.
  4. Give sentence 6 (you mustn't do the extra task) its full two minutes — it is the most valuable argument of the day and the reason RULES is on the board.
  5. Totals in one chalk row at the end. The winning team is almost never the richest — it is the one that argued most; say so.
📺 On screen: the ten sentences revealed one at a time on the screen with running totals beside them (Backup: write the sentences on the board one at a time as you reach them and keep totals in chalk — the auction was designed for chalk and loses nothing)
👦 Students do: bid, argue, justify before bidding. Expect heat on sentences 1, 6 and 8 — those are the three that pay for the lesson. Typical error: a team bids everything on the first correct sentence and cannot play on; cap first bids at 30 if you know your gamblers

27–33′
Find Someone Who
standing, whole class
Why: Eight questions, four tenses plus have to, everybody standing and asking rather than answering. The questions are the point: asking is what a revision lesson usually forgets to practise, and the syllabus's speaking carousel lives inside this mingle.

WHAT WE DO: eight questions, four tenses plus have to, everybody asking

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. One grid each, eight lines, one name per line, nobody signs twice.
  2. The eight, with the question forms printed beside them because the question IS the task:
    • …has tried surfing, windsurfing or kayaking. Have you ever tried…?
    • …went to another city this summer. Did you go to another city this summer?
    • …has known their best friend for more than five years. How long have you known your best friend?
    • …was sleeping at ten o'clock last night. Were you sleeping at ten last night?
    • …is learning something new this term. Are you learning anything new this term?
    • …has never broken a bone. Have you ever broken a bone?
    • …had to help at home yesterday. Did you have to help at home yesterday?
    • …gets up before seven on school days. Do you get up before seven on school days?
  3. The rule that makes it a language activity: You may not show anybody your paper. You have to ask.
  4. Five minutes, then three findings back in full sentences: Dasha has never broken a bone. Third person — watch has survive the report.
  5. Fast finishers: write a ninth question of their own and get two names for it.
📺 On screen: the eight lines printed on a grid, one per student — paper is the medium here on purpose because the sheet moves round the room, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: ask and answer standing for five minutes. Expect Did you have to help at home? to be the avoided one — stand next to whoever is avoiding it. Typical error: reading the statement instead of asking the question; the printed question form is there for exactly that

Move break

Why: A stirrer that also forces one English sentence. Teenagers who have been sitting for forty minutes need to stand before the second half, or the second half dies.

WHAT WE DO: two minutes of movement and the switch into the paper

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. I say a sentence — if it is finished, point behind you; if it reaches now, draw the bracket in the air; if it is a rule, arms crossed. Six sentences, fast.
  2. Then desks cleared, chairs in, one pen out. The clean desk is the beginning of the second half and it costs thirty seconds here instead of three minutes later.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: move on six sentences and clear the desks. Expect fast confident gestures — the sorting has just been done twice sitting down. Typical problem: nobody wants to clear first; clear the nearest desk yourself and the rest follow

Diagnostic briefing
desks
Why: The four minutes that turn a hundred-question paper from a threat into a map. The fifteen-second rule is given here, and it is what makes a hundred questions in thirty-two minutes possible.

WHAT WE DO: the four minutes that turn a hundred questions from a threat into a timetable

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Papers face down. Nothing is said until every desk has one and every pen is out.
  2. Then the framing sentence, slowly: This is the sentence that makes the paper safe: This is not a test of what you forgot over the summer. It is a map of the whole year. Every one of these hundred questions is something I am going to teach you between now and May. Everything you cannot answer today is not a hole — it is the timetable.
  3. Then the working rule that makes a hundred questions possible: The working rule, said before the pens move: Fifteen seconds per question, no more. If you do not know it, put a dot next to it and go on. The dots are the most valuable thing on this paper. A hundred questions in thirty-two minutes is only possible with that rule — and sitting and staring at grammar you have not been taught is worth nothing to anybody.
  4. Then the promise, twice: No mark, no grade, nothing in the register. Say it twice, before and after. The paper is self-marked, the number is the student's own, and the only person who writes anything down is you — one number per name in your own notebook, so that in May the two papers can be put side by side.
  5. Names on the papers, timer visible. Turn them over — go.
📺 On screen: the fifteen-second rule and the words «no mark · no register · you mark it yourself» written on the board while you say them — chalk is the medium on purpose, because it stays visible for the whole thirty-two minutes, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: listen with the paper face down. Expect the number «a hundred» to produce noise and the framing sentence to stop it. Typical problem: «may I leave questions blank?» — yes, with a dot, and that answer goes to the whole room

⚡ Entry diagnostic · the Cambridge paper
solo · silence
Why: The real Prepare 4 diagnostic, and the reason it is worth half a lesson is its structure: exactly five questions per unit, in coursebook order, so the finished paper is a map of the year with the student's own dots on it. It is also the September paper that goes on the desk in May, next to the mock.

WHAT WE DO: the real Prepare 4 diagnostic — 100 questions, 32 minutes, silent mode

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Silent mode, palm down. Procedure questions only; no content answers, however small.
  2. Call the time three times with a target — the pacing is the whole difficulty: Eight minutes — near question twenty-five. · Sixteen — near fifty. · Twenty-four — near seventy-five.
  3. At question forty, once: If you are thinking, you are guessing slowly. Dot it and go on.
  4. ⚠️ Note how far each student reached — the reach is data. A student who reached seventy honestly has told you more than one who filled a hundred boxes at random.
  5. Stop on the timer. Pens down, papers face up, nobody compares anything.
📺 On screen: the visible timer and the fifteen-second rule already in chalk — the room is deliberately quiet and free of anything projected for these thirty-two minutes, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: work alone in silence for thirty-two minutes. Expect questions 1–20 (the September units) to go noticeably better than the rest — worth naming afterwards — then honest dotting from fifty onwards. Typical problem: two students stop early and sit; send them back with «dot the rest, do not leave them empty»

Self-check + the map of the year
solo → solo
Why: They mark their own hundred against the key and then draw the map: three units with the most dots, circled. The paper stops being a score in the moment they do that.

WHAT WE DO: marking your own hundred against the key, and turning it into a map

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Key face up on every desk, read aloud in blocks of ten at the same time — Blocks of ten. Pens ready. One to ten… Ten blocks, one minute each, no stopping for questions.
  2. Ticks and crosses only. No total yet — the total is the least interesting number on the page.
  3. Now the instruction that turns it into a map: Write the numbers one to twenty down the side of a page — the twenty units. Your crosses go where they belong. Five questions per unit, in order: questions one to five are Unit 1, six to ten are Unit 2, and so on. The arithmetic is on the board: Unit N = questions 5N−4 to 5N.
  4. Give them the anchors so nobody has to guess: Anchors, checked item by item on 13.08.2026: 1–5 Unit 1 All about me (describing people, stative verbs) · 6–10 Unit 2 In fashion (clothes, adverbs, past simple) · 11–15 Unit 3 My way of life (life events, too/enough, comparatives) · 16–20 Unit 4 Champions (do/go/play sports, past continuous) · 21–25 Unit 5 Call the police! (crime words, reflexives) · 26–30 Unit 6 City life (rush hour, some/any, a little/a few) · 31–35 Unit 7 Getting on (do a favour, mustn't / don't have to) · 36–40 Unit 8 Going away (airport words, future forms) · 41–45 Unit 9 Shop till you drop (receipt, saving up, present perfect) · 46–50 Unit 10 Taste this! (tastes delicious, for/since) · 51–55 Unit 11 A healthy future (body, will / going to) · 56–60 Unit 12 Incredible wildlife (animals, must/might be) · 61–65 Unit 13 Mixed feelings (-ed/-ing adjectives, just/yet) · 66–70 Unit 14 On screen (film words, relative clauses) · 71–75 Unit 15 Digital life (phrasal verbs, present passive) · 76–80 Unit 16 Amazing science (experiments, if/unless) · 81–85 Unit 17 Talented (arts, advise/persuade) · 86–90 Unit 18 The world of work (jobs, second conditional) · 91–95 Unit 19 The written word (notice/article, reported speech) · 96–100 Unit 20 Seeing is believing (mind idioms, past passive).
  5. ⚠️ Unlike some diagnostic papers, this one has no stray questions — the five-per-unit rule holds everywhere, which makes the map honest and the instruction one sentence long.
📺 On screen: the official key read aloud in blocks of ten from the printed sheet, with the unit anchors on the screen (Backup: the anchors go on the board in two columns before the lesson starts, and the key is read from the teacher's copy — the stage was designed to work with chalk)
👦 Students do: mark a hundred items and map the crosses onto twenty units. Expect the mapping to change the mood: a page of crosses becomes three heavy clusters and seventeen light ones. Typical problem: somebody totals up and announces it; stop it once, publicly and lightly

WHAT WE DO: three written sentences about what happens next, and the paper goes into the envelope

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three units circled — the ones with the most crosses. Then three lines, written, in English, in the exercise book:
  2. The unit I will have to work hardest at is ___ , and it is about ___ . · The grammar I still get wrong is ___ . · One thing I could not do today that I want to be able to do by May is ___ .
  3. SB p.4–7 stays open while they write — the contents map tells them what each unit is about, so line one is finished honestly, not guessed.
  4. Kagan Timed Pair Share, one minute each way: read your three lines to a partner. Nobody comments, nobody compares.
  5. Collect the papers into the envelope in front of them, and say why: ⚠️ KEEP THE PAPERS. Collect them at the end, one envelope, date on it: in the last week of May (the mock B1 Preliminary week in the syllabus) the May paper goes on the desk next to this one, and that comparison is the single strongest thing in the final week. If the envelope goes missing, the backup is the first page of the exercise book from lesson 1.
📺 On screen: the three sentence frames on the board and SB p.4–7 open on the desk — chalk and the paper book are the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write three lines and read them to one partner. Structure

Form
present simple (-s, do/does) · present continuous (am/is/are + -ing) · past simple (-ed, irregulars, did) · past continuous (was/were + -ing) · present perfect (have/has + V3, been/gone, for/since, just/already/yet) · must/have to and the meaning split in the negative. The irregular verb list is at the back of the Student's Book, p.158.
Pron. watch
the weak forms that hide the grammar in fast speech — have as /əv/ in I've lived, was as /wəz/, to as /tə/ in have to /ˈhæftə/. Say them naturally; the correction is visual (the board), not phonetic.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I have seen it last week.» Is last week finished? → yes. So which tense? → past simple. One finished time beats the perfect every time.
«She has gone to London twice.» Did she come back? → if twice, yes — so been, not gone.
«You mustn't do the extra task.» Forbidden or not necessary? → as said, forbidden. If we mean it is optional → you don't have to.
«I was reading when the phone rang.» Which started first? → the reading. The long one takes was -ing.
ICQ before the diagnostic: How many questions? (a hundred) How long on one? (fifteen seconds) What do you do if you don't know it? (a dot, and move on) Does it go in the register? (no)

Diagnostic Test — the framing sentenceThis is the sentence that makes the paper safe: This is not a test of what you forgot over the summer. It is a map of the whole year. Every one of these hundred questions is something I am going to teach you between now and May. Everything you cannot answer today is not a hole — it is the timetable.
Diagnostic Test — the working ruleThe working rule, said before the pens move: Fifteen seconds per question, no more. If you do not know it, put a dot next to it and go on. The dots are the most valuable thing on this paper. A hundred questions in thirty-two minutes is only possible with that rule — and sitting and staring at grammar you have not been taught is worth nothing to anybody.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«A hundred questions?!»The reaction is to the number, so answer the number: A hundred questions with four options each. Fifteen seconds on one. That is twenty-five minutes of actual work, and the first twenty you will finish in five seconds each. Then the framing sentence, and start.
Students who stop and try to work out grammar they have not been taughtThe single biggest time loss; the fifteen-second rule exists for it. Repeat it once, at question forty: If you are thinking, you are guessing slowly. Dot it and go on.
A student who reaches question 55 and no furtherFine, and say so quietly as you collect: Fifty-five is the first eleven units — that is where we will be in February. Note how far each student reached; the reach is data too.
The auction bogs down in bookkeepingKeep the bank simple: 100 points per team, bids in tens, you track totals in one chalk row. The arguing is the lesson; the arithmetic is not.
«So what did I get?»The number stays theirs: You got what you got, and you are the only person who knows it. What I want from you is the three circles on your map. Then straight to the three-line task.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
