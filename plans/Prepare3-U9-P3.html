🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Read six short real-world texts and say what each one is trying to make you do, and talk about a real future possibility with the first conditional: if + present simple, will + infinitive.
What is genuinely hard
One thing, and it is stubborn: English puts the future in ONE half of the sentence only. Russian marks the future in both, so If I will go, I will play is not carelessness — it is a direct translation, and it survives into Year 9 unless it is caught here.
Typical error to expect
If I will go, I will play with my friends — will in the if clause. English puts the present simple after if and will only in the other half (SB p.103 ex.5) [high priority, A2/B1]
Can you borrow me your book? instead of lend me — one Russian verb covers both. I lend you = I give it; I borrow from you = I take it. The TB flags this on p.201 as the error of the unit [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The grammar is discovered inside the six notices rather than presented, because three of them contain a first conditional doing a real job — a library threatening a fine, a shop making an offer, a teacher applying pressure. That is far better input than three example sentences, and it means the reading stage pays twice. The phrasal verbs sit between the reading and the grammar because they come out of the same six texts and because the class needs a stage with no new syntax in it. The error hunt is aimed at one error and does not pretend otherwise. The lesson ends on the If game, where each sentence has to start from the second half of the last one — four conditionals in a row, for a reason that belongs to them.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Six pieces of paper. One of them will cost you money if you read it wrong. Then a sentence that English builds differently from Russian: we put the future in one half and never in both. By the end of today you will play a game where every sentence you say has to grow out of the one before.”

door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, three pieces of advice as a rewind, and the bridge into Unit 18

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson's rewind. Three problems read out, three pieces of advice back, and each one must use a different opener: I've got a test tomorrow and I haven't studied. · My best friend isn't talking to me. · I'm always tired in the morning.
  3. Insist on the fourth opener for the third problem — How about …? — and check the form out loud.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page one hundred and two. New unit — books. And six pieces of paper, one of which will cost you money.
📺 On screen: the three problems in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: give three pieces of advice in three different structures. Expect How about + ‑ing to be right for two thirds of the class, which is a good result one lesson on

Lesson frame

Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two boxes on the board with an arrow between them. In the left box write IF + present, in the right WILL + verb.
  2. Under them, one sentence: If you buy two books today, we'll give you another one free. Ask: Is this about now or about the future? (the future) Then why is «buy» not «will buy»? Take answers for 30 seconds — do NOT confirm.
  3. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: English puts the future in one half of the sentence only, and today everybody learns which half.
  4. Set the bar: By the end of the lesson you will make four conditional sentences in a row, and each one has to grow out of the one before it.
📺 On screen: the two boxes and the model sentence in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: try to explain why buy is in the present. Expect nobody to know, which is correct at this point. Typical problem: somebody says «because it's a rule» — accept it, that is exactly what it is

Lead-in · what have you read
solo → class
Why: Three days of their own reading, listed honestly, shows them they read constantly and almost never books. That is the frame the six notices then walk into.

WHAT WE DO: the About you box p.102: everything you have read in the last three days

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three days. Everything you have read in English or in Russian. Books, websites, messages, signs, the back of a cereal packet. Two minutes, write the list.
  2. Then the two questions from the book: How many of them are books? What kind are they?
  3. Collect the categories on the board rather than the items: books · websites · messages · signs · leaflets · emails. TB p.201 suggests running it as a mingle if the class is slow to start.
  4. Close it with the observation that frames the whole lesson: Almost none of what you read is a book. And almost all of it is short. That is what we are reading today.
📺 On screen: the six categories collected in chalk as they come in — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: list what they have actually read, then sort it. Expect two or three books in a group of ten and a very long list of everything else. Typical problem: they think the answer should be «books» and give it — ask for the honest list instead

14–26′
Reading · six notices
solo → pairs
Why: Six short texts in six registers — a phone message, a school email, a website, a library notice, a shop sign, a handwritten note. The skill is working out what a text is FOR, and it is the most transferable reading skill in the book.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.102 (track 131): six short texts, then six three-option questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 first: read text 1 and text 2 together with the class, and ask the TB p.201 questions — Whose book is it? (Amber's) Did Amber like it? (yes) Is the secretary giving new information? (no) Do students need to give the books back today? (no).
  2. Then, alone: Where would you see texts three to six? Key: 3 school · 4 library · 5 bookshop · 6 classroom.
  3. Name the strategy before the questions: TB p.201: the question to ask about every short text is not «what does it say» but what is it for — text 4 is giving an instruction, text 5 is making an offer. And the single vocabulary trap of the whole unit: lend and borrow. Hand a book to a student and say it out loud: I'm lending Maria the book. Maria is borrowing it.
  4. ex.2, six three-option questions, alone, 5 minutes, in silence. Key: 1 C · 2 A · 3 B · 4 B · 5 C · 6 A.
  5. Play track 131 afterwards, not before — the texts are recorded and hearing them read is a second pass at the same content.
  6. For your own reference, the six texts: SB p.102, six short texts in six different registers: 1 a phone message from Amber to Kat — she has just finished a book she could not put down and will lend it to Kat if she wants; 2 an email from the school secretary — return your library books by the end of term or you will have to pay for them; 3 the Hillcrest School website — the writer Jill Hadfield is speaking next Tuesday, and if you buy a book after her talk she will sign it; 4 a library notice — put books back on the right shelves when you have finished with them; 5 a shop sign — buy two books today and get a third from this table free; 6 a handwritten note to Class 9A from Miss Taylor — everyone who took a maths textbook home must bring it back tomorrow, otherwise she cannot lend them to 9B.
📺 On screen: the six texts on the screen so a line can be pointed at, and track 131 in the board app for the check (Backup: they are all printed on SB p.102 — work from the paper book and read the texts aloud yourself for the second pass)
👦 Students do: place the texts, then answer six questions in silence. Expect 4 or 5 out of 6. Typical error: question 5 (the shop offer) — they read «free» and stop reading
SB p.102 ex.1–2 · track 131
26–32′
Reading · pair defence
pairs
Why: An answer is only worth having if it can be defended with a line from the text. The modelled dialogue makes the defence the task, not an afterthought.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.102: compare answers, and change one only if the other person quotes the text

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Model the argument first, with a strong pair or with yourself and a student: TB p.201 gives the pair-check dialogue to model before they start: What do you have for 1? — I put A because she writes about finishing a book. — Yes, but she says she HAS finished it, not that she WILL finish it. — You're right, so A is wrong. The point is that the argument has to quote the text.
  2. Kagan RallyCoach, 4 minutes: partner A says the answer and the line that proves it, partner B either accepts it or quotes a different line. Then swap.
  3. The rule of the stage, on the board: You may change an answer. You may not change it without a line from the text.
  4. Take feedback only on the questions where pairs disagreed — the rest is already settled.
  5. Close with the TB p.201 question: How can you get better at this? (learn more words; read the signs and notices you see online and work out what they mean).
📺 On screen: the six texts still on the screen, one at a time, so a pair can point at their line (Backup: the texts are printed on SB p.102 — the pair that wants to prove something reads their line out loud from the book)
👦 Students do: defend answers with evidence. Expect one or two genuine changes of mind, which is the aim. Typical error: «I just felt it was A» — the rule of the stage is the answer to that

32–44′
Vocabulary · phrasal verbs
pairs → class
Why: Eight phrasal verbs that all mean some version of «return» or «remove», so the work is not meaning but discrimination — and the pronoun word order, which no student gets right by accident.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–5 p.103: eight phrasal verbs found in the six texts, then used

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Remind them what a phrasal verb is by eliciting two they already have (TB p.202): What do you do first in the morning? (wake up, get up) What does a plane do? (take off).
  2. ex.4: find and underline the eight in the texts on p.102, then match them to the meanings. Key: a give back · b pick up · c bring back · d find out · e take back · f put down · g put back · h take out.
  3. Now the trap, physically, before it becomes a habit — hand a book to a student: I'm lending Maria the book. Maria is borrowing it. Both sentences on the board.
  4. ex.5, eight gapped sentences, in pairs, 5 minutes. Three of them need a pronoun in the middle, which is the real work. Key: 1 find out · 2 pick up · 3 brought back · 4 take out · 5 put, down · 6 give, back · 7 take, back · 8 put, back.
  5. Say the word-order rule once, with two examples and nothing else: Put the book back. Put it back. Never «put back it».
  6. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with the eight verbs on cards, 3 minutes.
📺 On screen: the eight phrasal verbs and their meanings on the screen, revealed after the pairs have tried (Backup: everything is printed on SB p.103 — work from the book and write only the word-order rule and the lend/borrow pair on the board)
👦 Students do: find, match, then produce. Expect take back and bring back to be the pair that will not separate. Typical error: put back it, which is why the rule gets its own thirty seconds

Grammar · discovery
class → pairs
Why: Guided discovery aimed at one question and one only: is the object the same person as the subject? Everything else about reflexives is a table they can copy in thirty seconds.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.103: find the conditionals in the notices, then complete the rules

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open at p.102 again: Three of those six texts contain a sentence with «if» in it. Find them. Two minutes. Key: If you don't return your library books by the end of term, you will have to pay for them. · If you buy a book after her talk, she'll write in it for you. · If you don't, I won't be able to lend them to 9B..
  2. Write all three on the board and underline the two verbs in each. Ask the question that does the teaching: Which verb is in the future? How many verbs are in the future in each sentence? (one).
  3. ex.2, the three rule questions, in pairs, 2 minutes. Key: 1 No, it doesn't · 2 Present simple · 3 Very sure.
  4. The third rule is the one to dwell on: «Very sure» — this is a real possibility, not a dream. The shop really will give you a free book.
  5. Then the comma, in one line, with both orders on the board: If you come, I'll be happy. — I'll be happy if you come. Comma in the first, no comma in the second.
  6. Point them at SB p.164 for the reference and the practice.
📺 On screen: the three conditional sentences copied from the notices onto the board and their verbs underlined in coloured chalk — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: hunt for the sentences, then work out the rules from them. Expect the «only one future» observation to come from a student rather than from you, which is the point of finding them in a real text. Typical error: they find I'll lend it to you, if you want and say the comma is wrong — it is the book's, and it is a spoken afterthought; say so

A5 grammar card — FIRST CONDITIONAL: REAL FUTURE POSSIBILITY: the formula both ways round (If + present simple, will + base verb / will + base verb + if + present simple) with examples, a negative and a question, the COMMA RULE (if-clause first → comma; result first → no comma), the COMMON MISTAKE If I will finish ✘ → If I finish ✔, the IF/WHEN meaning distinction (possible vs expected) and an IDEA BUILDER of condition and result cards

  • Write the same sentence both ways round and check the comma each time.

Grammar · practice
pairs → class
Why: Controlled practice in two directions — choose the pronoun, then decide whether by belongs in front of it. The second one is the half students skip and the exam does not.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4 p.103: match the halves, then finish four sentences with your own ideas

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed first (TB p.202): write half a sentence on the board and collect endings — If I feel bored, … (I'll call my friend, I'll watch a film). Three or four endings, written up.
  2. Books open, ex.3, five sentence halves to match, 3 minutes. Key: 1 b · 2 a · 3 e · 4 d · 5 c.
  3. ex.4, four sentences to finish with their own ideas, 4 minutes, alone. This is the first free production of the form.
  4. Mixed ability, TB p.202: TB p.202: turn ex.4 into two stages for weaker students. First give them the four sentence endings jumbled and have them put the words in order — don't / train / you / more → you don't train more — and only then have them write endings of their own.
  5. Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Sentence two — number threes, stand up and read yours.
📺 On screen: the half-sentence and the collected endings in chalk, then the book exercises on the screen one at a time (Backup: both exercises are printed on SB p.103 — run them from the book and keep the two-box diagram on the board)
👦 Students do: match, then produce their own. Expect ex.3 to be quick and ex.4 to be where will starts appearing in the wrong half. Typical error: If our team get into the final, we are going to celebrate — accept the meaning, then ask for the will version

66–72′
Grammar · error hunt
teams
Why: Correcting other people's sentences is faster and safer than producing them, and the Showdown means every student answers every item instead of one student answering one.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5 p.103: five sentences to repair, all five the errors this class actually makes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Showdown: teams write the repaired sentence on a mini-whiteboard, all boards up together, one sentence at a time. Key: If I go, I will play with my friends. · I'll be happy if you come to my party. · If you join this class, you'll like it. · If you like, we'll go by car. · My mum will be angry if I don't wear these trousers..
  2. For each one the team must name the error before the point is given: «will» in the «if» half · no «will» in the other half.
  3. Sentences 2, 3 and 5 are the same error in three disguises — say so out loud after the third one, because seeing the pattern is worth more than three separate corrections.
  4. Leave the two-box diagram up and point at it every time a team gets one wrong. Do not re-explain.
📺 On screen: the five sentences in chalk, one at a time, and the mini-whiteboards in the students' hands — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair, show and name the error. Expect sentence 1 to be unanimous and sentences 3 and 5 to split the room. Typical error: they add will to both halves in the repair, which is the original mistake wearing a hat

72–78′
Pronunciation · sentence stress
class → pairs
Why: Six minutes on which word you lean on. It is the difference between a sentence that carries an argument and a sentence read off a page.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6 p.103 (track 132): which words you lean on, and why

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Start with the model item: SB p.103 ex.6, model item 0: I'll bring cake if you bring sandwiches. TB p.202: ask why those words are stressed — because they carry the contrast, I'll against you and cake against sandwiches. Sentence stress is not decoration; it is which word the speaker is arguing about.
  2. Play track 132. Students listen and mark the stressed words in the four sentences.
  3. Key: 1 They'll come if you invite them. · 2 If you pass the exam, I'll buy you a present. · 3 You'll miss the bus if you don't hurry. · 4 If you help Tom, I'll help Pete..
  4. Play it again for repetition, then in pairs: read all four to each other, twice, exaggerating.
  5. One test to prove it matters: say sentence 4 with the stress in the wrong place — If you help Tom, I'll help Pete. with everything flat — and ask what is lost. (The contrast: who helps whom.)
📺 On screen: track 132 in the board app with the four sentences on the screen (Backup: the sentences are printed on SB p.103 — say them yourself, twice each, once flat and once with the contrast, which demonstrates the point better than the recording does)
👦 Students do: mark the stress, then say the sentences. Expect the marking to be about half right and the imitation to be much better than the marking. Typical error: they stress if, which is never stressed

🎯 The If game
pairs → class
Why: The productive outcome: a chain of conditionals where each new sentence starts from the second half of the last one. It forces the form four times in a row for a reason that is theirs, and it is genuinely funny by sentence three.

WHAT WE DO: ex.7 p.103 and SB p.138: a chain of four conditionals, each growing out of the last

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Explain it by doing it on the board first, out loud, with the class shouting endings: SB p.138, the If game: choose one of three sentence starters and finish it, then start a new sentence with the second half of your first one, and keep going for four sentences. The printed model is If I have a party on my birthday, I'll ask all my friends. If I ask all my friends, we'll make a lot of noise. The three starters are If I have enough money, I'll buy … · If I pass all my exams, I'll … · If I move to a new class next year, I'll …
  2. The rule that makes it a game and not an exercise, written up: Your next sentence starts with the second half of your last one.
  3. Pairs choose one of the three starters on SB p.138 and write a chain of four, 5 minutes. Silly is allowed; ungrammatical is not.
  4. Kagan Think-Pair-Share to finish: pairs read their chain to another pair, who vote for the best last sentence.
  5. Two or three chains read to the whole class.
  6. If time is short, or as an alternative for a talkative group, the two role-play situations on the same page work just as well: SB p.138, the pairwork situations for ex.6, both of them about lending and borrowing: 1 Student A wants a book back by tomorrow at the latest; Student B has no idea where the book is and must not admit it, and tries to get more time. 2 Student A wants a library book that Student B has had for a very long time; Student B has not finished it and wants to keep it as long as possible. Each conversation must contain at least three phrasal verbs.
📺 On screen: the model chain built live in chalk and the rule of the game beside it — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed; SB p.138 has the three starters printed
👦 Students do: build a chain of four conditionals. Expect the chains to get funnier and more accurate at the same time, because the form is repeating. Typical error: the second sentence restarts from nothing instead of from the previous ending — the rule on the board is what catches it

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can form and use the first conditional in both orders, with the comma in the right place.
  • Subsidiary. Reading six short texts for purpose; eight phrasal verbs about borrowing and returning; sentence stress on the words that carry the contrast.

  • say what a short notice or message is trying to make you do
  • use eight phrasal verbs about borrowing, returning and finding out
  • say what will happen if something else happens
  • put the comma in the right place in a conditional sentence

if + present simple, will / won't + infinitive. Either half may come first; comma only when the sentence starts with if (SB p.164). Phrasal verbs with a pronoun object take it in the middle: put it back.

sentence stress on the contrasting words: I'll bring cake if you bring sandwiches. Also the weak 'll, which is the reason students think there is no will in the sentence at all.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«If you buy two books, we'll give you a third.» Is this about now or about the future? → the future.
So why is buy in the present? → because that is the rule after if. English puts the future in one half only.
«If I will go, I will play with my friends» — where is the mistake? → will in the if half.
«If you join this class, you like it» — what is missing? → will in the second half. Without it the sentence is about a habit.
Am I sure this will happen, or is it a dream? → sure. It is a real possibility.
If you come to my party ___ I will be happy. Comma or no comma? → comma: the sentence starts with if.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Half the class writes If I will go …Expected, and it is a translation, not a slip. Two sentences on the board, one crossed out: If I will go go, I will play. Then the rule as a picture, not a sentence: draw two boxes, write IF + present in the first and WILL in the second, and leave it up for the rest of the lesson.
«Can you borrow me your pen?»Do not explain — demonstrate, as TB p.201 says. Hand a book to a student: I am lending Maria the book. Maria is borrowing it. Say it twice, with the book moving both ways, and write both sentences up.
The If game collapses into four unrelated sentencesThe rule is the whole game and it has to be visible: Your next sentence starts with the SECOND HALF of your last one. Do the first chain yourself on the board, out loud, before they start.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 13–19 (one long text, True / False / Not stated). The first conditional is on the codifier at 2.4.11 and appears in the grammar paper every year.

Six short notices in six different registers is the best possible training for reading a text you did not choose. And the grammar carries a mark of its own later: in the exam the if-clause is where students write will twice, which is the single most common conditional error in the FIPI reports.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the OGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
