🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Order a narrative with six time adverbs (first, then, next, later, suddenly, finally) and write a story of about 100 words (B1 Writing Part 2) that begins with a given sentence, has a middle full of events and an ending that surprises or solves something.
What is genuinely hard
Not the vocabulary — the shape. Left alone, a teenage story is a chain of and thens with no beginning and no ending, and the mark for organisation goes with it. The second difficulty is the compulsory first sentence: students ignore it, or rewrite it, and lose marks for a task they otherwise did well.
Typical error to expect
«And then… and then… and then…» — the commonest shape of a teenage story and the fastest way to lose the organisation mark. Six time adverbs solve it.
«While I saw him, he was running» — while goes with the LONG action, when with the short one. Getting them the wrong way round inverts the whole picture.
Why the lesson is built this way
The adverbs come first and fast, because they are the skeleton of everything that follows: a short text about a birthday card teaches all six in context, and the Teacher's Book's four checking questions (which one means unexpected? which means the last one?) settle the meanings in ninety seconds. Then model-first writing: Evie's story is mined for its three parts, its two tenses and its four adverbs, and the Prepare to write box is read only after the class has found all of that themselves. The story is planned, written and peer-checked in class, because a story written at home is a story nobody checks.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Your English teacher gives you the first sentence: «I decided to make a gift for my friend.» Everything after that is yours — but it has to have a beginning, a middle and an ending that surprises someone. Today: the six words that hold a story together.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, three minutes of repairing the class's own tense-choice errors, and the bridge into the writing lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what's the best thing about today so far? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — delayed feedback from lesson 1. Four sentences on the board, two broken exactly as the class broke them: While I walked home, I saw the fire. · I was fixing my bike when it started to rain. · When I was watching TV, my phone rang. · While I was making the card, my friend knocked at the door.
  3. Pairs, sixty seconds: find the broken one and repair it, then explain why using the line-and-cross picture from yesterday.
  4. Redraw the picture on the board while they explain — it is the tool they will use in today's writing.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page thirty-four, bottom half. Six little words — and then you're writing a whole story.
📺 On screen: the four sentences and the line-and-cross picture in chalk — nothing to fail
👦 Students do: spot, repair, and explain with a picture rather than a rule

Lesson frame
board
Why: The standard is public before the work starts, which is the difference between a writing lesson and a writing period.

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and put the story checklist up before anything else

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the four-line checklist that governs the whole lesson and leave it up: 1 Start with the given sentence, exactly. 2 Beginning: who and where. 3 Middle: the events. 4 End: a surprise or a solution.
  2. Add the language rule underneath: At least THREE different time adverbs. Both past tenses.
  3. Say the shape in 30 seconds: fifteen minutes on the adverbs, then one model story, then their own.
  4. Set the bar: Success today is not «I wrote a hundred words». It's «my partner found all four parts».
📺 On screen: the four-line checklist on the board — it stays up all lesson, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: copy the checklist into their notebooks; it becomes the peer-review sheet later

8–20′
Vocabulary · six time adverbs
class → individual
Why: Vocabulary · six time adverbs

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: the adverbs found in an example, then used in a real text

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: read the three-sentence example aloud. Two questions: how many actions? which words tell us when? (then, Later).
  2. Write the six adverbs on the board and check the meanings the Teacher's Book way, as four fast questions: Which one means unexpected? Which means the last one? Which comes before all the others? Which comes right after?
  3. ex.2 individually, three minutes: the birthday-card text, six gaps. Set the gist question first: Did the friend like the card?
  4. Check by nomination. Then the position point, which the exam notices: five of the six open the sentence and take a comma; later is the one that lives comfortably at the end.
  5. Read the finished text aloud together, with real pauses at the commas. That is what an organised story sounds like.
📺 On screen: the six adverbs on the board with the four checking questions — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: answer the four meaning questions, complete the text, then hear what organisation sounds like
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE tasks 26–31 (linking words). Time adverbs and linking words do the same job: they hold a text together, and the exam prints them as gaps.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: add a five-minute round: six sentences with a gap and four linkers to choose from (however, in addition, although, finally). It fits inside the adverb relay without extending it.

A5 glue-in sheet — TELL THE STORY IN ORDER: six time adverbs with a definition and an example — first · then · next · suddenly · later · finally (First, I chose the materials. · Suddenly, the lights went out. · Finally, I gave it to my friend.) — with the PUNCTUATION REMINDER (a time adverb at the start takes a comma) and the CONTRAST box then/next = normal sequence · suddenly = surprise · finally = ending

  • Number the six adverbs in the order they appear in a story.
  • Retell what you did this morning using four of them.

Vocabulary · the adverb relay
teams, standing
Why: Vocabulary · the adverb relay

WHAT WE DO: a six-round story built by three teams, one sentence at a time

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three teams. You give the opening line: Last Saturday I found a key in the street.
  2. Round by round you call an adverb (First, Then, Next, Suddenly, Later, Finally) and one member of each team writes the next sentence of their team's story on the board, starting with that adverb.
  3. Two points for a sentence that works, one extra for a past continuous used correctly. Rotate the writer every round.
  4. Read all three stories aloud at the end and let the class vote on the best ending — that vote is the teaching point for the writing stage.
📺 On screen: the board divided into three columns, one story per team — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: the team composes, one member writes, everyone hears three complete stories built out of six adverbs

Move break · the standing story
standing circle
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that makes chronology physical

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Circle. You start: Last week I was walking home when…
  2. The ball goes round; each student adds ONE sentence, and it has to begin with a time adverb. Nobody may repeat an adverb that has already been used until all six have gone.
  3. If the story dies, start a new one with a new opening line. Two stories in four minutes is a good rate.
  4. Freeze and sit: Page thirty-five. Now a story that somebody actually wrote, and won with.
📺 On screen: no technology in this stage — a ball and a circle, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone contributes at least one sentence with an adverb in it; the no-repeats rule forces listening

Model · Evie's story

Why: Model · Evie's story

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: the model read, then placed into beginning, middle and end

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 as a thirty-second question: do you enjoy reading stories? What makes a story interesting? Two answers, no more.
  2. ex.2: read Evie's story, two minutes, one question — how many people had the same sunglasses? Then books closed: what do you remember? Collect the events on the board in order.
  3. ex.3: three sentences from the story. Beginning, middle or end? Pairs decide and, crucially, justify: sentence 1 is a surprise, sentence 3 is background.
  4. Only NOW read the Prepare to write box aloud. Point at the board and say it: You just discovered all three of these yourselves.
📺 On screen: the story's events listed on the board in order. Backup: the page is the primary source; nothing to fail
👦 Students do: read, remember, reconstruct the order, then place three sentences and justify each one

Mining the model
pairs → class
Why: Mining the model

WHAT WE DO: ex.4: three past simples, three past continuous and three adverbs, found by hand

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4 in same-ability pairs, four minutes: find three verbs in the past simple, three in the past continuous and three time adverbs. Underline them in three different ways.
  2. Check on the board in three columns. The past continuous column is the revealing one — were planning, were walking, were leaving, were wearing — and every one of them is background or interruption.
  3. Ask the question that turns analysis into instruction: Where in HER story are the continuous verbs? What does that tell you about where to put yours?
  4. Write the answer on the board as a rule of thumb: Background at the start, events in the middle, the surprise at the end.
📺 On screen: the three columns on the board, filled in by the students — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: find and classify nine items in a real text, then draw the instruction out of the pattern

Planning · four notes, no sentences
individual → pairs
Why: Planning · four notes, no sentences

WHAT WE DO: ex.5–6: the task, the plan, and a partner's improvement

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the task aloud, then write the given first sentence on the board in full: I decided to make a gift for my friend. It stays there all lesson.
  2. ex.5: four planning questions on the page — characters, main events, ending, and why the gift. Three minutes, notes only.
  3. ex.6 as Kagan Think-Pair-Share, sixty seconds each way: tell your partner your plan; the partner's job is to say whether the ending surprises them. If it does not, they suggest one that would.
  4. Two minutes to repair the plan after that conversation. The repaired plan is what gets written.
📺 On screen: the compulsory first sentence written on the board in full — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: plan alone, then have the ending challenged by a partner before a word is written

🎯 Writing out · the story
individual, silent
Why: 🎯 Writing out · the story

WHAT WE DO: ex.7: about 100 words, written and finished in class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Silent mode, palm down: eighteen minutes, no talking, phones face down. The checklist is on the board, the Prepare to write box is open, and the first sentence is on the board.
  2. Target as a range: 90–110 words. Count them and write the number.
  3. Circulate with a pen but do not rewrite: a dot in the margin marks a line with an error; the writer hunts it.
  4. At minute 14, the two-minute warning and the rule: Last two minutes: check your ending and your adverbs. No new events.
  5. Fast finishers write a second, different ending for the same story.
📺 On screen: the visible timer, the checklist and the first sentence, all on the board — nothing to fail
👦 Students do: write about 100 words alone and finish. Nothing goes home unfinished
📓 Interactive notebook — 06_U5_Writing_A_Story_A5
A5 writing card — A STORY of about 100 words with a given first line («I decided to make a gift for my friend.»): the five-point PLAN (characters · what gift · what problem happens · how it is solved · how they feel), a full model story annotated in the margin (opening · background action · sudden problem · solution · feeling), a USEFUL LANGUAGE strip (First, · While…, · Suddenly, · Then, · Finally,) and a CHECK list (past forms · time adverbs · beginning/middle/end · feelings · spelling)
  • Glue the card in on the page where the story will be written.
  • Mark the five labels in the model — the problem is the heart of it.
  • Write your own 100 words: the background in the continuous, the problem in the simple.

78–86′
Peer review + polish
pairs
Why: Peer review + polish

WHAT WE DO: a four-question checklist swap, then two minutes to repair

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap stories. The reader answers four questions IN WRITING at the bottom: is the first sentence exactly right? where does the middle start? how many time adverbs are there? does the ending surprise you?
  2. If the ending does not surprise them, they write one suggestion. No grammar correction in someone else's story.
  3. Two minutes back with your own story: repair what your reader could not find.
  4. Collect. Read two anonymous ENDINGS aloud — endings are what the class copies next time — and say exactly why they work.
📺 On screen: no technology in this stage — paper and pens, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: read someone else's story with four specific jobs, then repair their own

Structure
Last week we decided to go to the shopping mall. · We were walking past our favourite shop when I saw they had a sale! · First, we tried on lots of sunglasses. · Suddenly, we started laughing at each other.
Form
time adverbs at the start of a sentence with a comma (First, … · Suddenly, …) or later in the sentence (I finished it later); past continuous for the background, past simple for the events.
Pron. watch
the adverbs carry the sentence stress when they open it — SUDDenly, FINally — and the comma after them is a real pause. Reading a story aloud with those pauses is the fastest way to hear whether it is organised
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Suddenly» — planned or unexpected? → unexpected. That is why it almost always appears near the end.
«Finally» — the last event or a long time later? → the last event. Later is the one that means after some time.
The task gives you the first sentence. Can you change it? → no. Copy it exactly, then start writing.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The story is a chain of and thensThe checklist demands at least three different time adverbs and the peer reviewer counts them with a finger. Put all six on the board and say the rule: Each one once. Nobody uses then three times.
The given first sentence gets rewrittenIt is copied onto the board in full and stays there. The first check in peer review is a comparison, word for word.
The ending is «and then I went home»The model teaches the alternative: Evie's ending is a surprise (everyone is wearing the same sunglasses). Say the requirement aloud: Your last two sentences either surprise me or solve a problem.
Some students finish in eight minutesFast finishers write an alternative ending for their own story, then read both to their partner and let the partner choose. It is genuine extra writing, not filler.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 38 (the written opinion piece) and tasks 19–25.

A story is not on the Russian paper, but everything that makes one work is: sequencing adverbs (first, then, later, suddenly, finally), a controlled chronology, and past tenses that do not collapse into one. Examiners lose patience with a text where every sentence starts with And then — which is exactly what six time adverbs prevent.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
