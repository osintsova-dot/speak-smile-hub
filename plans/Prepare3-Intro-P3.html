🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Choose correctly between the present simple, the present continuous, the past simple and the past continuous in context — and, which is the real aim, say why in one sentence each time.
What is genuinely hard
Not the forms. The forms come back in twenty minutes. What is hard is the choice, because Russian marks these differences in places English does not: a routine and a thing happening now are one verb in Russian and two in English, and «I was doing» versus «I did» has no reliable Russian trigger at all.
Typical error to expect
Every day we are eating soup · We was watching · Did you went? · I am knowing. The first and the third are the ones that will still be there in April if they are not named today.
Why the lesson is built this way
The grammar half is thirty-two minutes and it is built the short way round: one story on the board with all four tenses inside it, then an auction, then a mingle. No rule is presented and no table is copied — the class builds the timeline and formulates the reasons, because a reason you said yourself is the only kind you can use under pressure. Then the second half is the Cambridge entry diagnostic, and it is here for a structural reason rather than a ceremonial one: its hundred questions walk through the coursebook in order, five per unit, so the finished paper is literally a map of the year with the student's own dots on it. That is why it is framed as a timetable and not as a test, why nothing is marked, and why the papers are collected and kept — in lesson 71 this sheet goes on the desk next to the May one, and the distance between them is the strongest thing in the last week of the year.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Four tenses. You have had all four of them for two years and today they come back in half an hour. Then something different: a hundred questions from Cambridge. Ninety-five of them are things you have not been taught yet — and that is the point. Every question you cannot answer today is a lesson on our timetable between now and May. No mark, nothing in the register, and I am keeping the paper until the twenty-sixth of May so that you can see what happened.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, the second error feedback of the year, and the bridge into the four tenses

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson feedback. Four sentences from your correction sheet on the board, none of them with a name attached: I have 13 years. · My house is on the fifth floor and it is a flat. · Can you lend me your charger? — no, borrow. · I am liking pizza.
  3. Kagan RallyCoach, two minutes: one pen between two, one writes the repair, the other says why. Then all four aloud, fast.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the lesson: The last one is today's whole lesson. Some verbs will not go in the continuous, and knowing which is half of English grammar.
📺 On screen: the four sentences in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair four sentences in pairs. Expect I have 13 years to be found instantly and I am liking to be argued about — which is the perfect way into today's grammar. Typical problem: they fix lend to borrow without being able to say the difference; make them say it

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write four words on the board with a lot of space under each: NOW · ALWAYS · THEN · WAS HAPPENING.
  2. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: four tenses, and the aim is not the forms — it is being able to say WHY, in one sentence, every time.
  3. Announce the second half honestly and in the words you will use all lesson: After the break there are a hundred questions. It is not a test, it is a map of the year — and I am keeping it until May.
  4. Set the bar: Every answer today comes with the word «because».
📺 On screen: the four words in chalk — they stay up all lesson and every example gets written under one of them, so the board is the medium here and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: read the four headings and start guessing what they mean. Expect «WAS HAPPENING» to be the one nobody names. Typical problem: a student announces the Russian names of the tenses; accept it once and then insist on the four English headings

8–18′
Four tenses back on their feet
class → pairs
Why: One story on the board, four tenses inside it, and a timeline they build themselves. This is the fastest honest way back into the grammar of Prepare 2 — and the timeline is the thing they will point at in October when the past continuous returns in Unit 4.

WHAT WE DO: one story on the board, four tenses inside it, and a timeline they build themselves

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Tell a thirty-second true story about your own summer with all four tenses in it, and write only the verbs on the board as you go: I usually spend August in Norilsk. This year I went to Kazan. On the third day I was walking along the river when it started to rain. Right now I am looking at the photographs.
  2. Pairs, three minutes: put each verb under one of the four headings and write the reason next to it. Nothing else.
  3. Take the reasons, not the answers, with Kagan Numbered Heads Together. The reasons are the lesson.
  4. Then draw the timeline for the last two — a long line for was walking, a cross on it for started. Thirty seconds of chalk that fixes the pair for the year.
  5. For your own reference, the four in one table:
    TenseWhat it is for · what gives it away
    Present simple — what is always / usually trueI get up at seven. She plays tennis on Fridays.
    usually · every day · on Mondays · never · always
    Present continuous — what is happening now or around nowI am doing my homework. They are building a new school this year.
    now · at the moment · today · this week · look!
    Past simple — a finished action at a finished timeWe went to Sochi. She didn't like the film.
    yesterday · last summer · in 2024 · two days ago
    Past continuous — what was already going on when something else happenedI was reading when the phone rang.
    while · when · at six o'clock yesterday
📺 On screen: the verbs and the timeline in chalk under the four headings — the board is the medium here and the timeline has to be drawn live, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: sort five verbs and write a reason for each. Expect the two present tenses to be sorted instantly and the two past tenses to split the room. Typical error: was walking explained as «it is longer», which is right but not usable — push to «it had already started when the other thing happened»
board · exercise books
18–27′
Grammar auction
teams of 3–4
Why: Correcting other people's sentences is faster and much safer than producing your own, and an auction makes the class argue about grammar with actual heat. Every sentence on the list is either a real Cambridge item or a real Russian-speaker error.

WHAT WE DO: ten sentences, some right and some wrong, bought with a budget

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Each team gets 1000 points. Ten sentences go up one at a time and teams bid on the ones they believe are correct. You only get your points back — with interest — if the sentence really is correct.
  2. The ten sentences, with what they are actually testing:
    • I am usually going to school by bus. → WRONG — a routine takes the present simple: I usually go to school by bus.
    • She doesn't understand the question. → RIGHT — understand is a state verb and stays out of the continuous.
    • What did you do when I called you? → WRONG in meaning, right in form. If the call interrupted something, it is What were you doing when I called you? Accept it only if the student can defend «what did you do next».
    • We was watching a film at nine o'clock. → WRONG — we were watching. This is the single most common past continuous error in a Russian-speaking group.
    • Yesterday I didn't went to the club. → WRONG — the past is already in did: I didn't go.
    • My brother is studying medicine at Kazan University this year. → RIGHT — a long temporary situation is exactly what the present continuous is for.
    • Every day in the morning we are eating soup. → WRONG — we eat. Taken from WB p.5 task 5, so it comes back in lesson 5.
    • While I was walking home, it started to rain. → RIGHT — long action interrupted by a short one, and the two tenses are in the right places.
    • Do you liked the competition? → WRONG — Did you like…? One past marker per question. Taken from SB p.30 Review 1, Grammar ex.1.
    • I don't know where the station is. I come from another town. → RIGHT — both state and routine, both present simple. Taken from SB p.30 Review 1, Grammar ex.2.
  3. ⚠️ Two of them are lifted straight out of the coursebook — Do you liked the competition? and I don't know where the station is are SB p.30 Review 1 Grammar ex.1 and ex.2, which the class meets properly in October. Say so when you reach them: it makes the auction feel like the future rather than a quiz.
  4. Kagan Team Statements before every bid: the team must agree one sentence of reason before anybody may bid, and you may ask for it.
  5. Totals on the board at the end. The winning team is almost never the richest one — it is the one that argued most.
📺 On screen: the ten sentences revealed one at a time on the screen with the running totals beside them (Backup: write the sentences on the board one at a time as you reach them and keep the totals in chalk in the corner — the auction was designed for chalk and loses nothing)
👦 Students do: bid, argue, and justify before bidding. Expect real heat on sentence 3 (What did you do when I called you?) — that is the most valuable argument of the lesson and it is worth two extra minutes. Typical error: a team bids everything on the first correct sentence and then cannot play
board or screen · exercise books
27–33′
Find Someone Who
standing, whole class
Why: Eight questions, three tenses, everybody standing and asking rather than answering. The questions are the point: the exam and the classroom both give marks for asking, and asking is what a revision lesson usually forgets to practise.

WHAT WE DO: eight questions, three tenses, everybody asking rather than answering

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. One grid each, eight lines, one name per line and nobody may sign twice.
  2. The eight, and the question form is printed beside each because the question is the actual task:
    • …went abroad this summer. Did you go abroad this summer?
    • …is reading a book at the moment. Are you reading a book at the moment?
    • …gets up before seven every day. Do you get up before seven every day?
    • …was sleeping at ten o'clock last night. Were you sleeping at ten last night?
    • …doesn't like hot weather. Do you like hot weather?
    • …swam in a river or a lake this summer. Did you swim in a river or a lake?
    • …is learning a second foreign language this year. Are you learning another language?
    • …was living in a different town five years ago. Were you living somewhere else five years ago?
  3. Rule that makes it a language activity: You may not show anybody your paper. You have to ask.
  4. Five minutes, then stop and take three findings back in full sentences: Dasha went to Sochi this summer. Third person, so the ‑s is back and somebody will drop it.
  5. Fast finishers: write a ninth question of their own and get two names for it.
📺 On screen: the eight lines printed on a grid, one per student — paper is the medium here on purpose because the sheet moves round the room, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: ask and answer for five minutes standing. Expect Were you sleeping at ten last night? to be the one they avoid — go and stand next to whoever is avoiding it. Typical error: they read the statement instead of turning it into a question; the printed question form beside each line is there for exactly that

33–35′
Move break
standing
Why: A stirrer that also forces one English sentence. Teenagers who have been sitting for forty minutes need to stand before the second half, or the second half dies.

WHAT WE DO: two minutes of movement and the switch into the paper

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. I say a sentence — if it is now, point at the floor; if it is always, point at the clock; if it is finished, point behind you. Six sentences, fast.
  2. Then desks cleared, chairs in, everything off the table except a pen. The clean desk is the beginning of the second half and it costs thirty seconds here instead of three minutes later.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: move on six sentences and clear their desks. Expect fast, confident pointing — the sorting has just been done sitting down. Typical problem: nobody wants to be first to clear the desk; do it for the nearest one yourself and the rest follow

Diagnostic briefing
desks
Why: The four minutes that turn a hundred-question paper from a threat into a map. The fifteen-second rule is given here, and it is what makes a hundred questions in thirty-two minutes possible.

WHAT WE DO: the four minutes that turn a hundred questions from a threat into a timetable

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Papers face down. Nothing is said until every desk has one and every pen is out.
  2. Then the framing sentence, and say it slowly: This is the sentence that makes the paper safe: This is not a test of what you forgot over the summer. It is a map of the whole year. Every one of these hundred questions is something I am going to teach you between now and May. Everything you cannot answer today is not a hole — it is the timetable.
  3. Then the working rule, which is what makes a hundred questions possible in thirty-two minutes: The working rule, said before the pens move: Fifteen seconds per question, no more. If you do not know it, put a dot next to it and go on. The dots are the most valuable thing on this paper. A hundred questions in thirty-two minutes is only possible with that rule, and the rule is not a compromise — sitting and staring at grammar you have not been taught is worth nothing to anybody.
  4. Then the promise, twice: No mark, no grade, nothing in the register. Say it twice, before and after. The paper is self-marked, the number is the student's own, and the only person who writes anything down is you — one number per name in your own notebook, so that in May you can put the two papers side by side.
  5. Names on the papers, pens up, timer visible. Turn them over — go.
📺 On screen: the fifteen-second rule and the words «no mark · no register · you mark it yourself» written on the board while you say them — chalk is the medium here on purpose, because it stays visible for the whole thirty-two minutes, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: listen with the paper face down. Expect the number «a hundred» to produce noise and the framing sentence to stop it. Typical problem: somebody asks whether they may leave questions blank — yes, with a dot, and that is the answer to give the whole room

⚡ Entry diagnostic · the Cambridge paper
solo · silence
Why: The real Prepare 3 diagnostic, and the reason it is worth half a lesson is its structure: five questions per unit, in coursebook order, so the finished paper is a map of the year with the student's own dots on it. It is also the September paper that goes on the desk in May.

WHAT WE DO: the real Prepare 3 diagnostic — 100 questions, 32 minutes, silent mode

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Silent mode, palm down. Answer procedure questions only; answer no content questions at all, however small.
  2. Call the time three times and give the target each time, because the pacing is the whole difficulty: Eight minutes — you should be near question twenty-five. · Sixteen minutes — near fifty. · Twenty-four minutes — near seventy-five.
  3. At question forty, once, repeat the rule out loud: If you are thinking, you are guessing slowly. Dot it and go on.
  4. ⚠️ Note down how far each student reached — the reach is data. A student who reached seventy honestly has told you more than one who filled a hundred boxes at random.
  5. Stop on the timer. Pens down, papers face up, nobody compares anything.
📺 On screen: the visible timer and the fifteen-second rule already in chalk on the board — the room is deliberately quiet and free of anything projected for these thirty-two minutes, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: work alone in silence for thirty-two minutes. Expect the first thirty questions to go fast, a slowdown around forty to fifty, and honest dotting after seventy — that is exactly the shape you want to see. Typical problem: two or three students stop early and sit; send them back with «dot the rest, do not leave them empty»

Self-check + the map of the year
solo → solo
Why: They mark their own hundred against the key and then draw the map: three units with the most dots, circled. The paper stops being a score in the moment they do that.

WHAT WE DO: marking your own hundred against the key, and turning it into a map

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Key face up on every desk, and read it aloud in blocks of ten at the same time — Blocks of ten. Pens ready. One to ten… Ten blocks, one minute each, no stopping for questions.
  2. Ticks and crosses only. No total yet — the total is the least interesting number on the page and asking for it first ruins the next instruction.
  3. Now the instruction that turns it into a map: Write the numbers one to twenty down the side of a page — the twenty units. Now put your crosses where they belong. The arithmetic is on the board: 100 three-option questions, and the structure is the thing that makes the paper worth half of a lesson: the questions walk through the coursebook in order, about five per unit. The arithmetic Unit N ≈ questions 5N−4 … 5N is right to within a question or two, and about eight of the hundred sit outside their block altogether — those eight are listed below, so nobody has to guess.
  4. Give them the anchors so nobody has to guess: Anchors, checked item by item against the paper on 12.08.2026 — this is what turns a score into a plan: 1–5 Unit 1, personality and present simple / continuous · 6–10 Unit 2, geography, animals, state verbs · 11–16 Unit 3, travel and the past simple · 17–21 Unit 4, homes and the past continuous · 22–25 Unit 5, school subjects and comparative adverbs · 26–29 Unit 6, materials and possession · 30–33 Unit 7, holiday activities and the present continuous for the future · 34–37 Unit 8, furniture, will and may · 38–42 Unit 9, sports, must and have to · 43–48 Unit 10, relationships, internet words and verb patterns · 50–55 Unit 11, places in a city and determiners · 56–58 Unit 12, films, relative pronouns and conjunctions · 60–63 Unit 13, outdoor activities and the present perfect · 64–69 Unit 14, shops, measurements, just / yet / already · 70–75 Unit 15, free time, for and since · 76–78 Unit 16, large numbers and the present perfect against the past simple · 79–82 Unit 17, body and emotions · 83–87 Unit 18, books and the first conditional · 88–92 Unit 19, cooking and the present simple passive · 93–97 Unit 20, life changes and the past simple passive.
  5. And the exceptions, or five students will map them wrongly: The eight that sit outside their block, so that a dot next to one of them is read correctly: 49 (cricket) is Unit 9 · 59 (if) is Unit 18 · 98 (change, as in coins) is Unit 14 · 99 (take three subjects) is Unit 5 · 100 (drawings) is Unit 18 · 74–75 (glad, a shopping list) could be argued into Unit 16 · 78 (articles) could be argued into Unit 10. Nothing else moves.
📺 On screen: the official key read aloud in blocks of ten from the printed sheet, with the unit anchors on the screen (Backup: the anchors go on the board in two columns before the lesson starts, and the key is read from the teacher's copy — the stage was designed to work with chalk)
👦 Students do: mark a hundred items and then map their crosses onto twenty units. Expect the mapping to be the moment the mood changes: a page of crosses becomes a page with three heavy clusters and seventeen light ones. Typical problem: somebody adds up their total and announces it; stop it once, publicly and lightly

WHAT WE DO: three written sentences about what happens next, and the paper goes into the envelope

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three units circled — the ones with the most crosses. Then three lines, written, in English, in the exercise book:
  2. The unit I will have to work hardest at is ___ , and it is about ___ . · The tense I still get wrong is ___ . · One thing I could not do today that I want to be able to do by May is ___ .
  3. SB p.4–7 is open while they write — the contents map tells them what each unit is about, so line one can be finished honestly rather than guessed.
  4. Kagan Timed Pair Share, one minute each way: read your three lines to a partner. Nobody comments, nobody compares, everybody listens.
  5. Then collect the papers into the envelope, in front of them, and say why: ⚠️ KEEP THE PAPERS. Collect them at the end, put them in one envelope with the date on it and do not lose it: in lesson 71 the May paper goes on the desk next to this one, and that comparison is the single strongest thing in the last week of the year. It is already written into the plan for lesson 71 (the year-end generator folder, plan P2) as the September paper. If the envelope does go missing, the backup is the first page of the exercise book from lesson 1.

Lexis

Structure
I get up at seven every day. · I am doing my homework at the moment. · Last summer we went to Kazan. · I was reading when the phone rang.
Form
present simple (‑s in the third person, do / does in questions and negatives) · present continuous (am / is / are + ‑ing) · past simple (‑ed and the irregulars, did in questions and negatives) · past continuous (was / were + ‑ing). The irregular verb list is at the back of the Student's Book, p.167.
Pron. watch
the weak was and were in the middle of a sentence — /wəz/ and /wə/, not /wɒz/ and /wɜː/. This is why we was survives: in fast speech the two sound almost alike, so the correction has to be visual as well as spoken.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I am doing my homework.» Right now or every day? → right now. And «I do my homework»? → every day.
«I was reading when the phone rang.» Which started first? → the reading. Which one is short? → the ring. The long one takes was ‑ing.
«I am knowing the answer.» What is wrong? → know is a state, not an action. You cannot be in the middle of knowing something.
«Did you went?» How many past markers are there? → two. How many are allowed? → one, and it is did.
ICQ before the diagnostic: How many questions? (a hundred) How long on one question? (fifteen seconds) What do you do if you don't know it? (a dot, and move on) Does it go in the register? (no)

Diagnostic Test — the framing sentenceThis is the sentence that makes the paper safe: This is not a test of what you forgot over the summer. It is a map of the whole year. Every one of these hundred questions is something I am going to teach you between now and May. Everything you cannot answer today is not a hole — it is the timetable.
Diagnostic Test — the working ruleThe working rule, said before the pens move: Fifteen seconds per question, no more. If you do not know it, put a dot next to it and go on. The dots are the most valuable thing on this paper. A hundred questions in thirty-two minutes is only possible with that rule, and the rule is not a compromise — sitting and staring at grammar you have not been taught is worth nothing to anybody.
Diagnostic Test — no mark, no registerNo mark, no grade, nothing in the register. Say it twice, before and after. The paper is self-marked, the number is the student's own, and the only person who writes anything down is you — one number per name in your own notebook, so that in May you can put the two papers side by side.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«A hundred questions?!»The reaction is to the number, not to the content, so answer the number: A hundred questions with three options each. Fifteen seconds on one. That is twenty-five minutes of actual work, and half of them you will finish in five seconds. Then the framing sentence and start.
Students who stop and try to work out grammar they have not been taughtThis is the single biggest time loss and the fifteen-second rule exists for it. Repeat it during the paper, once, at question forty: If you are thinking, you are guessing slowly. Dot it and go on.
A student who reaches question 60 and no furtherPerfectly fine, and say so as you take it in — quietly, to them: Sixty is the first twelve units. That is where we will be in February. Note how far each student reached; the reach is data too.
The self-check takes longer than ten minutesRead the key in blocks of ten and pause between blocks — Blocks of ten. Pens ready. One to ten… Reading it item by item on demand is what makes it overrun.
«So what did I get?»The number is theirs and it stays theirs: You got what you got, and you are the only person who knows it. What I want is the three circles on your map. Then move them straight to the three-line task.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
