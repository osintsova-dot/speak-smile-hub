🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name fifteen free-time activities as whole phrases, survey the whole class with them, and answer five three-option questions on a long text by pointing at the line that proves it.
What is genuinely hard
Two things. The vocabulary is almost all verb + noun (playing an instrument, not instrument), so half of it is grammar. And in the reading, every wrong option contains a word that IS in the text — recognising a word proves nothing at all.
Typical error to expect
They pick the option that repeats a word from the text. The correct option is almost always paraphrased; the wrong ones copy the wording [exam report]
I make sport / I make homework instead of do sport / do homework [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary is not presented and then practised — it is presented and then used to interview everybody in the room, which means each of the fifteen phrases is said aloud about a dozen times before the halfway mark. The reading comes second because the blog is full of the same phrases, so it reads as a reward rather than as a new load. The five questions get a strategy stage of their own BEFORE anyone answers them: without it students answer from what sounds sensible about hobbies rather than from what Lucy actually wrote. And the last teaching stage is not the answers but the wrong options — saying why B is wrong is the only part of this that transfers to the next text.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“How many hours are you free in a week? Write the number. Now — in twenty minutes this class is going to know exactly what all of us do with those hours, because you are going to ask every single person in the room. And then you are going to read about a girl who spends hers looking for boxes hidden in forests.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a three-round rewind of block 7, and the bridge into Unit 15

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last block rewind. Three fast rounds on the board: One — something you have never done. Two — something you have already done today. Three — a shop and what you buy there.
  3. Take one answer per round from three different students, then stop. Block 7 grammar (present perfect with ever, never, just, yet, already) is one week old and this is all the revision it needs today.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the new unit: Books open, page eighty-six. New block, new topic — and the topic is what you do when nobody is telling you what to do.
📺 On screen: the three prompts in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: produce one sentence per round. Expect already and yet to come out confidently after block 7. Typical problem — I have never went; write the participle above it in coloured chalk and move on

Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the two halves of the lesson on the board, one under the other: 1 What does this class do with its free time? (we will know by half past) and 2 What does Lucy do with hers? (and how do you prove it)
  2. Say the aim in forty seconds: today's words are almost all two or three words long and they only work whole — playing an instrument, never instrument.
  3. Set the bar for the second half, and mean it: Every reading answer today needs a line from the text underneath it. No line, no answer.
  4. Ask them to write one number in the margin of their exercise book right now: how many hours of free time they think they have in a week. It gets used in four minutes.
📺 On screen: the two halves of the lesson in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: copy the aim and write their number. Expect wildly different numbers, from four to forty. Typical problem: somebody asks whether sleeping counts — the answer is no, and the question is a good one

Why: Counting your own hours is a personal fact, so the topic arrives as information about them rather than as a page. It also produces the numbers the survey will need later.

WHAT WE DO: the About you box p.86: how many hours of free time, and is that the right amount

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The two questions from the box: How many hours of free time do you have per week? Is this the right amount, too much or too little?
  2. Kagan Think-Pair-Share, one round of ninety seconds. They compare the numbers they wrote in the frame stage.
  3. Collect the extremes only — the biggest number and the smallest — and put both on the board. Same school, same week. Forty hours and six hours. How?
  4. Close it with the TB p.177 question that opens the topic properly: What would you do with five more hours a week?
📺 On screen: the two About you questions and the two extreme numbers in chalk — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: compare numbers and explain the gap. Expect the answer to be sport, extra lessons or a long journey to school. Typical problem: they answer in hours per day, not per week — worth one line of clarification, not more

Vocabulary · free-time activities
pairs → class
Why: Fifteen phrases is a lot, and most of them are verb + noun rather than single words. Meaning comes from photos, and form is fixed by saying the whole phrase every time.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.86: match photos A–E to the questionnaire, then work through all fifteen activities

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed first (TB p.169): pairs get two minutes to write as many free-time activities as they can, with one rule — every one of them needs a verb. That rule is the whole vocabulary point of the lesson.
  2. Books open, ex.1 in pairs, 3 minutes: five photos, fifteen activities. Key: A going shopping · B playing computer games · C going out with friends / playing sport · D making things · E collecting.
  3. Pre-teach the three that are never guessed (TB p.169): bowling — rolling a heavy ball to knock down bottle shapes; knit — making clothes with wool and two long needles, so photo D is making things; badge — a small pin with a picture on it, which is what photo E is a collection of.
  4. Drill the whole list chorally, phrase by phrase, with the stress marked: PLAYing an INstrument · SPENDing time onLINE · GOing OUT with friends.
  5. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with picture cards, 3 minutes, five trades each. The card shows the picture; the answer must be the whole phrase.
  6. Fast finishers: TB p.174: fast finishers take their completed sentences and delete as many words as they can while keeping them grammatical — I had a wonderful time at the party last night → I had a party. It is a compression game and it teaches what the skeleton of a sentence is.
📺 On screen: the fifteen activities on the screen next to the five photos (Backup if the board is down: everything is printed on SB p.86 — read the fifteen phrases aloud yourself, have the class point at the questionnaire line in the paper book and repeat chorally; the Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards are printed in advance and need no technology)
👦 Students do: list from memory, match, then mingle. Expect 10–12 of the 15 known already. Typical errors: making things confused with cooking, and single-word answers (instrument) instead of the whole phrase — insist on the phrase every time

🎯 Class questionnaire
whole room, standing
Why: A real survey with a real result: every student asks every other student the same three questions, so each of the fifteen phrases is said aloud a dozen times in nine minutes.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.86: the real survey — ask everybody, all three columns, tally marks

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. SB p.86: a three-column class questionnaire — I do this · I'd like to try this · I'm not at all interested in this. Students walk round the room, ask everybody, and record the answers as tally marks. Then, in groups, they turn the tallies into sentences with the frames printed on the page: In our class, lots of / some / a few people already do these activities: …
  2. Demonstrate for fifteen seconds with one student so the shape is unmistakable: Cooking — do you do this, would you like to try it, or are you not interested? One answer, one tally mark, next person.
  3. Drill the three questions once chorally before anyone stands up (TB p.169 mixed ability): Which activities do you do in your free time? Which would you like to try? Which are you not interested in?
  4. Kagan Mix-Pair-Share: 9 minutes, stand up, every person in the room, no sitting down until the sheet is full.
  5. Walk with them and count in English out loud occasionally — it keeps the language of the room English without a single correction.
  6. Stop it at nine minutes even if two students are unfinished. The data does not have to be complete to be interesting.
📺 On screen: the three survey questions in chalk on the board where everybody can see them while they walk — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed; the tally sheet is the questionnaire printed in their own book
👦 Students do: interview every person in the room and record the answers as tally marks. Expect the room to be loud and entirely on task. Typical error: they forget to mark the third column at all, because «not interested» feels rude to say — name that in advance and it stops

Why: Turning tallies into sentences is where the vocabulary becomes grammar. The frames are printed, so the effort goes into the data and not into inventing a sentence shape.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.86: turn the tally marks into sentences and find the surprise

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of four pool their tallies and write three sentences with the frames printed on the page: In our class, lots of / some / a few people already do these activities: …
  2. The frames are given, so the work is entirely in the data. Four minutes, one reporter per group.
  3. Each group reads one sentence to the class. Write the three activities nobody does on the board — that is always the interesting column.
  4. The book's own closing question, and it is a good one: Were you surprised by any of your results? Why?
📺 On screen: the three sentence frames copied onto the board so every group works from the same shape — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: pool the data and report. Expect spending time online and listening to music at the top and collecting at the bottom. Typical error: lots of people likes — the frame prevents most of it, so correct it once and move on

Reading · what is geocaching?
class → solo
Why: One gist question on a text they cannot possibly know the answer to. It buys the reading a reason to exist before any comprehension question is asked.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.88: the photos and the title, then one gist question and two minutes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Photos and title only, books open at p.88, nothing read yet (TB p.173): What do you think they are looking for? What do you think is in the box? What is the boy writing?
  2. Say the word and let them hear it twice — /ˈdʒiːəʊˌkæʃɪŋ/ — and give them the one clue that cracks it: Geo- is the same geo- as in geography.
  3. Two minutes of silent reading with ONE question: was your guess right? Nothing else is asked yet.
  4. Take three answers and confirm: SB p.88, a blog by Lucy Barton, aged 14. She started geocaching because of her uncle, who has done it for years. A geocache is a box someone else has hidden; you find it with an app, and there are two million of them wor…
  5. Background for you, not for them: TB p.173: geocaching is the technological grandchild of letterboxing, where clues travelled by word of mouth and finders signed a log book. GPS made exact coordinates possible; the first cache, in 2000, was a black bucket with a can of beans in it. It is not uncontroversial — some people call the caches litter — but in 2008 two hikers lost in the Oregon snow found a geocache and could tell rescuers exactly where they were.
📺 On screen: the blog on the screen so the photos are big while they predict, then the text itself (Backup: all of it is printed on SB p.88 — work from the paper book, and if you want the photos bigger, hold the book up and walk the rows)
👦 Students do: predict from the photos, then read once for the idea. Expect «treasure hunt» within three guesses. Typical problem: somebody who already plays it tells everybody the answer — give that student the job of checking whether the text agrees

Reading · how the questions work
class
Why: The strategy stage comes BEFORE the questions, because without it students answer from memory and from what sounds sensible rather than from the text.

WHAT WE DO: the strategy before the questions: what a three-option question is actually asking

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.173: the five questions follow the order of the text, but the LAST one is usually general — «what is the writer doing in this text?». The questions themselves carry information: question 3 tells you a cache is a box, question 4 tells you Lucy talks about the future. Read the question before hunting in the text, not after.
  2. Show it on question 1 rather than saying it. Read question one. Is it asking what geocaching is, or what we learn about Lucy? (about Lucy). That single distinction is worth two of the five marks.
  3. Walk question 1 through as a class exactly as TB p.173 sets it out: TB p.173, for the reading: walk question 1 through as a class. Where is the answer? (first paragraph) Who started geocaching, Lucy or her uncle? (her uncle) So A is wrong. Does she like it? (yes) Does she say she didn't like it at first? (no) So B is wrong. What words does Lucy use to say she sometimes goes alone? (by myself).
  4. Write the rule of the stage on the board and leave it there: Answer, then line. No line, no answer.
📺 On screen: question 1 and its three options on the screen, taken apart live (Backup: it is printed on SB p.88 — copy the three options onto the board and cross them out one at a time as the class rules them out)
👦 Students do: watch one question being solved properly. Expect them to want to move on quickly — hold them, this is the stage that decides the score. Typical problem: they say «C because it's true» rather than «C because line four says by myself»

Reading · the five questions
solo → pairs
Why: The hardest reading format of the course so far: five three-option questions where the options are paraphrases. The rule of the room — an answer needs a line from the text.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.88 (track 115): five three-option questions, answered alone then proved in pairs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Silent mode, 6 minutes, alone. The instruction is one line: Five questions. Five underlined lines. Go.
  2. Play track 115 once while they check, so they hear the text at speed with their answers already on the page.
  3. Kagan RallyCoach in pairs, 4 minutes: partner A gives an answer, partner B may only accept it if A reads the line out. If there is no line, the pair goes back to the text.
  4. Reveal together. Key: 1 C · 2 A · 3 C · 4 B · 5 C.
  5. Take the two that split the room and ask for the line out loud, from two different students, before you confirm anything.
📺 On screen: track 115 in the board app with the text on the screen (Backup: the text is printed on SB p.88 and there is nothing in the recording that is not on the page — read the blog aloud yourself at natural speed while they check, or simply skip the listen-and-check step, which costs the lesson nothing)
👦 Students do: answer alone, then prove in pairs. Expect 3–4 out of 5 before the pair check and 4–5 after it. Typical error: question 2 — they choose the option about films because films are mentioned, when the point is that Lucy is young
SB p.88 ex.2 · track 115
72–80′
Why the other options are wrong
pairs → class
Why: Explaining why a wrong option is wrong is harder than choosing the right one, and it is the only thing that transfers to the next text. This is where the actual teaching happens.

WHAT WE DO: the distractor analysis: for each question, say why the OTHER two are wrong

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Numbered Heads Together: each pair takes one question and must produce a sentence beginning B is wrong because …
  2. Feed them the evidence where they stick. Question 1: Lucy's UNCLE has done it for years, so «longer than her uncle» is the opposite of the text. Question 3: the text says you put your name in the book — «check how many people found it» is never mentioned at all.
  3. Name the trap once, write it on the board, and make three students say it back: They pick the option that repeats a word from the text. The correct option is almost always paraphrased; the wrong ones copy the wording [exam report]
  4. Then the extra half-turn that costs thirty seconds and is worth it: In the Russian exam there is a third option — «the text says nothing about this». Question three would be exactly that. One sentence, and move on.
📺 On screen: the five questions and their evidence lines on the screen, revealed one at a time (Backup: read the evidence line out from the book for each question and write the key phrase on the board)
👦 Students do: explain why each wrong option is wrong. Expect this to be harder than choosing the right one, which is exactly why it is here. Typical error: «because it's wrong» — push for the line every single time

🎯 Talking points · would you try it
groups of 4
Why: The productive outcome: an opinion with a reason, using the unit's vocabulary about hobbies. Short, spoken, and it tells you who has the words and who has not.

WHAT WE DO: the Talking points box p.88: what makes it a good hobby, and would you do it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two questions from the box: What makes geocaching a good hobby? Would you like to try it? Why or why not?
  2. Set the standard before they start, and it is the standard of the whole course: «Yes» is not an answer. «Yes, because …» is.
  3. Groups of four, four minutes, everybody speaks. The listener's job is to ask one follow-up question — Who would you go with?
  4. Two groups report. Take a show of hands on the second question and write the split on the board — it is the class's own data, like the survey.
  5. Log errors silently on your correction sheet; do not correct aloud.
📺 On screen: the two Talking points questions and the phrase Yes, because … in chalk — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: give an opinion with a reason and ask one follow-up. Expect the free-time vocabulary to come back in unprompted, which is the point of putting it here. Typical error: I'm interesting in it — note it silently, it is next lesson's opener

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class

Structure
In our class, lots of people already do these activities: … · A few people want to try … · No one likes … · I'd like to try it because …
Form
the activities are gerund phrases and stay whole: playing an instrument, going out with friends, spending time online. After like, enjoy and be interested in the ‑ing form is the one that works.
Pron. watch
word stress inside the phrase — PLAYing an INstrument, GOing out with FRIENDS. And /ˈdʒiːəʊˌkæʃɪŋ/ for geocaching, which nobody guesses.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I've played the guitar for two years.» Do I play the guitar now? → yes. That is why it is not the past simple.
«three years» — for or since? → for. It is an amount of time.
«I'm not at all interested in this.» Is that stronger or weaker than «I don't do this»? → much stronger. It means you would not even try it.
Question 1 of the reading: does it ask what geocaching IS, or what we learn about LUCY? → about Lucy. Half the wrong answers come from answering the other question.
«The app takes you close to the cache.» Does that mean the app finds it for you? → no. Read to the end: then there is a puzzle.

The questions themselves carry information: question 3 tells you a cache is a box, question 4 tells you Lucy talks about the future. Read the question before hunting in the text, not after.
Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The survey turns into six conversations in RussianThe survey has a shape and the shape prevents it: one question, one tally mark, move on. Model it for ten seconds with a strong student, then set the rule out loud — Ask, mark, move. You have nine minutes and eleven people.
They answer the reading questions in twenty seconds and get three of fiveThat is the expected first result and it is what the strategy stage is for. Do not give the key: send them back with one instruction — Underline the line that proves it. If you cannot find the line, the answer is a guess.
Somebody says all fifteen activities are boringTake it seriously for exactly thirty seconds and use it: Good — then you are the person who answers «I'm not at all interested in this» eleven times. Go and do it honestly. The data is more interesting when somebody dissents.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
