🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Ask and answer Have you ever…? questions in the present perfect — with correct irregular participles — and choose been or gone by asking «is the person still there?»
What is genuinely hard
Two separate hurdles that look like one: the participles (worn, chosen, written — a memory problem) and the concept (experience without a time — a thinking problem). Russian has one past tense where English splits two, and one verb of motion where English splits been from gone.
Typical error to expect
I have never weared it · have you ever buyed…? — a regular ending glued onto an irregular participle [high priority, A2/B1]
She has been to the shops said about someone who is still out — Russian has one verb where English splits been (came back) from gone (still there) [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The participles are handled FIRST and as a game (the TB's own Bingo), because every later stage dies if the forms are cold. The rules are then extracted from sentences the class met in Alison's article last lesson — discovery, not presentation. been/gone gets its own stage with its own CCQs because it is a concept split, not a memory item. The book's p.122 pairwork drills the question shape in a controlled frame before the productive mingle (Find someone who…) opens the room. The written speed round near the end exists because the test in two weeks checks spelling, and worn/wore survives any purely oral lesson.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Have you ever worn something exactly once? Have you ever regretted buying something? By the end of today you can ask anyone about their whole life in one tense — and you'll know why ‘she's gone to the shops' means something different from ‘she's been to the shops'. One letter, different story.”

WHAT WE DO: the TB warmer + ex.2: ten irregular participles at speed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Run the TB warmer: TB warmer — participle Bingo: irregular verbs on the board; pairs choose six and write them down; you call infinitives, they write the participles; first pair with all six shouts Bingo.

📺 On screen: the verbs in chalk and the pairs' own six-word Bingo cards — paper and board are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: choose six, write participles at speed, shout Bingo. Expect worn and chosen to be the casualties. Typical error: payed — flag it now, it returns in the speed round

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: rules from Alison's sentences + ex.3 controlled practice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 — four example sentences, five rules to complete. Pairs get 3 minutes. Key: rules: b past participle · c never, past participle · d ever · e -ed (TB: do not explain been/gone at this stage — it has its own exercise).
  2. Point back at lesson 1: «I've bought hundreds of things online» — Alison's sentence. Does she say WHEN? No. That is the whole idea.
  3. ex.3 solo against the clock (3 minutes), pair check. Key: 1 has won · 2 have never bought · 3 've never used · 4 has never borrowed · 5 've visited · 6 've never had · 7 haven't met · 8 've never stolen.
  4. Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Item eight — number twos! (I've never stolen anything — say it with drama.)
  5. Concept check the never items: How many times? Zero. Can I add «last year»? No — no dates.
📺 On screen: the rules land under the HAVE + DONE shape in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: extract the rules, then produce eight forms. Expect item 4 (has never borrowed — third person) to catch the has/have split. Typical error: have never buyed — point at the Bingo corner
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare4_Unit9_Grammar_Present_Perfect_A5
A5 grammar card — PRESENT PERFECT: PAST + NOW: the formula have/has + past participle with the negative and the question, and the three uses — EXPERIENCE (I've visited Paris.) · RECENT RESULT (She's lost her receipt.) · UNFINISHED TIME (We've bought two things today.) — plus the CRITICAL CONTRAST been vs gone (Mia has been to the bank = she went and came back / Mia has gone to the bank = she is still there) and the time clue that no finished past time is possible (I've bought it yesterday ✘)
  • Glue the card in and mark the three uses in three colours.
  • Say the been / gone pair aloud — it is the difference the exam checks.
  • Write three sentences of your own, one per use.
  • Rule check: a finished time in the sentence means the past simple.
When: ~5 min at the start of the “Grammar · discovery” stagePrep: 1 × A5 per pupil. Scissors, glue sticks, pencils.

Grammar · been or gone
pairs → class
Why: One concept split carries the whole stage: been = came back, gone = still there. Russian has one verb for both, so the split needs its own minutes and its own CCQs.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5 the split + ex.6 practice + Showdown

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.5, the two sentences: match them to meanings. Key: been/gone: 1 b (gone = travelling / there now) · 2 a (been = went earlier, back now). Then the fork on the board gets its arrows: BEEN ⇄ (there and back) · GONE → (still there).
  2. Concept checks from the plan header — the London pair, cold-called.
  3. ex.6 solo, 3 minutes, pair check. Key: 1 Have, been · 2 has gone · 3 has gone · 4 have, been · 5 have, been · 6 has, gone.
  4. Kagan Showdown on mini-whiteboards, six scenarios you call: Sally is at the pool right now. (gone) · Dad came home from the shops. (been) · Mum is on the way to Grandma's. (gone)… Teams write, show on three, justify one per round.
  5. Signpost: Grammar reference page one four six — tonight, two minutes, if you want it as a table.
📺 On screen: scenarios read aloud, answers on mini-whiteboards — voice and boards are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: match, choose, commit on boards. Expect item 4 (Where have you BEEN? — you're back now) to be the one that needs the arrows. Typical error: gone for every motion — the still-there question is the fix

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 → SB p.122: the controlled interview with participles

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books to p.122. First fill the participle column solo: ex.4 pairwork participles (SB p.122): 2 lent · 3 taken · 4 lost · 5 received · 6 regretted · 7 bought · 8 saved — tick or cross the You column, then interview your partner with Have you ever…?.

WHAT WE DO: present perfect questions across the whole room

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Grid of eight on the board: Find someone who… has worn something only once · has never played volleyball · has paid too much for something · has read a book in English · has been to another country · has travelled by plane alone · has made something with their hands · has never lost their phone.
  2. Rules: Full questions only — Have you ever…? Short answer + one follow-up. A name per box, no name twice.

  4. You walk with a paper: log participle and short-answer slips SILENTLY.
  5. Debrief in the third person: Who found someone who has been to another country? Tell me about them.

WHAT WE DO: the written round that catches what the oral lesson hides

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Showdown, mini-whiteboards, eight calls: you say the infinitive IN a sentence, teams write the participle: wear — I've never ___ it! (worn) · pay — She's ___ me back. (paid) · write · choose · hear · try · travel · steal.
  2. Boards up on three; spelling counts, the contraction counts.
  3. Every miss goes into a «watch out» corner — it feeds the review lesson and the block test.
  4. Last call, the been/gone fork one more time: Your partner isn't here. Where's he ___? (gone)
📺 On screen: calls by voice, answers on mini-whiteboards — voice and boards are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write at speed, show, argue the spellings. Expect a visible drop from the oral round — that gap is the whole reason this stage exists. Typical error: payed, choosen

Structure
Have you ever bought clothes you have never worn? — Yes, I have. / No, I haven't. · I've never used a credit card. · Sally isn't here — she's gone swimming. · I've been to the shops (and I'm back).
Form
have/has + past participle; negatives with not/never; questions with ever; been = went and came back · gone = went and is still there; irregular list on SB p.158.
Pron. watch
worn /wɔːn/ ≠ won /wʌn/ · heard /hɜːd/ · I've /aɪv/ — the contraction must survive in questions and answers
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I've been to London» — am I in London now? → no: I went and came back. «She's gone to London» — where is she? → still there (or on the way).
«Have you ever eaten sushi?» — am I asking WHEN? → no: ever asks about your whole life, no date attached.
«I've never worn them» — how many times have I worn them? → zero. Can I add «last year» to that sentence? → no: a past time needs the past simple.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«have you ever buyed» — regular endings on irregular verbsThe Bingo warmer front-loads the forms; the p.158 list is signposted for home. Every slip in speech gets the board point-and-wait, not a spoken correction.
been and gone both translated as «ходил»TB mixed ability: weaker pairs ask themselves three questions per item — Is the person there now? (gone) · Have they come back? (been) · Ever in your life? (been).
Past time phrases sneak in («I've been there last year»)Do not open the contrast today — park it: Hold that thought until lesson five. Today: no dates allowed. Write «no dates» in the corner of the board.
Short answers collapse to yes/noThe rule of the round: a bare yes costs the point — Yes, I have earns it. Drill the two short answers chorally before the pairwork.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
