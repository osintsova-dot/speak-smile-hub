WHAT WE DO: 8 adjectives → superlative form in writing in 3 min; key; private scoring

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Slide: big · happy · expensive · good · bad · slow · thin · interesting.
  2. Write the superlative. With ‘the’. Three minutes. This is spelling, not English — and spelling is where the test points live.
  3. Key: the biggest · the happiest · the most expensive · the best · the worst · the slowest · the thinnest · the most interesting.
  4. Reveal: ‘The most big’? ‘The bigest’? ‘The slowest’ — did you double the W? Good news: five rules fix all of it. Six minutes.
📺 On screen: gate slide + key · timer (Backup: everything on the slide goes onto the board before the lesson (or onto printed cards, one per crew).)
👦 Students do: solo, they write 8 forms, count the clean ones. Expected: 4–6 out of 8; doubling and most/-est fall apart. Typical error: “the most good” — the most valuable one, we go over it at the sprint.

⚡ Base sprint · the table and the rules as a race
desks · crews

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 the rule → ex.2 the table by crews in 4 min → ex.3 matching rules a–e; a traffic light on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ex.1 p.102 in 60 sec: Three or more things · the + superlative · the opposite of most is LEAST.
  2. Ex.2 ▶ a 4-min crew race: fill in the table (the biggest · the healthiest · the most/least popular · the most/least dangerous · the best · the worst). First clean crew +3.
  3. I build a rule traffic light (ex.3): 🟢 1 syllable → the + -est · 🟢 CVC (except -w) → doubling · 🟡 2 syllables ending in -y → -iest · 🔴 3+ syllables → the most/least · ⭐ irregular good/bad. Key ex.3: a→3 · b→1 · c→4,5 · d→6,7 · e→2.
  4. Pronunciation: -est is /ɪst/ · least /liːst/ · worst — silent r, long /ɜː/. (TB p.116).
📺 On screen: SB p.102 · board: rule traffic light (stays up to the end of the lesson)
👦 Students do: they fill in the table for speed, tie the rules to the traffic light. Expected: the most/-est rule clicks through counting syllables. Typical error: they count the syllables in “interesting” as 3 — we clap it out by syllable.

🚀 GRAMMAR ROUND-UP · cumulative U1–8
desks · crews

WHAT WE DO: Quiz-Quiz-Trade: ex.7 p.102 “Is it a plane? Is it a car?” — 9 items on the whole year's grammar; a crew race with reasoning

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. I raise the status: This one is not about Unit 8. It's about EVERYTHING you learned this year. Nine gaps, four minutes.
  2. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with the unit's printed cards (handout GIA1-QQT: question on top, answer on the back after folding; no printer — the kids write their own cards in 2 min).
  3. Commands: Stand up, hand up, pair up! Quiz your partner — praise — TRADE cards — find a new partner! 3–4 swaps, EVERYONE asks at the same time.
  4. Strategy hint (TB p.116): Read the whole sentence around the gap and guess BEFORE you look at the options.
  5. Crews, 4 min. Key: 1 b · 2 a · 3 c · 4 b · 5 b · 6 b · 7 a · 8 c · 9 a.
  6. Reasoning for points: Why b in 1? — a flying car is singular and it's the past → was. Why c in 3? — 'didn't' comes before, so we need the infinitive. +2 for the explanation, +1 for just the right answer.
  7. Facts for flavour: The TF-X flies at 160 km/h and costs $400,000.
📺 On screen: SB p.102 ex.7 on screen/in the book · timer (Backup: everything on the slide goes onto the board before the lesson (or onto printed cards, one per crew).)
👦 Students do: they race the cumulative round-up by crews and defend their choices. Expected: 6–8 out of 9; they trip on 3 and 8. Typical error: item 3 (didn't + infinitive) — our anchor again, they recognise it.

WHAT WE DO: PERFORM: the ticket office under pressure — sceptical clerk, the 5-second rule, a mandatory superlative; peer review ex.5

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. I set up the “ticket office”: a desk, paper pounds, a PLATFORM sign. Complicators on the slide: 1 the clerk can answer Sorry, that train is full — the passenger must ask for an alternative; 2 a pause longer than 5 sec = the queue gets annoyed; 3 a superlative must appear in the dialogue.
  2. First EVERYONE in parallel in threes (clerk — passenger — judge with a checklist), rotation 3×90 sec.
  3. Then 3 pairs go “on air” to the class. Peer review ex.5 for the audience: Where is the passenger going and how much does the ticket cost? Which Key phrases do they use?

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, learners can form and use superlative adjectives accurately (the biggest · the healthiest · the most expensive · the best/worst) and run a ticket-office dialogue using 5+ Key phrases including «What's the fastest way?».

Target language
Lexis
What's the fastest way?How much does it cost?Where do I get the train?What time's the next train?Is that single or return?a singlea returna travelcardplatform
Structure
It's the fastest way to travel. · Cycling is one of the healthiest ways to get to school. · What's the fastest way to Liverpool? — Take the 10:15 train and change at Birmingham. · Is that single or return?
Form
the + -est (old→oldest) · CVC doubling (big→biggest, but NOT slow→slowest, we don't double -w) · -y→-iest (happy→happiest) · 2+ syllables → the most/least · irregular: good→the best, bad→the worst · always THE · “the best place IN the world”, not “of”.
Pron. watch
-est = /ɪst/ · least /liːst/ · worst — silent r, long /ɜː/ (TB p.116) · polite intonation at the ticket office: an even rising tone, no sharp “How much?!”
Concept checks (CCQs) · Assumptions
“Two things — superlative?” → No, three or more. Two — comparative.
“The most slow?” → No: the SLOWEST. Most — only for long adjectives.
“A return ticket — is that only one way back?” → No: there AND back. Single — one way only.

Assumptions: Comparatives were drilled in U5 (Tech life), superlatives came up in GMF4 — but spelling and “the” are shaky. Transport and collocations are active after P1–P2. Gate is diagnosis, not humiliation: the ceiling must be REAL (didn't + infinitive / question word order / superlative spelling), results are private tallies, the reveal is “so THIS is our growth zone”.

Classroom management — signals
«Eyes up in 3-2-1» — count down calmly → all eyes on you at «1». No shouting over noise, ever.
Visible timer — timer on screen = we work; timer stops = pens/hands down. The timer is the boss, not you.
Volume dial — arm up/down = louder/quieter; «radio whisper» for rehearsals.
«Boards up!» — mini-whiteboard rounds: everyone commits, all boards up together — nobody hides, nobody shouts first.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, class answers with two and freezes — instant reset mid-game.
