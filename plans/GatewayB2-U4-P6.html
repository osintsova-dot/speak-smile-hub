🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Work through the four Exam success tasks for Units 3–4 with the strategy named for each: key word transformation (SB p.56), comparing two photographs and answering a question about them (p.56), note completion from a recording (p.145) and a guided essay of 140–190 words (p.145); then launch Collaborative project 2 — a genius from your country.
What is genuinely hard
Each task fails for a different reason and each has a fixable rule. Transformations fail because students change the given word or write six words; photo comparison fails because students describe instead of comparing and never answer the question the examiner actually asked; note completion fails because students write a whole clause where one or two words go; the essay fails because the third point — «your own idea» — is quietly ignored.
Typical error to expect
«It was such lovely beach that …» — the transformation needs the article: such a lovely beach. Every transformation is marked all-or-nothing, so one missing word is one lost mark.
Writing «he was not conscious» instead of «conscious» in note completion — the words around the gap already supply the grammar; copy exactly what you hear.
Why the lesson is built this way
This is the one lesson in the unit where the exam is the figure rather than the ground, and it is built as a carousel: strategy box → do the task → check with the reason. Reading and speaking come from p.56, listening and writing from p.145, and each half is broken by movement so that four exam formats in ninety minutes do not feel like a mock. The project launch at the end is deliberate: it sends them out of a demanding lesson talking about something they chose themselves.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
No new grammar today. Four tasks that both your exams — Cambridge B2 First and the Russian EGE — use, and one rule for each that turns a lost mark into a mark. Then we form the project groups, and you choose a genius from your own country to make a case for.

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting at the door, then the marked papers returned with one action each

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what is the best thing that happened to you this week? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — papers back. Hand the unit tests back face down, with two comments each: one specific strength, one specific fix. Give them sixty seconds to read in silence.
  3. Then one instruction: find one mark you lost for spelling and write the word correctly three times, now. No discussion of scores, no comparing papers.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): That was the unit. Today is the exam — the same language, four different shapes. Page 56.
📺 On screen: no display. Backup: not applicable — this stage is paper only.
👦 Students do: read the comments in silence, then rewrite one word three times.

5–20′
Reading · key word transformation, with the rules named
desks → pairs
Why: Transformations are a procedure, not a talent. Counting words aloud in the peer check fixes the single commonest reason the task scores zero.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.56 exercises 1–3 — the Cambridge Part 4 task, taught as a procedure

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the Reading exam tip aloud, then convert it into four rules on the board: (1) use the key word, do not change it. (2) two to five words. (3) contractions count as two. (4) the meaning must stay the same.
  2. Exercise 1 as a whole class, so the analysis is public: the example is It's been over a year since I last flew to Italy → I haven't flown to Italy for/in over a year. Ask the two questions printed in the book — which words stay the same (I, to Italy, over a year), and which part moves first (the subject and the verb).
  3. Exercise 2 (five transformations) individually, six minutes.
  4. Check in Rally Coach — and the coaching question is always the same: count your words. Answers: SB p.56 · example: I haven't flown to Italy for/in over a year. Exercise 2: 1 not as/so cold as · 2 such a lovely beach · 3 are better at science than · 4 learning · 5 disobey.
  5. Exercise 3, ninety seconds: which one was hardest and why? Take two answers. The «why» is where the strategy gets internalised.
  6. Name the grammar behind each item aloud as you check: comparison (not as cold as), such a + adjective + noun, be better at, consider + -ing, obey. Three of the five are this unit's grammar.
📺 On screen: the four rules on the board, staying up all lesson. Backup: the same tip box is printed on SB p.56 — read it aloud if the board is unusable.
👦 Students do: analyse the example together, transform five sentences alone, then count words in a partner's answers.

20–26′
Stirrer · the word-count sprint
open floor
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a fast standing drill on the two-to-five-word rule, using this unit's grammar

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. You say a full sentence; the class must call out the transformation in the given word, and then count the words in the air on their fingers.
  2. Six items, all from the unit: «Sasha is more resourceful than me» — AS (I'm not as resourceful as Sasha). «She was the best in the class» — ANYONE (she was better than anyone in the class). «I managed to finish it» — ABLE (I was able to finish it). «He couldn't swim then» — ABLE (he wasn't able to swim then).
  3. Anyone whose finger count goes past five sits down for one round and comes back — nobody is out for long.
  4. Ninety seconds of it is plenty. Finish standing and move straight to the photographs.
📺 On screen: no display; your voice and their fingers. Backup: none needed.
👦 Students do: call out the transformation and count the words on their fingers — every student does both, every item.

pairs
Why: One timed minute per student, twice, means every student in the room speaks for two minutes on an exam task — that is the whole value of the stage, and it is impossible in a front-of-class format.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.56 exercises 4–5 — the Cambridge Part 2 task, which is also the picture task of the Russian oral

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the Speaking exam tip and turn it into the one rule that matters: compare, do not describe. And answer the examiner's question — it is printed above the photos.
  2. Board the language they need, and make them copy it: Both photos show … · One similarity is … · One big difference is that … · whereas / while · In the first photo …, but in the second … · Perhaps / Maybe they … · In my view … (they met most of this in Unit 3 — say so, and ask them to add three more from memory).
  3. Exercise 4. Student A: photos a and b (people on a walking holiday by a lake; two girls with a map in a city) — compare them and say what you think the benefits of each holiday are. One minute, timed. Then Student B comments on A's photos in twenty seconds and says which holiday looks most enjoyable.
  4. Swap roles with Student B's task (SB p.150) so that both partners do a full minute.
  5. Exercise 5, three minutes: tell each other one thing your partner could have compared but didn't. That is the most useful feedback in the whole task.
  6. Two volunteer pairs repeat in front of the class if the room is warm enough for it. Correct comparison structures only.
📺 On screen: the comparison frames on the board. Backup: if you were planning to project the photos, the book has them on p.56 and p.150 — paper is the primary source here, not the screen.
👦 Students do: speak for a full timed minute each, then comment on the partner's photos, then give one concrete improvement.

44–58′
Listening · note completion, one or two words only
desks
Why: Note completion is a discipline, not a comprehension test: word class predicted first, exact words written second. Both hearings are used for different jobs.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.145 exercise 6, recording ES2 — the story of Phineas Gage, ten gaps

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the Listening exam tip and convert it to three rules: (1) the notes are in the order you hear them. (2) think what KIND of word is missing before you listen. (3) one or two words, exactly as you hear them — do not rewrite.
  2. Ninety seconds of silent prediction: for each gap, write N for noun, ADJ for adjective, NUM for number. Take two or three predictions aloud.
  3. Play ES2 once. Then thirty seconds in pairs, then play it again. That second hearing is not a kindness — it is the exam format.
  4. Check with the answers and, for the three hardest, quote the line that gave it: SB p.145, recording ES2 (Phineas Gage): a employers · b material · c conscious · d ridiculous stories · e basic maths · f (human) behaviour · g evidence · h extreme · i facts · j decision.
  5. Exercise 7, four minutes in pairs: How far do you believe personality can change? What kind of events might change yours? That is the unit's topic closing the exam task.
📺 On screen: recording — Exam success track ES2 in the board app, cued before the lesson. Backup: the full script is printed in the Teacher's Book (TB p.138) — read it aloud at a steady pace, twice, exactly as the exam would.
👦 Students do: predict the word class, listen twice, compare in pairs, then discuss the follow-up question.

58–72′
Writing · the guided essay, planned in the room
desks → pairs
Why: A guided essay is won at the planning stage. Doing the plan and the opening in class means the piece that comes back is a real sample of their writing, not of their evening.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.145 exercises 8–9 — a 140–190-word essay with three given points, planned and opened here

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the task with them and pull out the answers before anyone writes: How many words? What is the topic? What three points must you include? (140–190 · whether we should stop travelling by plane · the distance of some journeys, protecting the environment, and your own idea).
  2. Say the thing that costs the most marks: point three is «your own idea». It is not optional. If you skip it, you lose content marks however good the English is.
  3. Board the paragraph plan: 1 introduce and state your position · 2 distance · 3 environment · 4 your own idea · 5 conclusion.
  4. Four minutes of planning in note form, then RallyRobin: pairs exchange their «own idea» — a stolen idea is fine, it is the writing that is assessed.
  5. Six minutes of silent writing: the introduction and paragraph 2 only. The rest is homework — but the hardest part is done here.
  6. Exercise 9 next lesson: they will swap and use the Writing checklist on SB p.141.
📺 On screen: the five-paragraph plan and the word count on the board. Backup: the same plan can be dictated in one minute; the Writing checklist is on SB p.141 in the book.
👦 Students do: answer the three set-up questions, plan in notes, swap ideas with a partner, then write two paragraphs in silence.

🎯 Collaborative project 2 · «A genius from your country»
groups of three or four
Why: The project is the unit's long-form output and it is launched at the end of a demanding lesson on purpose — the room leaves talking about something it chose.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.57 — the project is chosen, the roles are agreed and the deadline is set

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Recall the Culture exchange text from Lesson 3 in thirty seconds using prompts on the board: astronomer · mathematician · the Principia · the story about the apple · mercury.
  2. Form groups of three or four — deliberately different from the last project's groups, and mixed by ability.
  3. While they read the project task, board three questions: Who are you doing the project FOR? What is the aim? Who is it going to be about?
  4. Groups shortlist candidates from the Research areas, then vote. Push them to justify the choice by three criteria: the content they can find, the audience, and the skills in the group (who draws, who edits video, who speaks).
  5. Read the tips aloud in turn — Digital skills, Collaboration, Intercultural awareness — and take one discussion question from each: What is the difference between constructive and negative criticism? Is it a problem if your genius is not well-known abroad?
  6. Set the deadline out loud and write it on the board, plus one interim checkpoint. A project without a date is a project that does not happen.
📺 On screen: the three set-up questions and the deadline on the board. Backup: everything needed is printed on SB p.57; no technology is required today, only the phones they will use at home.
👦 Students do: form new groups, shortlist and vote, allocate roles by real skills, and write the deadline into their own diaries.

Workbook p.95 (Exam trainer)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will be better able to handle four B2 exam task types — key word transformation, comparing photographs, note completion and a guided essay — with a named strategy for each.
  • Subsidiary. Recycling the unit's grammar inside exam formats; collaborative planning and role allocation for the project; giving concrete peer feedback on a speaking task.
  • Personal. Do not let the strategy talk grow. Each tip box is ninety seconds, then they do the task — the strategies are learned in the doing.
By the end, students can…
  • transform a sentence using a given word in two to five words
  • compare two photographs for a full minute and answer the examiner's question
  • complete notes from a recording with one or two exact words
  • plan a guided essay so that all three given points are covered
  • agree a project topic, roles and a deadline in a group

Structure
Both photos show … · One big difference is that … · whereas … · Perhaps they're …ing · In my view … · It was such a lovely beach that … · They're better at science than I am.
Form
key word transformation: the given word unchanged, two to five words, contractions counted as two · comparison and speculation language for photographs · note completion: one or two words, exactly as heard · essay: five paragraphs, all three content points, 140–190 words.
Pron. watch
in the photo task, the contrast falls on the compared element: in the FIRST photo … but in the SECOND photo …. Flat stress here makes a comparison sound like a description, which is exactly the mark that is lost.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
Transformation: Can you change the given word? — No. How many words minimum? — Two. Maximum? — Five.
Photo task: Do you describe both photos in detail? — No. What do you do? — Compare them, and answer the question.
Note completion: Do you write a sentence? — No. How many words? — One or two, exactly as you hear them.
Essay: How many points must you write about? — Three. Is the third one optional? — No.

Assumptions: Students met photo comparison in Unit 3 and have written guided essays before. Transformations have appeared in the Grammar in context sections but not as a named exam task; note completion is the least familiar of the four.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Four exam formats in one lesson feels like a mock and the energy drops.The stirrer at 20–26 and the project launch at the end are the structural answer. Also say the frame out loud at the start: no new grammar, four rules, one project.
Students describe instead of comparing in the photo task.The frames on the board force comparison syntax, and exercise 5 makes the partner name one missed comparison. If a pair still describes, give them the question again — the question is what forces comparison.
Transformations come back with six words or with the key word changed.Counting on fingers in the stirrer, then counting aloud in the peer check. Two minutes of drill saves the same mark every time it appears this year.
The essay is started but never finished at home.The introduction and one body paragraph are written in class, and the plan is in their notebooks. What is left is short and scaffolded — and it is checked next lesson in the peer-review exercise, which makes it socially visible.
The project groups form by friendship and one group has all the strong students.You form them, not they — mixed by ability and different from the last project, exactly as the Teacher's Book advises. Do it fast and do not explain.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
