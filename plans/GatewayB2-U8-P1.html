🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name and use thirty-nine words for extreme weather and natural disasters in three sets — the events themselves (avalanche, drought, flash flood, gale, heatwave, landslide, torrential rain, volcanic eruption), what happens to people and buildings (casualty, collapse, debris, survivor, tremor, aftershock, put out) and what happens afterwards (evacuate, evacuee, relief worker, aid, supplies, destruction) — and then read a 500-word article for four multiple-choice answers where the correct option always paraphrases the text.
What is genuinely hard
Two things, and neither is meaning. First, compound formation: the class will produce a rain storm, a fire forest, a wind gust — the elements are right and the order and the fixedness are not. Second, which word class: collapse and spread are verbs here, destruction is a noun, devastating is an adjective, and exercise 2 explicitly says the form must be changed.
Typical error to expect
«debris were everywhere», «many debris» — debris is uncountable and normally stands alone: there was debris everywhere. If you must be specific, a piece of debris.
Also expect victims and casualties used interchangeably. They are not: a casualty is somebody injured or killed in the event, a victim is anybody harmed by it, and a survivor is somebody who came through it. Draw the three on the board once.
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary arrives inside two news reports — one about an earthquake, one about a volcanic eruption — so that every word is read as information rather than as a list entry, and the two reports also supply the model for the speaking task at the end. Then the reading is genuinely interesting rather than dutiful (drones that carry a wi-fi signal fifty times further, robots competing in a simulated disaster zone in Germany, a solar-powered inflatable lamp invented by two architecture students after the Haiti earthquake), and its four questions are the exam mechanism of the whole course: the right answer never repeats the text's words.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
In 2018 alone there were more than three thousand one hundred floods, storms and other extreme events. That number has quadrupled since 1970, and nobody fully agrees why. Today you get the words to talk about all of it — and then you read about three pieces of technology that are already saving lives: a drone that carries the internet into a disaster zone, robots that compete every year to find people in a fake earthquake, and a lamp the size of a wallet that floats.

WHAT WE DO: greeting at the door, then reported speech aimed at weather news

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — the last time the weather changed your plans. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — rewind, Unit 7. On the board: Report one thing somebody told you this week — «She told me that …», «He asked me whether …». RallyRobin in pairs, then three aloud. Correct only the backshift.
  3. Then bend it straight into today: Now report one thing the news said about the weather this winter. That single sentence puts last unit's grammar inside this unit's topic, which is where revision belongs.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): You've just reported what the news said. This unit is about what the news reports. Page 96.
📺 On screen: the two prompts written on the board. Backup: say them aloud twice — this stage needs no equipment at all.
👦 Students do: one reported sentence each in pairs, then a second one on this unit's topic.

Why: It wakes the class in two minutes, it is genuinely competitive, and it fixes the single error the following stage would otherwise generate for the next three lessons.

WHAT WE DO: the Teacher's Book warmer, which pre-empts exactly the error the vocabulary will produce

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. Write on the board with the gaps exactly as shown: down____ · dust ____ · earth____ · heat____ · land____ · torrential ____ · volcanic ____
  2. Pairs, two minutes, complete all seven. Answers: downpour · dust storm · earthquake · heatwave · landslide · torrential rain · volcanic eruption.
  3. Then the second half of the warmer, which is the real teaching point: Which of these four nouns go with «storm» — sand, snow, rain, thunder? All four do. Write sandstorm · snowstorm · rainstorm · thunderstorm and let them see the pattern.
  4. Name the trap before it happens: The order never changes. «Fire forest» and «wind gust» do not exist. Learn them as one word.
  5. Finish with the frame for the lesson: Today: the words for when weather stops being weather and becomes news.
📺 On screen: the seven gapped compounds on the board. Backup: dictate them; the stage is board-and-voice only.
👦 Students do: complete seven compounds in pairs, then find four nouns that go with «storm».

WHAT WE DO: SB p.96 exercises 1, 2 and 3 with tracks 41, 42 and 43 — the words met inside real reports

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 1, the twenty events (track 41). Play it once, then let pairs decide which of the twenty could describe the two photos on the page (exercise 2 shows an earthquake, exercise 3 a volcanic eruption). Do not explain all twenty — elicit, and only explain what nobody has.
  2. Exercise 2, the earthquake report (track 42). Pre-teach the two that always need it: aftershock — a smaller earthquake that follows a bigger one and casualty — somebody injured or killed. Then the class completes the gapped report, changing the form where needed. Warn them in advance, in these words: Most of these words will not fit as they stand. You will have to change them.
  3. Check with the recording, then read the answers: track 42, the earthquake report: a casualties · b devastating · c tremor · d victims · e survivors · f debris · g collapsed · h put out · i aftershocks.
  4. Exercise 3, the eruption report (track 43). Before they write, the Teacher's Book move that turns a gap-fill into thinking: fours, one minute — If a volcano erupts, in what ORDER do these things happen, and why? Then complete and check: track 43, the eruption report: a evacuate · b ash · c molten lava · d spreading · e destruction · f sweeping down · g evacuees · h supplies · i aid · j relief workers. The photo is Mount Mayon, near Legazpi City in the Philippines, January 2018 — an active volcano that has erupted about fifty times in five hundred years.
  5. One grammar aside worth thirty seconds, because it comes back in Unit 9: on the board, rocks and landslides sweeping down the mountain — ask what has been left out. (which are — a reduced relative clause.)
  6. Board the three words the group hesitated over most and drill them chorally: almost always debris /ˈdebriː/, drought and avalanche.
📺 On screen: tracks 41, 42 and 43 played from the class audio. Backup: if the audio fails, read the two reports aloud yourself from the Student's Book — they are eight lines each — and for track 41 simply model the twenty words and have the class repeat. Nothing in this stage is lost without the recording.
👦 Students do: decide which words fit the photos, complete two gapped reports in the correct form, sequence the stages of an eruption in fours.

WHAT WE DO: five minutes of movement that also sorts the new words by what they describe

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Label three corners: WATER · EARTH · FIRE AND AIR.
  2. Read the words out one at a time — avalanche, drought, landslide, tsunami, wildfire, gale, flash flood, tremor, dust storm, heatwave, hurricane, torrential rain — and the class walks to the corner they think it belongs to. Five seconds per word, no discussion.
  3. Two or three will genuinely split the room (avalanche and dust storm always do). Take one sentence of justification from each corner before you move on. That is the language point of the stage.
  4. Sit them down on a fast one: Last word — landslide. Go.
📺 On screen: the three corner labels on paper. Backup: none needed — the labels can be shouted rather than posted.
👦 Students do: walk to a corner for each of twelve words and justify one choice out loud.

🎯 Use it · a disaster that was in the news
pairs → whole class
Why: The first productive outcome of the lesson, with the vocabulary target counted out loud by a peer so that using the words is a rule of the game rather than an optional extra.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.96 exercises 4 and 5 — the new words used to talk about something real

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 4, three questions, Timed Pair Share, ninety seconds each way: Is any extreme weather common where you live? Has anybody ever had to be evacuated here? Would you consider volunteering as a relief worker? The first one always produces the local answer — snowstorms, blizzards, gales — and that is the point.
  2. Exercise 5, the productive task: Talk about a famous natural disaster that was, or is, in the news. Use words from exercises 1, 2 and 3. Two minutes' preparation in note form, then three minutes of talking.
  3. Make the vocabulary requirement explicit rather than hoped for: I am listening for six words from this page. Your partner counts them on their fingers.
  4. Two pairs report to the class. While they talk, the rest tick off on their own page every word from exercises 1–3 that they hear — the Teacher's Book move, and it keeps the listeners working.
  5. Follow up with the fact the class will want: Most earthquakes happen around the Pacific Rim — the Ring of Fire — because that is where the tectonic plates move: Japan, Indonesia, the Philippines, New Zealand, Chile, Peru.
📺 On screen: nothing on screen. Backup: no equipment at any point — this is a spoken stage.
👦 Students do: answer three personal questions in pairs, then describe a real disaster using at least six of the new words while a partner counts them.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.97 exercises 1 and 2 — three devices, three predictions, then a fast check

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open at page 97. Three photographs across the top: a drone carrying something over water, a tracked robot next to a person lying down, a small folded lamp.
  2. Exercise 1 in pairs, two minutes: What do you think these are, and how could they help in a natural disaster? Push for modals of speculation, which they did in Unit 5: It could be … · It must be … · It might be used for …
  3. Take three or four guesses on the board without confirming any of them.
  4. Exercise 2: read the whole article quickly — three minutes, timed — and check the predictions. Say the purpose out loud so nobody starts translating: Three minutes. You are not reading every word. You are checking three guesses.
  5. Check against the three predictions: 1 drones — assess damaged areas, help restore power, search and rescue, and extend the reach of a wi-fi signal up to fifty times · 2 rescue robots — could help rescue casualties or survivors · 3 emergency lighting — can light up disaster areas.
📺 On screen: the three guesses written on the board, then ticked or crossed. Backup: the photographs are in the students' own book — nothing to project.
👦 Students do: speculate about three photographs with modals, then skim a 500-word article against the clock to check.

Reading for detail · four questions, and why the answer is a paraphrase
desks → pairs
Why: Multiple choice on a B2 text is a paraphrase-detection skill, not a reading speed skill. Requiring a line of evidence for every answer converts a guessing game into a reading lesson.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.97 exercise 3 with track 44 — the exam mechanism of the whole course, taught explicitly

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the Exam tip on the page aloud, then say the sentence that matters in your own words: The correct option will almost never use the same words as the text. If an option repeats the text word for word, be suspicious of it.
  2. Give the four-step routine and write it on the board, because they will need it in every unit from here to May: (1) read all three options carefully — sometimes the difference is one word; (2) find the section of the text the question comes from; (3) read that section again slowly; (4) eliminate what you know is wrong.
  3. Play track 44 while they follow the text — the second exposure — then eight minutes on the four questions, alone first.
  4. Numbered Heads Together in fours: agree the four answers and, for each one, agree which line of the text proves it. Then call a number.
  5. Take the answers with the justification, never without it: Which line? Read it to me. 1 c (the one certainty is that the need for these technologies will only increase — a and b are still being debated) · 2 c (both a and b: relief workers can coordinate AND people in the zone can use their smartphones) · 3 b (the ultimate aim is a higher degree of autonomy; robots are only being used in SIMULATIONS at present) · 4 c (Anna and Andrea created a solar-powered inflatable LED that is waterproof and floats)
  6. Push once on question 3, which is the one that separates the group: Why is «a» wrong? The text says robots are used in SIMULATIONS. It does not say they are already finding real victims. That distinction — what the text says versus what we assume — is worth two minutes.
📺 On screen: track 44 played while they read, plus the four-step routine written on the board. Backup: if the audio fails, have them reread the relevant paragraph silently instead — the recording is a second exposure to a text they already have in front of them, and the stage works without it. The class audio script is in the Teacher's Book, printed page 131.
👦 Students do: read the options first, answer alone, then agree answers and evidence in fours and be called by number.

pairs
Why: Meaning-from-context is the vocabulary skill that transfers to an unseen exam text; going to the dictionary second rather than first is what makes it a skill rather than a lookup.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.97 exercise 4 plus the Teacher's Book fast-finisher questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 4, five minutes, pairs, dictionaries closed: work out quadrupled · reach · perform · ultimate · inflatable · folded from the sentences they sit in. Then check with a dictionary — in that order, not the other way round.
  2. Answers: quadruple = become four times bigger · reach (n) = the distance something can travel · perform = do an activity or action · ultimate = final, best or most important · inflatable = has to be filled with air before you use it · fold (v) = bend a piece of paper or cloth and press one part over another.
  3. For anyone who finishes early, four text-detective questions from the Teacher's Book, written on the board: What does 3,100 refer to? What is the main reason for using drones in disaster relief? What is the Rescue Robot League? Who benefits from LuminAID? (3,100 = the number of floods, storms and other extreme events in 2018 · drones save lives · a German competition where international teams use robots to find victims in a simulated disaster area · victims of natural disasters who have no electric light.)
  4. Board quadruple next to double and triple — it is the one the class will actually use in three lessons' time, when the statistics lesson arrives.
📺 On screen: the four fast-finisher questions on the board. Backup: dictate them.
👦 Students do: negotiate six meanings from context in pairs before opening a dictionary; fast finishers hunt four facts in the text.

🎯 Critical thinkers · should big tech pay for this?
fours → whole class
Why: The productive outcome of the lesson: the new vocabulary used to argue a real position. Writing both sides before choosing is what turns an opinion into an argument, and it is exactly what task 38 of the Russian paper and the Cambridge essay both require.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.97 exercise 5 — a structured argument that recycles the unit's vocabulary under pressure

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board the question as it stands: Should big tech companies be spending more money on new technology for disaster relief? What makes you say that?
  2. Alone, three minutes, and this is the Teacher's Book instruction that makes the stage work: write reasons for both sides before deciding which one you are on. Two columns in the notebook.
  3. Round Robin in fours: one reason each, round the group, no repeats, twice round. Then the group must agree one sentence beginning In our opinion, … and one member reads it.
  4. Make the language requirement explicit: Your group sentence has to contain two words from page 96. I am listening for them.
  5. Model the level you want once, quickly: In my opinion they should. A drone costs almost nothing next to their profits, and if it means relief workers can coordinate on the first day, it saves lives. On the other hand, it is not really their job — that is what governments are for.
  6. Correct nothing during the discussion. Collect three errors and hold them for the next stage.
📺 On screen: the question and the two column headings on the board. Backup: nothing to fail — this is a spoken stage.
👦 Students do: write reasons on both sides alone, contribute in turn in fours, agree and read out one group position containing two unit words.

WHAT WE DO: five honest minutes on why the paraphrase rule earns marks in the Russian paper too

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Only for those sitting it — say so first: This next bit is for the people taking the EGE in May. Everyone else, listen if you're curious.
  2. Take question 1 from exercise 3 back to the board and show the mechanism nakedly: the text says experts are continuing to debate the causes; option a says the reason(s) why there has been an increase — the words match, and that is precisely why it is wrong.
  3. Tasks 12 to 18 of the Russian paper work the same way. Seven questions, four options each, and the option that shares the most words with the text is the trap. The correct one says the same idea in different words.
  4. Give them the one-line strategy to write down: Cover the options. Answer the question from the text in your own head. THEN look for the option that matches your answer.
  5. Close it and move on: That is the same routine we used on page 97. It is not exam technique — it is reading.
📺 On screen: the text sentence and the wrong option written side by side on the board. Backup: dictate both lines; five minutes, no equipment.
👦 Students do: compare a line of the text with a wrong option and see why the overlap is the trap.

Structure
Thousands of people had to be evacuated. · The fire spread through the city centre. · Firefighters have been working hard to put out the fires. · There was debris everywhere. · It could be a drone — it must be used for search and rescue.
Form
compounds are fixed and stressed on the first element: flash flood, dust storm, heatwave, landslide, wildfire · adjective + noun keeps two stresses: volcanic eruption, torrential rain · the same root shifts class and the exercise requires the shift: collapse (v) / collapsed, destroy → destruction (n), devastate → devastating (adj), evacuate (v) → evacuee (n) · debris, aid and destruction are uncountable.
Pron. watch
three traps in the word list and one rhythm. debris is /ˈdebriː/ — the final s is silent and every class says it; avalanche is /ˈævəlɑːnʃ/ with the stress right at the front; drought rhymes with out, not with ought. The rhythm is the compounds: ˈFLASH flood · ˈDUST storm · ˈHEATwave take the stress on the first element, but volˈcanic eˈruption and torˈrential ˈrain do not — adjective + noun keeps two stresses
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
evacuate: «They evacuated the village» — did the people stay? — No. Did they choose to leave, or were they told to? — They were told to. Is it safe there? — No.
casualty vs survivor: A casualty — is the person hurt? — Yes, or killed. A survivor — is the person hurt? — Maybe. Are they alive? — Yes.
aftershock: Does it come before or after the earthquake? — After. Is it bigger or smaller? — Smaller.
ICQ before the skim: How many minutes? Do you read every word? What are you looking for — three things or all the details?

Assumptions: Students can talk about the weather at B1 level (rain, snow, storm, hot, freezing) and have met modals of speculation in Unit 5 and reported speech in Unit 7. The disaster and relief vocabulary is new as production, though several items will be passively familiar from the news.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Twenty words in exercise 1 is too many to hold.They are not being learned in exercise 1 — they are being met. The learning happens in exercises 2 and 3, where nine and ten of them come back inside a text, and again in the corner sort and the speaking task. Do not drill all twenty; drill the three the class stumbles on.
Students write the base form in exercise 2 and lose every item.Say the warning before they start, in the Teacher's Book's own words: most of these will not fit as they stand. Do the first gap together on the board (casualty → casualties) and the rest will follow.
The reading is long and the four questions overrun.The three-minute skim is timed and non-negotiable; the detail work is eight minutes and then Numbered Heads. If the clock is tight, take questions 1 and 3 with full justification and give 2 and 4 as answers — those two are the straightforward ones.
The Critical thinkers discussion is one-sided and lasts four minutes.The both-sides column is written before anybody speaks, so nobody arrives with nothing. If the room agrees too fast, assign the sides: two groups argue for, two against, regardless of what they wrote.
«Debris» is pronounced /ˈdebrɪs/ for the rest of the unit.Cross out the final s on the board the first time it appears and again in the wrap-up drill. Two exposures in one lesson fixes it for most of the group.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 10–18 (reading — headings and detail) and tasks 32–36 (lexical choice, where disaster and weather collocations live).

The environment and natural disasters are a standing topic of the Russian paper: they turn up in the reading section, in the oral card and, since the topic is close to ecology, in the project essay of task 38. What a candidate lacks is not ideas but the noun: drought, flood, debris, casualty, relief worker. And the reading task done today — four multiple-choice questions where the correct option paraphrases the text rather than repeating it — is the exact mechanism of tasks 12–18.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
