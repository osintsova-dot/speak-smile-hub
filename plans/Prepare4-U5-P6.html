🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use look/taste/smell + ADJECTIVE correctly, follow seven short conversations about food (exam-shaped, run as ordinary listening), and order food politely with I'll have… / I'd like… / Could I have…?
What is genuinely hard
Two interferences meet: Russian «вкусно пахнет» puts an adverb after smell, so «it smells sweetly» writes itself; and the Russian direct «Дайте пиццу» maps onto «Give me pizza», which in an English café is a small act of war. Politeness here is grammar, not decoration.
Typical error to expect
It tastes well / it smells sweetly — an adverb after look/taste/smell where English wants an adjective; Russian «вкусно пахнет» is an adverb, so the interference is systematic [A2/B1]
A one-word reply («Sweet.», «Yes.») does not count as an answer at all: an answer needs a verb in it [national exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
⚠️ SB p.61 is missing from the scanned PDF — the students' printed books have it; every key below is rebuilt from TB p.118 and complete. The sense verbs open the lesson because the listening and the café both run on them. The seven extracts get the TB's own three-pass procedure — check one, replay with pauses, straight through — because that is how listening is taught rather than tested. The café order is built as model → round 1 → clinic → round 2 with an audience, the block's proven two-round shape: improvement inside one lesson is audible and cheap. This is the last teaching lesson of the half-year — everything after it is review, test and the half-year paper — so the wall charts stay up for next lesson's stations.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Tonight you could walk into a café in London and get exactly what you want — politely, in English, with extra cheese. Today: three little verbs that need adjectives, seven tiny listening puzzles, and the three magic phrases every café in the world understands.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts. The rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a tense-gate hot round, and the bridge into the sense verbs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — hot round: the tense gate. Kagan RallyRobin in pairs: A says a time phrase (yesterday · since Monday · never · two weeks ago · for years), B fires back a true sentence in the right tense. Three each, swap.
  3. One choral How-long question for the road: How long have you been at this school?
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the lesson: Page sixty, bottom half — and then the café opens. Today you learn to get exactly what you want. Politely.
📺 On screen: time phrases by voice — the voice is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: fire tense-correct sentences at speed. Expect the gate rule to hold for most — one point-at-the-box for any dated perfect

Vocabulary · look, taste, smell
pairs → class
Why: One rule carries the stage: an adjective follows, never an adverb. The Russian «вкусно пахнет» interference is named out loud and drilled against.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 the rule + ex.2 controlled practice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 — three examples from Ollie's unit: what type of word follows? ADJECTIVES. Rule on the board: After look, taste and smell comes an ADJECTIVE, not an adverb: it looks horrible · they taste bitter · it smells disgusting. Russian «вкусно пахнет» is the interference to watch.
  2. Concept checks from the plan header — the soup and the sweetly one, cold-called.
  3. ex.2 solo, 4 minutes, pair check. Key: 1 smells/looks delicious · 2 tastes bitter · 3 look/smell clean · 4 look exhausted · 5 looks upset · 6 tastes disgusting (example: look new). Box ex.2's trap: the box word new is used in the example — cross it out first.
  4. Kagan Showdown, four calls: you mime (sniff the milk, taste the lemon, look exhausted), teams write verb + adjective and show on three.
📺 On screen: calls and mimes by voice, answers on mini-whiteboards — voice and boards are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: extract the rule, complete, commit on boards. Expect item 4 (look exhausted — about people, not food) to widen the rule usefully. Typical error: smells deliciously

Why: The book's p.122 riddles: describing food without naming it forces sense-verb + adjective sentences at speed — retrieval disguised as a game.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 → p.122: describe a food without naming it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Model first: They taste delicious and juicy. They're small and red. — Strawberries!
  2. Kagan RallyCoach: A gives two sense-verb clues, B guesses in a full sentence (They must be… no — Are they strawberries?); three riddles each, swap.
  3. Best riddle from each pair goes to the class; the class answers chorally.
  4. Every clue must carry look, taste or smell + adjective — the partner polices it.
📺 On screen: SB p.122 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: riddle, guess, police the pattern. Expect giggles at smell clues — free consolidation. Typical error: clues with is instead of the sense verb — gently bounce them back

28–44′
Listening · seven conversations
solo → pairs
Why: Three passes, exam shape, ordinary run: predict, listen, justify why the wrong pictures are wrong. The justification pass is where listening is actually taught.

WHAT WE DO: track 48, three passes, exam shape run as ordinary listening

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the scene: Seven people, seven little food moments. One question each, three pictures. You hear everything twice. Pre-teach: available, ingredients.
  2. Pairs predict for 2 minutes: what words will each picture need? Collect a few onto the board, no feedback.
  3. Run the TB procedure: TB procedure: play extract 1, check together; replay all with pauses; then straight through without pauses; for two or three items discuss WHY the two other pictures are wrong.
  4. Key: track 48 — 1 C · 2 A · 3 A · 4 B · 5 C · 6 C · 7 A.
  5. For items that split the class, the deciding line is replayed and QUOTED: Which words chose picture C?
  6. Fast finishers during replays: write why one wrong picture is wrong.
📺 On screen: track 48 in the whiteboard app, played with the TB's pause pattern (Backup: read the seven extracts from the audioscript, TB p.269, in two voices at natural speed, twice — the task works identically by ear)
👦 Students do: predict, listen twice, justify the choices. Expect 5–6 of 7 on the second pass. Typical problem: choosing the picture that is MENTIONED rather than the one that ANSWERS — name the trap, it is the exam's favourite

44–54′
Speaking model · the café order
class → pairs
Why: A recorded model shows the bar before students have to reach it: prices read aloud, Emma's order sorted line by line, the three polite frames drilled with melody.

WHAT WE DO: the menu, the prices, Emma's order (track 49)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Menu first, ex.1: three questions. Key: menu questions: 1 vegetarian · 2 £9 · 3 two different sizes.
  2. Price drill, choral, five prices off the menu: three pounds fifty · nine pounds · fifty p.
  3. Play track 49 — ex.2: What does Emma order? How much does she pay? Key: track 49 — Emma orders a veggie pizza, a green salad and a small cola; she pays £12.50.
  4. ex.3 — sort the lines: server or Emma? Key: who says what: Server — What can I get you? · And to drink? · Eat in or take out? · Here's your change. Emma — Could I have…, please? · I'll have…, please. · I'd like…, please. · Here you are..
  5. Drill the three customer frames with the polite fall on please; then the server script chorally: What can I get you? … And to drink? … Eat in or take out?
📺 On screen: track 49 in the whiteboard app (Backup: read Emma and the server from the audioscript, TB p.269, in two voices — change position for the server; the sorting works identically)
👦 Students do: read the menu, drill prices, sort the lines by speaker. Expect «Here you are / Here's your change» to need one clarifying gesture each. Typical error: flat please — replay the melody

🎯 Role-play · round 1
pairs
Why: The productive outcome: a real two-way café order with the frames compulsory. Round 1 is the diagnosis — you log errors and frame-dodging for the clinic.

WHAT WE DO: server and customer, menu in hand, frames compulsory

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Roles: A is the server (script on the board), B the customer (three frames + please). The menu is SB p.61 in B's hands.
  2. Kagan Timed Pair Share shape: 2 minutes per order — order, drink, eat in or take out, pay, change. Then swap roles.
  3. The customer must order a MAIN + a drink + one extra; the server must ask all three script questions.
  4. You walk and log SILENTLY: bare orders, adverb slips, price stumbles.
  5. Freeze at minute twelve: Kitchen closes for two minutes — clinic time.
📺 On screen: the server script and customer frames stay in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: order and serve both ways with the frames. Expect stiffness in round 1 — normal; the clinic and round 2 exist because of it. Typical error: Give me pizza — logged with a smile

70–76′
Order clinic
class
Why: Delayed correction between the rounds: the flat «Give me pizza» gets repaired while the conversation is warm and there is still a round 2 to use it in.

WHAT WE DO: delayed correction between the rounds

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three lines from your round-1 log on the board, anonymous: a bare «Pizza.», a «tastes sweetly», a price stumble.
  2. Kagan RallyRobin in pairs: fix all three, hands up, repair together fast.
  3. One melody replay: the class orders from YOU chorally, politely; you serve with a straight face.
  4. Announce the round-2 delta: New partner, new order — and the servers may invent ONE problem: no cola today. Solve it politely.
📺 On screen: three faulty lines in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: fix, drill the melody, reset. Expect the invented problem to delight the servers — it forces real negotiation in round 2

🎯 Role-play · round 2 · the café opens
new pairs
Why: Same task, new partner, an audience: the improvement is audible within one lesson, which is the cheapest motivation there is.

WHAT WE DO: same task, new partner, one twist, an audience

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Mix-Pair-Share: stand, mix, new partner, new roles.
  2. Round 2 — 2 minutes each way; servers spring their one problem; customers solve it with the frames (Then I'll have sparkling water instead, please.)
  3. Two pairs perform their best order to the class; the class plays other customers and reacts only with menu language.
  4. Praise the delta by name: Round one you said «Pizza». Round two you said «I'll have the veggie pizza, please — and could I have extra cheese?» That's the lesson, done.
📺 On screen: the board cards stay up; performing pairs stand where everyone sees them — the room is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: re-run the order better, handle the twist, perform. Expect audibly longer, smoother orders — say so out loud. Typical leftover: please-dropping under speed; one reminder, not a correction

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«The soup tastes strange» — strange describes the soup or the tasting? → the soup: that is why it is an adjective.
«It smells sweetly» — fix it. → sweet: after smell comes an adjective. Russian puts an adverb there; English refuses.
«Give me pizza» in a café — will you get pizza? → probably, with a frown. What earns the smile? → I'll have a pizza, please.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
