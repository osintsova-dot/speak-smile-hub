🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Build adjectives from nouns with -al and -ful (nature → natural, history → historical, colour → colourful) with the spelling changes under control, and write a ~150-word biography that moves through a life with the five time expressions.
What is genuinely hard
Word building is a spelling discipline: the noun usually changes shape before the suffix lands, and no rule tells you which suffix a word takes — success takes -ful, nature takes -al, and both must be learned. In the writing, the real risk is a list of dates with no story in it.
Typical error to expect
«naturel», «beautyful», «historicial» — the noun usually changes shape before the suffix lands (nature → natural, history → historical) and -ful keeps ONE l.
Expect also He was born in 1989 year — the year is already in the number, and the model text on p.101 never does it.
Why the lesson is built this way
Second and last lesson of Unit 17, so the spread has to close cleanly: vocabulary first and fast (rules extracted, then the book's own information-gap pairwork, so every adjective is said aloud), then the whole second half is writing. The Taylor Swift model is mined for craft before anything is written; the five time expressions are drilled on a stranger's life; the plan is four questions in note form; and the biography is started AND finished in class, which is the only way the checklist ever gets used. Peer review runs on the same checklist. Homework is the workbook's parallel task — a second biography, written alone, of a person they choose.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“A farm that grew Christmas trees. A 200-kilometre drive for singing lessons. An album at sixteen. That's a life in three facts — and today you write somebody's, in a hundred and fifty words, so that somebody else wants to read it.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a four-error clinic in pairs, and the bridge into word building

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — delayed feedback. Four anonymous sentences from lesson 1's log on the board (a said me, a to not, a missing object, an ask/tell swap). Kagan RallyRobin: pairs fix all four, the fastest pair performs the corrections.
  3. Praise precision, then move: All four fixed in ninety seconds. Now put the grammar down — today you build words and then you build a life.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): name the destination: Page one hundred. Two endings — al and ful — and then page one hundred and one, where you write somebody's life in a hundred and fifty words.
📺 On screen: four sentences in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: fix yesterday's errors without knowing whose they were. Expect the said me item to be caught first — that is the one that landed

WHAT WE DO: name the aim, put up the biography checklist

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The checklist goes up now and stays up until the bell: 1 When and where born · 2 Two or three dates that MEANT something · 3 One unusual fact · 4 Four time expressions · 5 One -al or -ful adjective · 6 About 150 words.
  2. Explain the shape in 40 seconds: twenty minutes on adjectives, then the rest of the lesson is a biography — read one, plan one, write one, edit one.
  3. Set the bar: A date without a meaning is not a biography. It's a calendar.

8–20′
Vocabulary · nouns become adjectives
pairs → class
Why: Word building taught as a rule, not as a list: the class extracts where the adjectives come from and what happens to the spelling on the way (nature → natural, history → historical) before practising it.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: extract the rule and the spelling changes; ex.2: eight adjectives built

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 in pairs, two minutes: three example adjectives, two questions. Key: a they are all made from NOUNS · b nature → natural (the e disappears), history → historical (y becomes ic). Two more rules worth saying: care → careful (the e survives before -ful) and mathematics → mathematical (the s goes).
  2. The three spelling changes go on the board in a box and stay there: nature → natural (the e goes) · history → historical (y becomes ic) · care → careful (the e stays).
  3. ex.2 solo, four minutes, then Kagan RallyCoach in pairs, one pen. Key: 1 colourful · 2 peaceful · 3 original · 4 painful · 5 cheerful · 6 political · 7 stressful · 8 central.
  4. Board check with spelling out loud, letter by letter, for the three dangerous ones: peaceful. P-E-A-C-E-F-U-L. One L.
  5. The eleven adjectives go up on the wall in a third colour — they are test items in two weeks.
📺 On screen: SB p.100 in every book plus the spelling box in chalk — paper and board are the media, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: extract, build, coach, spell aloud. Expect political (from politics, with the s gone) to be the one nobody gets first time — it is worth thirty extra seconds

Word-building pairwork
pairs
Why: The book's own information-gap: each partner holds half the sentences, so the twelve adjectives have to be SAID and heard, not silently ticked.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: the information gap on p.125 and p.127

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Split the room: Student A works from p.125, Student B from p.127. Nobody looks at the other page — that is the whole task.
  2. Six sentences each way, read aloud and completed by ear: Say the whole sentence, not the word. Key: pairwork p.125 / p.127 — Student A: 1 digital · 2 musical · 3 personal · 4 professional · 5 useful · 6 hopeful · Student B: 1 traditional · 2 musical · 3 environmental · 4 cultural · 5 helpful · 6 stressful.
  3. Mini spelling showdown to close, mini-whiteboards, five words called by you: musical · environmental · successful · natural · historical. Boards up on three.
  4. Fast finishers: three more nouns to convert — beauty, culture, hope.

👦 Students do: read, listen, complete by ear, then spell under pressure. Expect musical to appear in BOTH halves — a free moment to point out that some nouns take the ending unchanged

28–30′
Move break
standing
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: 90 seconds: the -al wall and the -ful wall

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. That wall is -AL. That wall is -FUL. I say a noun, you walk. Colour! Nature! Pain! History! Success! Peace! Centre! Two seconds per word, no thinking time.
  2. Last one: stress. Peaceful — walk right. And sit down where you are. Now: a life in a hundred and fifty words.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: vote with their feet on every noun. Everyone answers every item, and you SEE the two or three who follow the crowd

30–42′
Model biography · Taylor Swift
solo → groups
Why: Model-first writing: the class mines the model for what makes a biography readable — dates that matter, one unusual fact, and the time expressions that move a life along — before anyone writes a word.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: read the model, then mine it for craft

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open at p.101, one minute of silent reading, one question on the board: Which fact surprised you most? Three answers, no more.
  2. ex.2 in groups of three, three minutes: What makes this interesting to read? Find three things this writer does that a boring biography wouldn't. Harvest onto the board.
  3. Then compare their list with the Prepare to write box — expect a large overlap, and say so: You just wrote the book's own advice yourselves.
  4. ex.3 with books closed: you call the years, the class calls the events. Key: 1989 born in Pennsylvania · 2004 the family moved to Nashville · 2006 the first album Taylor Swift · 2008 Fearless · 2017 Reputation, and the video with 40 million views in 24 hours.
  5. One line of craft named explicitly: The Christmas tree farm and the 200-kilometre drive. Facts nobody else would know. Your biography needs one of those.
📺 On screen: SB p.101 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: read, analyse, recall by date. Expect the charity paragraph to be skipped in the recall round — point out that it is the Nowadays paragraph, and every biography needs one
SB p.101 ex.1–3 · board
42–50′
Prepare to write · the time expressions
solo → pairs
Why: The five phrases (As a child… · At the age of… · As soon as… · By the time he was… · Nowadays…) are the difference between a list of dates and a story. They are drilled on a stranger's life before students use them on their own.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4: five gapped sentences from somebody else's life

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The five expressions go on the board as a strip: As a child… · At the age of… · As soon as… · By the time he/she was… · Nowadays,…
  2. ex.4 solo, three minutes, then pairs compare. Key: 1 at (at the age of 14) · 2 as, as (as soon as) · 3 as (as a young child) · 4 Nowadays · 5 By, time (by the time — it often travels with already).
  3. One concept check on item 5, because it decides the whole sentence: By the time he started college he was already in two bands. Bands first or college first? Bands. By the time means BEFORE.
  4. Fast oral drill, your turn to be interrogated: students throw you a time expression, you produce a true sentence about your own life. Then two of them do the same.
📺 On screen: the five-phrase strip in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: complete, compare, hear the phrases in real sentences about a real person. Expect as soon as to be confused with as long as — one example kills it

Plan · my person
solo
Why: Planning is where the marks live: four questions answered in note form, one unusual fact chosen on purpose. A planned biography writes itself; an unplanned one becomes a list of years.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5: choose the person, four questions in note form

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The choice rule, said once and enforced: Somebody whose life you can tell in four facts WITHOUT looking at your phone. A famous person, a relative, a coach, a neighbour.
  2. Four questions on the board, notes only, five minutes: 1 When and where born? 2 Which dates matter? 3 Main achievements? 4 One unusual fact?
  3. You circulate and ask one question per student — usually And why does that date matter? That question is the whole lesson.
  4. Anyone stuck at minute 54 gets the fallback: their own grandmother. It always works.
📺 On screen: the four planning questions in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: choose, note, answer your one question. Expect two or three footballers with no facts attached — redirect them fast

WHAT WE DO: 90 seconds of walking and stealing

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up, notes in hand. Find someone. Tell them who you're writing about in ONE sentence. Steal one idea from them. Go. Two partners, forty seconds each.
  2. Sit down. Pens up. Eighteen minutes. The checklist is on the board — use it while you write, not after.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: walk, pitch their person in one sentence, come back with an idea. The movement also resets attention for a long writing stage

🎯 Writing out · the biography
solo, silent
Why: The productive outcome in writing: a real ~150-word biography built on a model, a plan and a checklist — started AND finished in class, which is the only way the checklist ever gets used.

WHAT WE DO: the ~150-word biography, written from the plan against the checklist

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Eighteen minutes of real silence. Clock visible; you announce minute 10 and minute 15 only.
  2. The checklist stays on the board; the wall of -al and -ful adjectives stays up. Say once: At least one adjective from that wall.
  3. You circulate silently, marking nothing — a finger on a missing time expression is enough. Answer only spelling questions, and only in writing on the corner of their page.
  4. Stronger students get one extra instruction quietly: Put in one reported command — «Her mum told her not to give up.»
  5. Pens down at 76 means pens down. Unfinished is fine — the workbook version at home is the second chance.
📺 On screen: the checklist and word wall in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write for eighteen minutes with their plan beside them. Expect the first five minutes to be slow and the last five to fly — the announcement at minute 10 is what causes that
notebooks · board checklist · clock
76–86′
Peer review + polish
pairs
Why: A checklist swap turns every student into an editor: missing time expressions and missing facts are far easier to spot in someone else's text — and both sides learn from the catch.

WHAT WE DO: the checklist swap, then two minutes of repair

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap notebooks. Kagan RallyCoach with a rule: the reader marks nothing — the reader TELLS.
  2. Three questions to answer about the partner's text, four minutes: 1 How many time expressions did you find? 2 Which date has no meaning attached? 3 What's the one unusual fact — say it back.
  3. Books back, three minutes of repair: every student fixes at least one thing their reader named.
  4. Two volunteers read their opening two sentences aloud; the class listens for the time expression only. Hands up when you hear it.
  5. Collect the drafts — they come back with the test papers in lesson 6.
📺 On screen: notebooks and the checklist in chalk — paper and board are the media, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: read, question, repair. Expect question 2 (a date with no meaning) to find something in almost every text — that is why it is question 2

Structure
As a child, she sang on her family's farm. · At the age of nine, she travelled to New York. · As soon as she learnt the guitar, people noticed her talent. · By the time she was 16, her first album was already popular. · Nowadays, she uses her success to help others.
Form
noun + -al / -ful, with spelling changes (nature → natural, history → historical, mathematics → mathematical); -ful always keeps ONE l; the time expressions take past simple in a biography.
Pron. watch
the suffixes are unstressed and weak: /ˈnætʃrəl/, /ˈpiːsfl/, /səkˈsesfl/ — never «-fool» · the stress does not move in colour → colourful, but it DOES in history → hisTORical
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«nature» → «naturel» or «natural»? → natural. The e disappears and the ending is -al. Write it three times, not once.
«care» → careful or carful? → careful. Before -ful the e survives; before -al it usually does not.
«By the time she was sixteen…» — does the album come before or after sixteen? → before. That is why already fits so often here.

Assumptions: SB p.100 (right) + p.101, syllabus lesson 55. Successful, colourful and natural are recognisable by sight; producing them from the noun is the new skill. Past simple narrative is secure from Prepare 3, so the writing half can be about craft and time expressions rather than tense.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students write a list of years instead of a lifeThe checklist forbids it: every date has to be followed by WHAT IT MEANT. The model does this in every paragraph — point at the Nashville sentence and ask why the family moved.
Word building becomes guessing the suffixHonest answer, given once: there is no rule, so the eleven adjectives get a colour block on the wall and a spelling round at the end. The rules that DO exist (nature, history, care) get said aloud every time they appear.
The writing stage runs out of timeHard timing: notes at minute 50, pens down at minute 76 — 18 minutes of actual writing, announced with the clock visible. Anyone still planning at minute 58 gets your four questions answered orally and starts writing immediately.
A student picks a person they know nothing aboutThe choice is made at minute 50 with a rule: someone whose life you can tell in four facts WITHOUT a phone. A friend, a grandmother or a coach beats a half-remembered celebrity every time.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
