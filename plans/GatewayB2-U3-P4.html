🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Compare and contrast two photographs for about a minute, using a fixed set of expressions and speculating about what is not shown; and write an opinion essay of 140–190 words in four paragraphs with a stated opinion, ordered arguments and a contrasting view.
What is genuinely hard
In the speaking task, students describe each photo separately and in detail, which is the wrong task and runs out of time; the examiner wants comparison and speculation. In the writing, the difficulty is register and structure: an opinion essay is formal (no contractions), needs one argument per paragraph, and must not turn into a list of everything the student knows about traffic.
Typical error to expect
«On the first photo» — English says in the first photo. A one-word preposition error, repeated eight times in a two-minute answer.
Describing photo A for fifty seconds and photo B for ten — the task is comparison, and a monologue about one picture cannot score the comparison marks. In the essay: contractions («don't», «it's») in a formal text.
Why the lesson is built this way
The speaking half is built on one model and a fixed phrase bank, and the model is deliberately imperfect — the students are asked whether she described each photo in detail (she did not, and that is why she finished the task). Then they do it twice, once as speaker and once as silent examiner, with the roles swapped, so everybody speaks for a full minute and everybody has to listen without helping. The writing half starts from the students' own opinion about the school run — a topic every one of them has an opinion about — and only then meets the model essay and the linkers. The plan is written and checked in class; the essay is finished at home.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two photographs, one minute, no preparation. And you may not describe them one after the other — you have to compare them. Then a question you already have an opinion about: should parents drive their children to school?”

8–16′
Similarities and differences
desks
Why: Generating the content before the language means the phrase bank has something to carry when it arrives.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.40 ex.1a–1b: the pilot and the unicyclist

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Warmer, sixty seconds in pairs, books closed: as many nouns and adjectives as you can for describing a person's appearance.
  2. Ex.1a: two columns in the notebook — similarities and differences between the two photos. Three minutes in pairs.
  3. Board a mind map for support: people (age, number, clothes) · place · background and weather · actions.
  4. Take answers: both are in or on a vehicle, both are holding something, both wear protective clothing; she is in a plane, he is on a unicycle; she wears headphones, he wears a helmet.
  5. Ex.1b as a whole class: They look secure. Why? — and every answer must contain «because».
  6. Thirty seconds of culture: in January 2019 Ellie Carter became the youngest person in Britain to fly a plane solo, three days after her sixteenth birthday.
📺 On screen: the mind map on the board. Backup: dictate the four branches.
👦 Students do: build two lists in pairs and give reasons for a speculation

16–26′
The model answer · and what she does NOT do
desks
Why: A model that is evaluated rather than admired teaches strategy; the seven expressions are then taken from something the students have already judged as effective.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.40 ex.2a–2b, ex.3 (track 14): the model judged, then mined for phrases

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the three questions before you play: does she mention any of YOUR ideas? does she describe each photo in great detail? does she do both parts of the task?
  2. Play track 14. Answers: some of them; no, she does not describe each photo in detail; yes, she does both parts.
  3. Land the point: She did not describe the photos in detail — and that is why she finished. Detail is what makes people run out of time.
  4. Ex.3, second play: complete the seven Speaking bank expressions. Key: 1 Both · 2 both · 3 similarity · 4 difference · 5 difference · 6 On · 7 On.
  5. Elicit the meaning of «whereas» — and drill the auxiliary-only contrast: The boy is wearing a helmet whereas the girl isn't.
  6. Ex.2b in pairs, ninety seconds: would you like to try either of these activities?
📺 On screen: track 14, played twice. Backup: read the model answer from the Teacher's Book script, p.126, at natural speed.
👦 Students do: judge a model against three criteria, then complete seven expressions from it

The Speaking bank · comparing and contrasting
desks
Why: Immediate use on content they generated ten minutes earlier is what turns a phrase list into speech.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.40 ex.4: the phrases used immediately on the same two photos

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the bank in two halves — comparing, then contrasting — and drill the stress: similarity · difference · whereas · between.
  2. Ex.4 in pairs, three minutes: use the phrases to say the similarities and differences you wrote in ex.1. Every sentence starts with a phrase from the bank.
  3. Monitor for «on the first photo» and correct it on the board, unnamed: IN the first photo.
  4. Sixty-second drill: you say «compare» or «contrast», they produce a phrase. Eight rounds.

WHAT WE DO: three minutes standing, comparing at speed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand each student a card with one photo description on it (a crowded bus · an empty motorway · a bicycle in the rain · a plane taking off · a queue at an airport gate · a family in a car …).
  2. Find someone whose card is as DIFFERENT from yours as possible. Then say one sentence with «whereas».
  3. Two rounds, two different partners.

36–46′
🎯 One minute each, twice
desks in pairs
Why: Both roles matter: speaking for a full minute is the performance, and being a silent examiner is the assessment training. Everybody does both.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.40 ex.5a–5c (Student B's photos on p.149): the real task, both roles

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set it up exactly: Student A looks at the photos on page forty. Student B looks at page one hundred and forty-nine. You do NOT show each other.
  2. Ex.5a, two minutes of silent preparation: two or three similarities and two or three differences, notes only.
  3. Ex.5b: Student A speaks for one minute. Student B is the examiner and says nothing at all. Time it.
  4. Debrief in one question: Examiners — did they compare, or did they describe?
  5. Ex.5c: swap roles and repeat with the other set of photos.
  6. Record one answer on a phone (with permission) and play back twenty seconds. Ask the class what worked.
📺 On screen: a visible timer for each one-minute turn, and a phone for the optional recording. Backup: if nobody consents to being recorded, skip the playback — the two rounds are the lesson.
👦 Students do: prepare in silence, speak alone for one minute, then act as a silent examiner

The school run · your opinion first
desks
Why: An opinion formed before the model is read is the student's own; an opinion formed afterwards is the model's.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.41 ex.1–2: the topic before the model

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board the collocation «the school run» and explain it (the daily driving of children to and from school). Then ask for other travel collocations from the unit: carbon emissions, departure lounge, seat belt, traffic jam, lost property, ticket inspector.
  2. Ex.1: read the Culture exchange text on the UK school run. Two questions to the room: what do you think of it, and does the same thing happen here?
  3. Ex.2 in pairs, four minutes: Do you think it is OK for parents to drive their kids to school every day? Decide your opinion FIRST, then find at least three reasons. Notes only.
  4. Take two opposing opinions from the floor and leave them unresolved — the essay resolves them.
📺 On screen: the collocations on the board. Backup: not applicable.
👦 Students do: read a short culture text, then form an opinion and list three reasons

54–62′
The model essay · structure and register
desks
Why: Analysing a model by function rather than by content is what makes it reusable for a different question.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.41 ex.3–4: five paragraphs, four functions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ex.3: read the essay. Is the writer's opinion similar to yours? Ignore the gaps. Two minutes.
  2. Ex.4 in pairs: how many paragraphs, what does each one do, and what is the writer's own idea? Key: five paragraphs — 1 introduce and give the opinion · 2 traffic jams and pollution · 3 danger versus independence · 4 a healthier lifestyle (the writer's own idea) · 5 a short conclusion.
  3. Board the register rule with two examples: don't → do not · it's → it is. An opinion essay is formal. No contractions.
  4. One question that always sharpens the class: Which paragraph would you delete if you were twenty words over?
📺 On screen: the paragraph map on the board. Backup: dictate it.
👦 Students do: read the model, name the function of each paragraph, learn the register rule
SB p.41 · board
62–70′
Linkers by function
desks
Why: Linkers are retrievable only by function, and the drill is what puts them in the mouth before the writing starts.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.41 ex.5–6: eight expressions into four groups

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ex.5 alone, four minutes: complete the essay with the eight expressions. Support: give two options per gap, as the Teacher's Book suggests. Key: a As far as I'm concerned · b To begin with · c Furthermore · d However · e I agree up to a point · f On the other hand · g Lastly · h To sum up.
  2. Ex.6: sort them into the four Writing bank groups — expressing opinions · adding and ordering · contrasting · concluding.
  3. Sixty-second drill: you name a function, they produce an expression. Ten rounds, whole class.
  4. Read the Exam tip and reduce it to three lines on the board: one argument per paragraph · formal style, no contractions · the conclusion adds nothing new.
📺 On screen: the four functional groups on the board with two examples each. Backup: the bank is printed on p.41.
👦 Students do: complete the essay with eight linkers, sort them by function, produce them on demand

🎯 Plan and write the first two paragraphs
desks
Why: Writing begun in class can still be influenced, and the two underlined linkers make «use linkers» a checkable instruction rather than advice.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.41 ex.7a: their own essay, begun where you can see it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The task, boarded: What can we do to cut down the pollution caused by transport and travel? Notes for 1 cars · 2 long-distance travel · 3 your own idea.
  2. Four minutes of planning in note form: four paragraphs — introduction and opinion · argument one · argument two · conclusion.
  3. RallyCoach, two minutes: swap plans and check ONE thing — is there exactly one argument per paragraph?
  4. Eight minutes of silent writing: the introduction and the first argument. Silent mode. No questions during the timer.
  5. The visible requirements on the board: two linkers, underlined · no contractions.
  6. Walk the room and pencil-dot every contraction you see. No commentary.
  7. Announce the finish: The rest is homework — Workbook page twenty-nine, exercise seven, 140 to 190 words.
📺 On screen: a visible timer and the two requirements on the board. Backup: a phone stopwatch on the desk.
👦 Students do: plan four paragraphs, check a partner's plan, write two paragraphs in silence

Structure

Form
comparing: both / in both / one similarity / another difference · contrasting: whereas, but, on the one hand … on the other hand · speculating: they're probably …, it looks like …, I think … because … · the auxiliary-only contrast: The boy is wearing a helmet whereas the girl isn't.
Pron. watch
word stress in the long unit words: photograph vs photographic · similarity · difference · between · whereas · the contracted future perfect is three sounds, not five: I'll have /aɪləv/ · I'll have been /aɪləv bɪn/
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«On the first photo» — correct? → no: IN the first photo.
Do you have to describe every detail of each photo? → no. The task is comparison — details are what makes you run out of time.
In an opinion essay, do you have to give both sides? → no, but you should acknowledge the other side and then answer it.
ICQ before writing: how many arguments per paragraph? → one. How many words? → 140 to 190.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The student describes photo A, then photo BBan the word «and then» for the stage. The first sentence must contain «both», and the second must contain «whereas» or «but». Board those two openings and require them.
Silence after thirty secondsGive the five content questions from ex.1 as a visible checklist: how many people · what they are doing · where · what is in the background · how they feel. Nobody runs out of things to say with five questions on the board.
The examiner partner starts helpingSay it once and enforce it: The examiner says nothing. Not one word. It is uncomfortable, and it is exactly what the exam is like.
Contractions in the essayBoard the rule with an example before writing: don't → do not · it's → it is. Then it is a checkable item in the peer check rather than a correction afterwards.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 38 (the opinion essay) and tasks 39–40 (the oral part: describing and comparing).

Both halves of this lesson map onto the Russian oral and written papers almost directly. Comparing two photographs is a task the Russian exam has had in one form or another for years, and the language is the same: both photos show …, whereas …, on the one hand … . The opinion essay is task 38's smaller sibling — four paragraphs instead of five, 140–190 words instead of 200–250, but the same requirement: a stated opinion, arguments in order, a contrasting view acknowledged, a conclusion that adds nothing new.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
