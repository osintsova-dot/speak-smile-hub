🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Describe a person's age, looks, hair and character with the Unit 1 adjectives and choose between present simple and present continuous — including the verbs that refuse the continuous — while talking about yourself.
What is genuinely hard
The tenses themselves are old friends after Prepare 3. The genuinely hard part is (1) the stative verbs — I'm not understanding feels right to a Russian ear — and (2) sorting sixteen describing words into age / looks / hair instead of one undifferentiated pile.
Typical error to expect
I'm not understanding / I am liking pizza — stative verbs pushed into the continuous [A2/B1]
My brother play football every day instead of plays — the dropped ‑s in the third person singular [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
Vocabulary comes before reading because the profiles are made of exactly these adjectives. The reading then does triple duty: gist, an exam-style open cloze done as ordinary careful reading, and the raw material the grammar is mined from — so the rule is discovered, not announced. The stative verbs get their own stage because they are the one new corner: everything else is consolidation. The productive stage sits at 72′, after two rounds of controlled practice, because fluency needs the forms to be at least warm. During that stage you say nothing about errors: you log them and open the next lesson with them.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Sixteen words and one trap. By the end of today you can describe anybody in this room — age, hair, character — and talk about yourself without falling into the trap every Russian speaker falls into: I'm not understanding. You'll know why that's wrong — and what to say instead.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a team adjective brainstorm around a stick person, and the bridge into the unit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — warm-up brainstorm (TB warmer). A stick person on the board. Thirty seconds in your team: adjectives for appearance and character. One point per word — spelled correctly!
  3. Collect words around the stick person; keep the list — it shows you what today's vocabulary can build on.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the unit: Books open, page ten. This unit is about you — what you look like, what you are like, and what you are doing right now.
📺 On screen: a stick person and the class's adjectives in chalk — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: shout out and spell adjectives in teams. Expect 8–12 words, mostly character (kind, funny, lazy); age words almost never come up — that is today's gap

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

WHAT WE DO: ex.2: sixteen words into the three columns, then the ex.3 guessing game

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pairs sort the ex.2 box into Age / Looks / Hair — 4 minutes, books open.
  2. Check against the columns on the board. Key: Age: elderly, middle-aged, teenage, in his/her (early/late) twenties/thirties · Looks: attractive, good-looking, handsome, pretty · Hair: bald, blonde, curly, dark, fair, straight.
  3. Drill the four pronunciation traps by ear: bald /bɔːld/ · curly /ˈkɜːli/ · straight /streɪt/ · thirties /ˈθɜːtiz/.
  4. ex.3 guessing game in pairs, model first: A: He's tall and middle-aged. He's got dark hair. — B: Is it Grace's dad?
  5. Kagan RallyCoach: partners take turns — one describes a person in the photos, the other guesses, then swap. Three people each.
📺 On screen: the ex.2 word box on the screen while pairs sort (Backup: the sixteen words are in the book — sort straight from the page and fill the chalk columns by hand)
👦 Students do: sort, drill, then describe-and-guess. Expect Age to be the hardest column (middle-aged, in his twenties are new). Typical error: He has bald hair — unpack it: bald = NO hair

WHAT WE DO: the three profiles on p.11: gist match, the one-word-per-gap cloze, detail

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the gist task BEFORE they read: ex.2 — Match each person to ONE interest from the box. One interest is extra. Ninety seconds, read fast. Key: Lucas – technology · Grace – fitness · Alfie – going to the cinema (fashion is the extra option). The text is recorded as track 03.
  2. ex.3 — Alfie's profile has six gaps, ONE word per gap. Frame it as careful reading, not as an exam: Small words carry the sentence. Read around the gap. Key: 1 can (modal) · 2 of (preposition) · 3 them (pronoun) · 4 in (preposition) · 5 one (pronoun) · 6 to (part of the infinitive).
  3. ex.4 in pairs — write the correct name. Key: 1 Alfie · 2 Grace · 3 Lucas · 4 Grace · 5 Alfie · 6 Lucas. The evidence rule: No line from the text — no point. Finger on the line. Kagan RallyCoach to check.
  4. Fast finishers: ex.5 — the highlighted phrases. Key: 1 take part · 2 make progress · 3 only child · 4 code · 5 be into (sth) · 6 patient.
📺 On screen: the profiles on the screen so a line can be pointed out to everybody at once (Backup: everyone works from the book and you keep a running «who found the line» score on the board)
👦 Students do: read against the clock, fill the gaps, check in pairs with the evidence line. Expect gaps 1 (can) and 6 (to) to be closed fast, gap 5 (one) to cause the argument. Typical error: a content word forced into a gap that needs a grammar word

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 p.12: match the profile sentences to the five rules, then sort the time markers

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books-closed dictation first («My name is… I'm teaching right now. On Saturday morning I usually play tennis, but this Saturday we're playing in the afternoon.» Students name the tenses and say which sentence is about now, which is a habit and which is a future plan.)
  2. Pairs open p.12 and do ex.1: match the five example sentences from the profiles to rules a–e. Key: 1 c · 2 e · 3 b · 4 d (or c) · 5 a.
  3. ex.2 — complete the six sentences. Key: 1 get (b) · 2 is working (d) · 3 isn't concentrating (c) · 4 Are, doing (e) · 5 plays (a) · 6 goes (b). Kagan Showdown: teams write the answer on a mini-whiteboard and reveal at once.
  4. ex.3 — sort the time markers into the two chalk columns from the start of the lesson: never / sometimes / every day / on Saturdays → simple; at the moment / this week / tonight / next weekend → continuous.
📺 On screen: the five rules on the screen, matched live (Backup: the same five rules written on the board before the lesson, students come out and draw the arrows)
👦 Students do: derive the rules from sentences they have already read. Expect rules a–c to be matched fast, d (temporary) and e (future plans) to need talk. Typical error: Tonight she's going resisted — «but it's future!» — that is rule e, settle it with the marker columns
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare4_Unit1_Grammar_Present_Simple_vs_Continuous_A5
A5 grammar card — PRESENT SIMPLE OR PRESENT CONTINUOUS?: the question «Is it usual or a fact — or happening now?» splitting into USUAL/REPEATED/FACT (base verb · verb-s · don't/doesn't · Do/Does…? · usually, often, every day) and NOW/TEMPORARY (am/is/are + verb-ing · isn't/aren't · Am/Is/Are…? · now, at the moment, today), the paired example She wears glasses. / She's wearing sunglasses today. and two COMMON MISTAKES fixed on the sheet (Does she wears…? ✘ · She wearing… ✘)
  • Glue the card in and colour the two sets of time clues in two colours.
  • Highlight the two fixed mistakes — they are the ones the class makes.
  • Write a pair of sentences about yourself: one usual, one right now.
  • Check the helper: after do/does the verb goes back to the base form.

solo → pairs → class
Why: The one genuinely new corner of the grammar: verbs of thinking, feeling and owning refuse the continuous. Concept questions close the traps before practice opens them.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5–7: the verbs that refuse the continuous — the one new rule of the day

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Spring the trap first: Translate: я не понимаю. …Everyone who said 'I'm not understanding' — welcome to today's rule.
  2. ex.5 — the list: believe · hate · know · like · love · mean · need · own · prefer · understand · want. These verbs are about thoughts, feelings and owning — no continuous.
  3. ex.6 solo, 4 minutes, then pair check. Key: 1 like/love · 2 don't know / don't believe · 3 does, mean · 4 hate / don't like · 5 don't/can't understand.
  4. ex.7 — choose the form, Kagan Showdown again. Key: 1 're having · 2 need · 3 'm writing · 4 's going · 5 'm having · 6 never forget.
  5. Concept check before moving on: I'm having problems — OK? (yes: not 'own'). I'm needing help — OK? (no: need is stative).
📺 On screen: the ex.7 sentences one at a time on the screen so the whole class repairs the same one (Backup: write the six sentences on the board before the lesson and uncover them one by one)
👦 Students do: trip over the trap, then close it themselves. Expect I'm having problems (correct!) to split the class — that argument is the learning. Typical error: overcorrection — banning ALL -ing after learning the rule

WHAT WE DO: the pairwork questions (SB p.120): eight questions that force the tense choice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the frame: Eight questions. Answer with a verb and two facts. One word is not an answer. Questions from SB p.120 on the board: What subjects are you studying this year? · What are you doing after school today? · Do you own more than one mobile phone? · What TV programmes do you watch every week? — and four more for round two.
  2. Kagan Timed RoundRobin: in fours, each student answers one question for 40 seconds in turn while the rest stay silent.
  3. Listeners hold up two fingers and drop one for each fact that arrives. An open hand at the end means the answer did not reach the bar.
  4. Round two — new fours, the other four questions. The target: longer and without pauses.
  5. Log errors silently on your correction sheet; do not correct aloud — the feedback slot is the start of the next lesson.
📺 On screen: the eight questions and a 40-second timer on the screen (Backup: questions on the board and you time it on the wall clock, calling «stop» yourself)
👦 Students do: speak in turn against the clock while listeners count facts on their fingers. Expect the second round to be visibly longer. Typical error: I'm owning two phones — note it, say nothing

WB Unit 1 (p.4–7)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can describe a person with the age / looks / hair and character adjectives and choose correctly between present simple and present continuous, keeping stative verbs out of the continuous.
  • Subsidiary. Reading three online profiles for gist and detail (SB p.11); an open-cloze task done as careful reading (ex.3); guided discovery of the five tense rules from the profiles (SB p.12 ex.1).
  • Personal. Do not explain the stative rule until students have tripped over it in ex.5–7 — the trap teaches better than the lecture. During the speaking stage log tense errors silently; delayed feedback opens lesson 2.
By the end, students can…
  • sort sixteen describing words into age / looks / hair and use them
  • describe a classmate's character with 3–4 adjectives and their opposites
  • say what they usually do and what they are doing at the moment
  • catch and correct a stative verb used in the continuous

Structure
He's a teenage boy and he's good-looking. · My friends say I'm a cheerful person. · Right now, I'm taking part in a competition. · This term I'm having tennis lessons. · I don't understand this word.
Form
present simple: facts + regular habits; present continuous: now / around now, temporary, future plans. Stative verbs (believe, know, like, need, own, understand, want…) — present simple only.
Pron. watch
bald /bɔːld/ · curly /ˈkɜːli/ · straight /streɪt/ · thirties /ˈθɜːtiz/ · word stress: atˈtractive · ˈconfident · ˈmiserable.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Mum works late this week» — what's wrong? → this week = temporary, so Mum is working late this week.
«I'm needing help» — is that OK? → no: need is a stative verb, I need help.
«Tonight she goes to a party» — why continuous? → a fixed future plan: tonight she's going.

Assumptions: SB p.10–12 (top), syllabus lesson 4. Both tenses are familiar after Prepare 3 and were re-diagnosed in intro lesson 3. Of the sixteen describing words the class usually already owns six or seven (blonde, dark, pretty, funny, shy…). The stative-verb rule is effectively new.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
“We know these tenses already” the moment the grammar startsDon't argue — feed them ex.7 (choose the form) against the clock first. The stative items (need, forget) catch half the class, and after that they work without commentary.
bald / curly / straight mispronounced from spellingDrill by ear before they see the words: bald /bɔːld/ · curly /ˈkɜːli/ · straight /streɪt/ · thirties /ˈθɜːtiz/. Three choral repetitions, then individually.
One-word answers in the speaking stageThe rule of the lesson is one word is not an answer: an answer counts if it contains a verb. The partner holds up two fingers — the second fact is compulsory.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
