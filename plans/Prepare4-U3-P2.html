🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Choose correctly between -self/-selves pronouns and each other, and write a 100-word story with a beginning, middle and end, both past tenses and describing words — starting from a compulsory first line.
What is genuinely hard
Russian «себя» and «друг друга» map onto English unevenly: «мы видимся» has no pronoun at all, so students produce «we see us» or nothing. And in writing, the hard part is not language — it is architecture: stories arrive as one long breathless sentence without a plan.
Typical error to expect
We see us every day / they looked at themselves and smiled (meaning each other) — Russian «друг друга» and «себя» both surface as the wrong pronoun [A2/B1]
becouse · tomorow · alot · wich — the most frequent misspellings; a misspelt word scores nothing, in a class test and later in the exam
Why the lesson is built this way
The pronoun work comes first and is deliberately loud (an auction), because the writing half needs quiet — the lesson spends its energy budget early. Ellen's story is read as a story first, mined as a model second: events ordered, tenses counted, describing words harvested — so when students write, every tool has been touched in the last half hour. The compulsory first line (While I was closing my curtains…) is a gift, not a constraint: it locks the past continuous opening in place and kills the blank page. Peer review against the same checklist closes the loop.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Tonight you close your curtains and see something strange. That is how your story starts — the book decided it for you. By the end of the lesson you'll have a finished 100-word story with a title, a hero and a twist — and it will be read by a real reader, not just by me.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts. The rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a three-sentence repair clinic from last lesson's alibi stage, and the bridge into the pronouns

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — error clinic from the alibi. Three anonymous sentences from your lesson-1 log on the board (a tense slip, a «robbed my phone», a question-order error). Kagan RallyRobin in pairs: find and fix all three, then hands up.
  3. Repair together and be generous: These are OUR mistakes — and they're already better than last week.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page thirty-four, bottom half. Two photos, one small word — and then you write a story.
📺 On screen: three faulty sentences in chalk — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: hunt the errors in pairs and fix them. Expect the question order (What you were doing?) to be found last — it hides best

Vocabulary · -self, -selves, each other
pairs → class
Why: One photo pair carries the whole concept: mirror vs one another. The rule costs a minute; the practice is where Russian «себя/друг друга» interference surfaces.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 photo concept + ex.2 controlled choice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open, the two photos: Photo B — where are they looking? At THEMSELVES: a mirror. Photo A — at EACH OTHER. Key: 1 B (looking at themselves — the mirror photo) · 2 A (looking at each other). Rule on the board: subject = object → a -self/-selves pronoun (She's looking at herself: a mirror). Two or more people doing it to one another → each other (They're looking at each other).
  2. Concept checks from the plan header — the Josie one and the Tom one, cold-called.
  3. Build the pronoun ladder chorally: myself, yourself, himself, herself — OURSELVES, YOURSELVES, THEMSELVES. Fist-bump the -selves plurals.
  4. ex.2 solo, 3 minutes, pair check. Key: 1 himself · 2 myself · 3 yourself · 4 yourselves · 5 ourselves · 6 themselves. Watch item 4: yourselves — the plural trap of the set.
📺 On screen: the pronoun ladder in chalk, singulars left, plurals right — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: anchor the concept on the photos, then choose pronouns. Expect item 4 (yourselves) to catch half the class — it is the item the auction recycles. Typical error: theirselves
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare4_Unit5_Vocabulary2_Reflexive_Pronouns_A5
A5 grammar card — WHO DID IT?: two halves — SELF / SAME GROUP with the -self and -selves forms (myself · yourself · ourselves · yourselves · themselves) and the models We protected ourselves. · They introduced themselves.; and TOGETHER / ONE ANOTHER with each other (They looked at each other. · We help each other.) — plus an ENDINGS MAP (we → ourselves · you plural → yourselves · they → themselves) and the contrast They looked at themselves (in a mirror) vs They looked at each other
  • Glue the card in and draw the two arrows: the action comes back, or goes across.
  • Say the mirror pair aloud — that is the whole difference.
  • Fill the endings map from memory with the sheet covered.
  • Write two sentences about your class, one per column.

18–30′
Grammar auction
teams of 3–4
Why: The TB's own idea, upgraded: bidding forces every team to COMMIT to an answer and argue for it — engagement does the drilling.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 run as an auction: teams bid, argue and commit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Rules in one minute: TB idea for ex.3, run as a GRAMMAR AUCTION: teams get £100; for every item you read three candidate answers; teams bid for the right to answer — correct answer wins the pot, wrong answer loses the bid. The arguing about pronouns IS the practice.
  2. Run the five ex.3 items plus three of your own («We text ___ every day» · «I taught ___ to swim» · «Look at ___ in the mirror, you two!»). For each: read the sentence, offer three candidate answers, open the bidding at £10.
  3. Key for ex.3: 1 each other · 2 herself · 3 each other · 4 each other · 5 yourselves.
  4. Bank the scores on the board; the winning team names the rule that earned them the pot.
  5. Collect the two hardest items with Kagan Numbered Heads Together afterwards: Why each other and not themselves? Number threes!
📺 On screen: auction board in chalk: teams, budgets, pots — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: bid, argue, commit, defend the rule. Expect the arguing to BE the practice — teams talk each other out of «theirselves». Typical error: betting big on «yourselves» items and small on «each other» ones — note which and recycle in the wrap-up

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: read Ellen's story as readers, then order its events

46–54′
Story anatomy
pairs
Why: Beginning-middle-end, both past tenses, describing words — the Prepare to write box turns a nice story into a checklist a 13-year-old can actually follow.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–5: count the tenses, harvest the describing words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4 as a race: 2 minutes — How many past simple verbs? How many continuous? Key: past simple ×15 (went, left, took, arrived, noticed, realised, shouted, heard, looked, started, were, ran, caught, was, was) · past continuous ×6 (was feeling, was getting, was standing, was happening, was trying, was talking) — 15 against 6: a story RUNS on past simple; the continuous paints its background.
  2. ex.5: sort the highlighted words — adjective or adverb? Key: adjectives: exhausted, early (in his early twenties) · adverbs: early (left school early), suddenly, immediately, luckily, soon.
  3. Harvest onto the board into two baskets: FEELINGS (exhausted, nervous…) and STORY GLUE (suddenly, immediately, luckily, soon). These are the words their own stories will borrow.
  4. Mini-drill: Say «suddenly» like something actually happened. Choral, dramatic, once.
📺 On screen: two word-baskets in chalk — the board is the medium and both baskets stay up through the writing stage, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: count, sort, harvest. Expect «early» to confuse (adjective AND adverb) — flag it as a bonus fact, not a problem. Typical error: calling luckily an adjective because it describes feelings
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare4_Unit5_Writing_Story_A5
A5 writing card — WRITE A STORY: the five-point PLAN (where + when · who was there · what was happening · what happened suddenly · how it ended), the model story A HERO FOR A DAY annotated in the margin (title · setting · background · action · sudden event · ending), a USEFUL LANGUAGE strip (Last… · While… · Suddenly… · Luckily… · In the end…) and a CHECK list (beginning/middle/end · past simple · past continuous · adjectives and adverbs · spelling and punctuation)
  • Glue the card in on the page where the story will be written.
  • Mark the six labels in the model — a story has a skeleton.
  • Write your own story with a title and all five linkers from the strip.
  • Check the two pasts: the background is -ing, the sudden event is not.
When: ~6 min at the start of the “Story anatomy” stagePrep: 1 × A5 per pupil. Scissors, glue sticks, pencils.
SB p.35 ex.4–5 · board
54–62′
Planning · notes
solo
Why: Four questions stop the blank-page freeze: nobody starts writing without knowing where the story goes. Planning is where weak writers are made average.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6–7: four questions, four lines — the plan before the pen

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Give the task exactly as the book does: Your story MUST begin: “While I was closing my curtains one night, I noticed something unusual.”
  2. Four planning questions on the board: Where are you? What do you notice? What are the 2–3 main events? How does it end — and how do you FEEL? Four answers, one line each, 5 minutes, silence.
  3. ex.7: show your plan to your partner for 60 seconds — one question or one idea from the partner, no more.
  4. Walk and unstick: anyone with an empty line gets a menu, not an answer — a light? a cat? a person? a police car?
📺 On screen: the four planning questions in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: plan in four lines, exchange one idea. Expect two or three students stuck at «what do you notice» — the menu trick unsticks them without writing their story for them

🎯 Writing out · my story
solo, silent
Why: The productive outcome in writing: a real 100-word story with a compulsory first line, built on a model, a plan and a checklist. Started AND finished in class.

WHAT WE DO: ex.8: write the story — about 100 words, checklist on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Eighteen minutes. About 100 words — 80 is the floor. The first line is already written for you. Checklist is on the board. Go.
  2. Visible timer on. First five minutes: questions allowed in a whisper. After that — silent mode, palm down.
  3. Walk the room with a pencil: underline (don't fix) one spelling and one tense slip per draft as you pass.
  4. Minute twelve, quiet announcement: Time for a title. Steal the shape of Ellen's: HERO for a day — X for a night.
  5. Fast finishers: swap with another fast finisher and write an alternative two-sentence ending for their story.
  6. Your own model in reserve if anyone needs to SEE the bar: TB model answer: «False alarm! While I was closing my curtains one night, I noticed something unusual. A man was standing in the street… He left his keys and his phone inside.»
📺 On screen: visible timer counting down 18 minutes (Backup: write the end time in the corner of the board and update every five minutes by hand)
👦 Students do: write, hit the floor of 80 words, title the story. Expect the middle to sag around minute eight (the events pile up without glue) — point silently at the STORY GLUE basket. Typical error: the whole story in continuous — the underline pencil catches it

80–88′
Peer review + polish
pairs
Why: A checklist swap turns every student into an editor: they catch each other's tense slips and misspellings faster than they catch their own — both sides learn.

WHAT WE DO: checklist swap: every story gets a real reader

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap notebooks. Kagan RallyCoach against the board checklist: tick the five points, circle ONE spelling doubt, write ONE compliment word at the bottom (scary! funny! wow!).
  2. Two minutes back with your own draft: fix what your reader found.
  3. Ask for volunteers: Whose partner's story deserves ten seconds of fame? Read one or two first lines beyond the compulsory one.
📺 On screen: the checklist is already on the board from minute 5 — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: review, compliment, polish. Expect reviewers to be stricter than you are — channel it: circles, not crossings-out. Typical find: a missing title — cheap fix, instant win

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can use -self/-selves pronouns and each other correctly and have written and peer-checked a ~100-word story with a clear shape, both past tenses and describing words.

  • choose between myself/ourselves/themselves and each other — and say why
  • put the five events of a story into beginning / middle / end
  • open a story with a past-continuous line and interrupt it with a simple
  • check a partner's story against a five-point list and give one compliment

Lexis

Structure
They're looking at themselves. · They're looking at each other. · Did you all enjoy yourselves? · While I was closing my curtains one night, I noticed something unusual. · Suddenly… · Luckily… · Immediately…
Form
subject = object → -self (sg) / -selves (pl); mutual action between 2+ people → each other; story shape: beginning (continuous background) → middle (simple events) → end (+ feeling).
Pron. watch
ourselves /aʊəˈselvz/ — stress on SELVES · each other linked: /iːˈtʃʌðə/ · exhausted /ɪɡˈzɔːstɪd/
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Josie introduced herself» — who did Josie introduce? → Josie. Same person → -self.
«Tom and I argue with each other» — does Tom argue with Tom? → no: Tom with me, me with Tom — mutual → each other.
«Did you enjoy yourselves?» — how many people am I asking? → several: yourselves is the plural of yourself.

Assumptions: SB p.34 (bottom)–35, syllabus lesson 16. The class met myself/yourself passively in Prepare 3; the -selves plurals and the each-other contrast are effectively new. Past tenses were combined last lesson — the story recycles them under pressure. Lesson 1's error log is in your folder.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«We see us every day» — the Russian mutual verb has no pronounAnchor with the mirror photo pair: mirror = -self, two arrows = each other. Then drill the three worst offenders as chunks: see each other, help each other, text each other.
Stories arrive as one endless sentenceThe plan is compulsory and checked BEFORE writing starts: four answers, four lines. No plan — no pen.
Fast finishers at minute ten of the writing stageTB fast-finisher: swap stories and write an alternative ENDING for your partner's story — two sentences, same characters.
Weak writers freeze at 100 wordsThe floor is 80 words and the model is open on the desk: copying the SHAPE of Ellen's story is allowed and said out loud to be allowed.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the OGE — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 35 (an email to a friend, 100–120 words, four marking criteria) — organisation and accuracy criteria in particular.

A story is not the exam genre, but it trains the exam muscles harder than the email does: a beginning-middle-end plan, linking words (suddenly, luckily, immediately), past tenses under control and a spelling check at the end. Students who can write a 100-word story write the exam email with room to spare.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the OGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
