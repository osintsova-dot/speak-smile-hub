🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use modals of obligation, prohibition and advice in the present (have to, must, need to · don't have to, needn't · mustn't, aren't allowed to, can't · should, ought to, 'd better) and in the past (had to, didn't have to, wasn't allowed to, needn't have, should have, shouldn't have); then use eleven phrasal verbs for money and shopping.
What is genuinely hard
One distinction carries the whole lesson and Russian does not make it: mustn't forbids, don't have to frees. «Не надо» covers both. The past forms add a second layer that is genuinely B2: needn't have bought (you did it and it was unnecessary) versus didn't need to buy (it was unnecessary; we don't know whether you did).
Typical error to expect
«You mustn't pay in cash» for «you don't have to pay in cash» — the single most expensive modal error in the unit. Mustn't forbids; don't have to frees. Russian «не надо» covers both, which is why it has to be taught as a choice, not as a translation.
«I must to pay», «you don't must» — must takes a bare infinitive and has no negative of obligation at all: the negative of must is a prohibition (mustn't), and «it isn't necessary» is don't have to / needn't.
«should have went», «must have be», «can't have saw» — past speculation and past advice are modal + have + THIRD form. Always have, never has, and never the second form.
«I'll pay back you», «set aside it» — with a pronoun object, the particle goes after the pronoun: pay YOU back, set IT aside. With a noun, both orders work.
Why the lesson is built this way
Guided discovery from four sentences on the board, then the form (which of these take to?), then the meaning contrast drilled physically — students stand on one side of the room for FORBIDDEN and the other for NOT NECESSARY, because that distinction is spatial before it is verbal. The past forms come after the stirrer, when the present forms are secure. Phrasal verbs close the lesson: high-frequency, low-effort, and immediately usable in the speaking task.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
Four sentences: I should get up early. I have to get up early. I don't have to get up early. I mustn't get up early. Four completely different lives. In Russian, three of them can be «надо» or «не надо» — which is exactly why this is worth a whole lesson. And by the end you'll be able to say what you should have done differently last weekend.

Lead-in · four sentences, four different lives
desks → pairs
Why: Naming the Russian interference in the first ten minutes is worth more than correcting it for a term. The class does the naming.

WHAT WE DO: the four-way contrast is established before the book opens

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write on the board, one under the other: I should get up early today. I have to get up early today. I don't have to get up early today. I mustn't get up early today.
  2. Ninety seconds in pairs: what is the difference? Give me a situation for each one.
  3. Take four answers and label them on the board: advice · necessary · not necessary · forbidden.
  4. Then the killer question, and ask it straight: which two are the same in Russian? They will say «не надо» for both the third and the fourth. That is the lesson's whole problem, and now the class has named it themselves.
  5. Each student writes four sentences of their own about this week, using the same four verbs. Two minutes, silent.
📺 On screen: the four sentences and their four labels on the board — they stay up all lesson. Backup: dictate them; four lines takes a minute.
👦 Students do: compare the four meanings in pairs, then write four personal sentences using the same frame.

Grammar in context 1 · meaning, then form
desks → pairs
Why: Meaning first, then form, then error correction — the order that stops modals becoming a list of translations.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.60 exercises 1a–1b and 2 — the rules, the syntax, and six corrections

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 1a in pairs, four minutes: sort the modals into four meanings. Answers: a have/has to, must, need to (it is necessary) · b don't/doesn't have to, needn't, don't/doesn't need to (it is not necessary) · c mustn't, aren't/isn't allowed to, can't (it is forbidden) · d ought to, should, 'd better (advice).
  2. Exercise 1b — the form questions, and this is where the marks are: which take to, how questions are made, what 'd better stands for. Answers: a to + infinitive: ought to, have to, don't have to, am not allowed to, need to, don't need to; bare infinitive: should, must, mustn't, 'd better, needn't, can't · b questions use do: Does everybody who works have to pay tax? · c the negatives of should and 'd better · d need to and should · e 'd better = HAD better.
  3. Write the two ghosts on the board and leave them: NOT «I must to pay» · NOT «you don't must».
  4. One language note worth ten seconds: «You'd better do the washing-up» is not neutral advice — it carries a threat. If you don't, something bad happens.
  5. Exercise 2 (six sentences with mistakes) individually, four minutes, then Rally Coach. Answers: 1 You'd/had better pay online · 2 We ought to ask · 3 Correct · 4 Do you have to be British · 5 You needn't carry cash · 6 You mustn't / can't / aren't allowed to / shouldn't use somebody else's ID.
  6. Mixed ability: weaker students find and underline the modal in each sentence before they try to correct anything.
📺 On screen: the two ghosts and the four meaning labels on the board. Backup: the same rules are in the Grammar reference on SB p.68.
👦 Students do: sort meanings in pairs, answer form questions in open class, then correct six sentences and coach a partner.

WHAT WE DO: the mustn't / don't have to distinction made physical

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two walls: FORBIDDEN and NOT NECESSARY. Everyone stands in the middle.
  2. You read situations; students walk to a wall and then say the sentence: You … use your phone in the exam. (→ mustn't) · You … bring a dictionary. (→ don't have to) · You … be British to open an account here. (→ don't have to) · You … use somebody else's ID. (→ mustn't) · You … pay in cash — cards are fine. (→ don't have to) · You … take photos in the gallery. (→ aren't allowed to)
  3. Eight items, ninety seconds. Anyone at the wrong wall says the correct sentence and stays standing — nobody is out.
  4. Finish standing, then straight into the past forms while the contrast is warm.
📺 On screen: the two wall labels. Backup: name two walls aloud — no materials needed.
👦 Students do: walk to a wall on every item and produce the full sentence when pointed at.

Grammar in context 1b · the past forms
desks → pairs
Why: Past modals are where B2 is won or lost. They get eighteen minutes, a visual organiser and thirteen written items — and they come after a stirrer, not after forty minutes of sitting.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.60 exercises 3, 5a–b, 6 and 7 — the present text, then past obligation and past criticism

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 3 first (the bank-account text, ten gaps, one word each), five minutes, in pairs. Answers: a don't · b must · c had · d can't/mustn't · e have/need · f ought · g have/need · h can't/mustn't · i needn't/shouldn't · j should/ought to.
  2. Now the past. Exercise 5a in pairs, then check: a had to, needed to (past necessity) · b needn't have (it wasn't necessary but you did it) · c didn't have to, didn't need to (it wasn't necessary) · d weren't/wasn't allowed to, couldn't (past prohibition) · e should/ought to have, shouldn't have (past criticism or regret). The form is modal + HAVE + past participle.
  3. Draw the contrast that decides the marks, on the board, with two sentences: I didn't need to come early (it wasn't necessary — maybe I came, maybe I didn't). I needn't have come early (it wasn't necessary AND I came anyway). Concept-check it: In sentence two, did I come? — Yes. In sentence one? — We don't know.
  4. Exercise 6 (seven sentences) individually, five minutes. Answers: 1 We ought to have gone to a cheaper restaurant · 2 I wasn't allowed to open an account · 3 I had to show my ID · 4 You needn't/shouldn't have paid for my sandwich · 5 We didn't have to go to the bank · 6 We didn't need to buy bread · 7 Anne shouldn't have had such a big lunch.
  5. Exercise 7 (six transformations) in pairs, four minutes. Answers: 1 should/ought to have opened a bank account · 2 needn't have bought me a present · 3 had to show my student ID · 4 weren't allowed to speak to the director · 5 didn't need to pay a fee · 6 didn't have/need to pay the waiter a tip last night.
  6. Before they start exercise 6, make them draw the spider gram the Teacher's Book suggests: three branches — past modal + to (had to, didn't have to, weren't allowed to), modal + have + past participle (needn't, should, ought to, shouldn't), modal + bare infinitive (couldn't).
📺 On screen: the two-sentence past contrast and the three-branch spider gram on the board. Backup: both are reproduced in the Grammar reference on SB p.68.
👦 Students do: complete the text in pairs, draw the spider gram, then work through thirteen written items with one peer check.

Developing vocabulary · eleven phrasal verbs
desks → pairs
Why: Phrasal verbs are best taught in a semantic field — all eleven are about money — and the word-order rule is the difference between recognising them and using them.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.61 exercises 1–3 — money and shopping phrasal verbs, worked out from context

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed, three sentences on the board: I'm saving __ to buy a bike. · Hotels often put __ their prices in July. · Prices have gone __ a lot here. One word fits all three. (up.) Thirty seconds, and it frames the whole stage.
  2. Exercise 1 in pairs, six minutes — meaning from context, no dictionaries. Answers: 1 set aside = save · 2 cut down on = reduce spending on · 3 pay back = return money you borrowed · 4 splash out = spend a lot on something unusual · 5 get by = survive · 6 come to = add up to; chip in = contribute · 7 shop around = compare prices in different shops · 8 sell out = all be bought · 9 run out of = have none left · 10 rip off = make somebody pay far too much.
  3. Fast finishers: which of these take a direct object? (set aside, cut down on, pay back, run out of, rip off — and «comes to» takes a complement, not an object.)
  4. Teach the word-order rule with two examples on the board, because it is a written error all year: I'll pay YOU back · I'll pay back the money — but never «pay back you».
  5. Exercise 2 (seven rewrites) individually, four minutes. Answers: 1 My brother is getting by on very little money · 2 I'm setting aside some of my allowance · 3 That video game is selling out really fast · 4 I'll pay you back as soon as I can · 5 They ripped you off! · 6 I've run out of money · 7 She needs to cut down on buying clothes. Exercise 3 orally, fast: 1 aside · 2 back · 3 out · 4 off · 5 in.
📺 On screen: the three «up» sentences and the pay-you-back word-order rule on the board. Backup: no equipment required.
👦 Students do: predict, work out eleven meanings in pairs, then rewrite seven sentences alone.

Workbook p.42–43
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will be better able to express obligation, prohibition, absence of obligation and advice in the present and the past, in the context of money and rules.
  • Subsidiary. Money and shopping phrasal verbs, including word order with pronoun objects; weak forms in have to, ought to and 'd better.
  • Personal. Do not translate the modals into Russian, even once. The moment «не надо» is said aloud, the distinction the lesson is built on becomes invisible.
By the end, students can…
  • say what is necessary, what is not necessary and what is forbidden, without confusing the last two
  • give advice with should, ought to and 'd better and know the difference in force
  • criticise a past action (you shouldn't have) and cancel a past necessity (you needn't have)
  • use eleven money phrasal verbs and put the pronoun in the right place
  • explain the money rules of their own country to a visitor

Structure
You have to show ID. · You don't have to pay in cash. · You mustn't use somebody else's card. · You'd better pay online. · I needn't have bought it. · We ought to have gone somewhere cheaper.
Form
must / mustn't / needn't / should / 'd better + bare infinitive · have to / need to / ought to / be allowed to + to + infinitive · questions and negatives of have to and need to use do · past: had to, didn't have to, wasn't allowed to, couldn't · modal + HAVE + past participle for needn't have, should(n't) have, ought to have · phrasal verbs: pay somebody back, set something aside (pronoun always in the middle).
Pron. watch
modals live on weak forms and this is where listening breaks down: have to is /ˈhæftə/, ought to is /ˈɔːtə/, 'd better is a half-audible /d/ that students never hear and therefore never produce. Drill the three of them in whole sentences, never in isolation
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
mustn't / don't have to: «You mustn't pay in cash» — is cash allowed? — No. «You don't have to pay in cash» — is cash allowed? — Yes.
needn't have: «I needn't have bought bread» — did I buy it? — Yes. Was it necessary? — No.
didn't need to: «I didn't need to buy bread» — do we know if I bought it? — No.
'd better: Is it neutral advice or is there a warning in it? — A warning.

Assumptions: Students use must, have to and should at B1 level and can form the past participle of common verbs. The four-way meaning contrast and all past modal forms are new as a system.

Likely problemWhat you do
«You mustn't pay by card» when they mean «you don't have to».The two-wall stirrer is the fix, and it is repeated verbally every time it recurs: forbidden, or just not necessary? Do not explain again — ask the question.
«Should have went», «must have be».Write MODAL + HAVE + THIRD FORM on the board as three boxes and point at it silently. If irregular participles are shaky, put the list on the desk for the writing stages.
The lesson has two big halves and the phrasal verbs get squeezed.Protect exercise 1 on p.61 (the meanings) and exercise 2 (the rewrites); exercise 3 can be done orally in ninety seconds, and the workbook covers the rest tonight.
Students translate every modal into Russian in their notes.Give them a different note-taking frame: four columns headed NECESSARY / NOT NECESSARY / FORBIDDEN / ADVICE, and the English verbs underneath. No Russian column exists, so none gets filled.
«I'll pay back you» survives into the writing.Two examples on the board and one correction in the delayed-correction stage. It is a word-order habit, so it needs to be seen, not explained.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 19–25 (grammar in a text — modal verbs are a named line of the codifier) and task 37 (the personal email, where advice is asked for by name).

Obligation and advice are the modal cluster the Russian exam tests most: have to is a rule, needn't is a free choice, mustn't is a prohibition and should is only advice. Half of Russian candidates render all four with «надо». The past forms (should have, needn't have) are B2 territory and the difference between a good mark and a very good one in writing.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
