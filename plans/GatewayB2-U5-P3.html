🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Listen to four short news stories and match eight statements to the right story; then speculate and deduce with modals in the present (must be, can't be, might / may / could be) and in the past (must have been, can't have been, might have done).
What is genuinely hard
The listening task is a matching task, which means the words in the statements will NOT be the words in the recording — students who hunt for identical words score nothing. The grammar half has one Russian-specific trap: can is not used for guessing (he can be at home ✗), although can't is the standard negative deduction (he can't be at home ✓).
Typical error to expect
«He can be at home» for a guess — English uses may / might / could for possibility and reserves can for ability and permission. Can't, however, IS the negative of a certain deduction: he can't be at home, his car isn't here.
«should have went», «must have be», «can't have saw» — past speculation and past advice are modal + have + THIRD form. Always have, never has, and never the second form.
Why the lesson is built this way
The listening is placed early and given two hearings with two different jobs — who/where/what first, then the matching. The grammar then grows straight out of it: the class already wants to speculate about how a gold bar ended up in a Bavarian lake, so must have been and can't have been are the language they are reaching for. The lesson finishes with a picture-based speculation task, which is also the exam's picture task — but it is framed as a puzzle, not as practice.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
A cleaner in a South Korean airport finds seven gold bars. A girl on holiday finds a gold bar in a lake in Bavaria. Somebody leaves a hundred pounds under a paper towel on a train. Four true stories — you'll hear them, and then you'll need a new piece of grammar, because «I think maybe» is not enough: it must have been … it can't have been …

Lead-in · would you keep it?

Why: Prediction that is written down and later checked converts a listening from a test into a task.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.64 exercise 1 and the Teacher's Book warmer — the topic opened with a moral question

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three questions, asked to the whole room and answered in pairs: If someone was really kind to you, would you pay it back or forget it? If you won some money, would you splash out or set some aside? Two minutes — and the phrasal verbs from last lesson are the point.
  2. Open at p.64. Pairs describe the four photos, taking turns so that both speak: What can you see? What do you think happened?
  3. Take three predictions for the whole class, write two of them on the board, and promise to check them against the recording. That promise is what makes the first listening active.
📺 On screen: two of the class's predictions written on the board so they can be checked later. Backup: no equipment.
👦 Students do: answer two personal questions in pairs, then describe photos and predict the stories.

Listening · four stories, two hearings, two jobs
desks
Why: Matching tasks fail for a nameable reason — word-matching instead of idea-matching — and naming it before the second hearing is worth more than a third hearing.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.64 exercises 2 and 3 (track 25) — first the facts, then the matching task

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. First hearing — exercise 2: who, where, what. Play track 25 once. Answers: track 25, four news stories: 1 Lisa Grace and Adam Staples — in a field — 2,528 ancient silver coins · 2 a 16-year-old girl — Lake Königssee in Bavaria — a gold bar · 3 Ella Johannessen, 23 — on a train, under a paper towel — £100 · 4 a cleaner — an airport in South Korea — seven gold bars.
  2. Check in pairs, then whole class, and go back to the two predictions on the board — right or wrong?
  3. Before the second hearing, teach the strategy for matching, and give it thirty seconds of your best attention: read all eight statements first. Underline the IDEA in each one. The recording will NOT use those words — if you hear the same word, be suspicious.
  4. Second hearing — exercise 3, eight statements to four stories. Play track 25 again. Answers: matching A–H to the four stories: A 1 · B 4 · C 3 · D 2 · E 3 · F 1 · G 2 · H 3.
  5. Check with the evidence: for three of the eight, read the line from the script that proves it (Teacher's Book, class audio script p.123).
  6. Exercise 4 · Critical thinkers, four minutes in fours: who does old treasure belong to — the finder, the country, the landowner, or the country it came from originally? One position per group, one speaker.
📺 On screen: recording — track 25 in the board app, cued before the lesson; the four prediction notes on the board. Backup: the full script is in the Teacher's Book (class audio script, TB p.123) — read the four stories aloud yourself at a steady pace, twice.
👦 Students do: listen twice with a different job each time, then argue a group position on who owns found treasure.

32–38′
Stirrer · what's in the bag?
open floor
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies. It also front-loads the exact three structures the presentation stage is about to formalise.

WHAT WE DO: the target grammar produced physically before it is presented

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hold up your own bag (or a colleague's). You have twenty seconds to guess what is inside. You may not say «I think». Use these three: and write them on the board — It must be … · It can't be … · It might be …
  2. Everyone stands; guesses come round the room, one each, no repeats. You confirm or deny with a straight face.
  3. Second round with a photo on the wall or an object in your hand, and this time force the negative: tell me something it CAN'T be, and why.
  4. Sit down on the last item and go straight into the book — the grammar stage now has language already in the air.
📺 On screen: the three frames written on the board. Backup: your bag and your voice — nothing else needed.
👦 Students do: stand and produce one speculation each, then one negative deduction each.

38–52′
Grammar in context 2a · speculating about now
desks → pairs
Why: Speculation is a probability scale, so it is taught as three questions rather than as three words — and the class is forced off «must» by design.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.64 exercises 1–3 — the rules, seven items and a picture-based speculation task

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Warmer on the board: Dan's dad has got five houses. He can't / might / must be rich. Which one, and why? (must — we are almost certain.)
  2. Exercise 1 in pairs. Before they do it, put three questions on the board and point at them after each sentence: Is it almost certainly true? Is it possible? Is it almost certainly NOT true? Answers: a might, may, could (and might not, may not) = possible · b must = almost certainly true · c can't = almost certainly not true · d can't is the opposite of must.
  3. Name the Russian trap explicitly: «He can be at home» is not English. For a guess, use may, might or could. But «can't» IS correct for a negative deduction. Write both on the board, one crossed out.
  4. Exercise 2 individually, four minutes. Answers: 1 must · 2 can't · 3 must · 4 can't · 5 must · 6 may not · 7 might, may not.
  5. Exercise 3a–3b in pairs with the picture: speculate about the person, then correct the sentences. Answers: 1 must · 2 can't · 3 can't · 4 may/might · 5 must · 6 can't · 7 must/may/might.
  6. If everyone defaults to must, force variety with the Teacher's Book prompts on the board: Her bag ___ be very small. She likes basketball but she ___ like other sports too. Her surname is Schmidt, but she ___ be German.
📺 On screen: the three checking questions and the crossed-out «he can be at home» on the board. Backup: the rules are also in the Grammar reference on SB p.68.
👦 Students do: sort the rules in pairs, complete seven items alone, then speculate about a photo with three different modals.

52–70′
Grammar in context 2b · speculating about the past
desks → pairs
Why: Past speculation is the most useful single structure of the unit for both exams, and it is drilled here in three formats: rules, correction and connected text.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.65 exercises 4–6 — modal + have + past participle, ending with the Satoshi Nakamoto text

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board first: My great-grandmother had five houses. — Really? She must be / must have been rich. Which one, and why? (have been — we are talking about the past.)
  2. Exercise 4 in pairs. Answers: past speculation: a may have, might have, could have; couldn't have · b must have · c can't have. The form is modal + HAVE + past participle, always HAVE, never HAS. Write the form on the board as three boxes: MODAL + HAVE + 3rd FORM, and say the rule that saves the marks: always HAVE, never HAS.
  3. Exercise 5 (eight sentences to correct) individually, five minutes. Before they start, elicit what to look for: wrong modal, wrong participle, missing have. Answers: 1 You must have seen Liam · 2 she might have read it already · 3 You can't have finished that film already · 4 You must have had a good rest · 5 It can't have been Kim · 6 Joel might have seen that film · 7 She mightn't have received it · 8 The exam can't have been very hard.
  4. Exercise 6 — the Satoshi Nakamoto text, seven minutes. Set it up with two questions: What do you know about bitcoin? Who was Satoshi Nakamoto? Point out that every gap needs a PAST modal. Answers: the Satoshi Nakamoto text, all past modals: a might/may/could have been · b may/might/could have used · c may/might/could have invented · d couldn't have done · e must have wanted · f may/might/could have been · g must have created · h may/might/could have been · i must have made.
  5. Check in Rally Coach, and the coaching question is why that modal? — certain, possible or impossible.
📺 On screen: the three-box form (MODAL + HAVE + 3rd FORM) on the board, left up until the end of the unit. Backup: SB p.68 has the same in the Grammar reference.
👦 Students do: sort rules, correct eight sentences alone, then complete a nine-gap text and justify each modal to a partner.

🎯 Freer practice · six situations, six explanations
pairs → fours
Why: Explaining an odd situation is the natural home of past speculation, and the puzzle at the end is the one activity teenagers ask to repeat.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.65 exercises 7–8 «Use it … don't lose it» — the class explains six odd situations

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Model one first so the level is clear: Sandra can't have been riding her bike carefully yesterday, because she had an accident.
  2. Pairs write one explanation for each of the six situations, six minutes. Every explanation must contain a past modal and a because.
  3. Pairs Compare: two pairs join, compare all six, and choose the best explanation for each situation.
  4. Whole class: take the best version of three situations. Possible answers to have ready: Something serious must have happened to make Joe furious · My uncle might have won the lottery · My sister must have studied really hard · My cousin might have had private lessons.
  5. Extra, if there is time and energy: the Teacher's Book puzzle. A man was pushing something. When he got to a hotel, he realised he had lost all his money. What happened? Let them speculate with past modals; the answer is that he was playing Monopoly.
📺 On screen: the model sentence on the board. Backup: nothing to fail — spoken and written on paper.
👦 Students do: write six explanations in pairs, then negotiate the best version in fours.

Accuracy check · six sentences, marked by a partner
desks
Why: A written check makes the invisible visible: students who can say «must have been» often write «must have be».

WHAT WE DO: a short written consolidation of both halves of the grammar

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Dictate or board six prompts: (1) Somebody has eaten my sandwich. (2) The lights are on in their flat. (3) He failed the exam. (4) She's smiling at her phone. (5) My keys aren't here. (6) They arrived two hours late.
  2. Each student writes one speculation for each — present or past as the prompt requires. Five minutes, silent.
  3. Swap notebooks. The partner marks exactly two things: is it MODAL + HAVE + third form where the past is needed, and is the modal the right strength?
  4. Take three to the board and let the class polish them.
📺 On screen: the six prompts on the board. Backup: dictate them in ninety seconds.
👦 Students do: write six speculations, then peer-mark a partner's for two named features.

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will be better able to speculate and make deductions about the present and the past using modal verbs, in the context of unexplained discoveries.
  • Subsidiary. Listening to match statements to speakers (idea, not word); connected speech in must have been and can't have known.
  • Personal. Play the recording exactly twice. The third play is almost always a substitute for teaching the strategy, and the strategy is what transfers.
By the end, students can…
  • match eight statements to four short news stories by idea rather than by word
  • speculate about now with must, can't, may, might and could
  • speculate about the past with modal + have + past participle
  • avoid «he can be at home» while keeping «he can't be at home»
  • explain an odd situation with a past modal and a reason

Structure
It must be expensive. · They can't be serious. · She might not have received it. · He must have wanted to stay anonymous.
Form
present: modal + bare infinitive (must be, can't be, may / might / could be) · past: modal + HAVE + past participle (must have been, can't have been, might have done) · always HAVE, never HAS · can is not used for positive speculation, but can't is the negative of must.
Pron. watch
past speculation is one connected blur: must have been → /ˈmʌstəvbɪn/, can't have known → /kɑːntəvnəʊn/. Students who expect three separate words hear none of them. Say it fast, then slow, then fast again — and make them say the fast version
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
must: «He must be rich» — am I sure? — Almost completely. Do I know it? — No, I'm deducing.
can't: «It can't have been Kim» — is it possible? — Almost certainly not.
might have: «She might have read it» — did she? — Maybe. Is it about now or the past? — The past.
ICQ before the matching task: Do you look for the same words? — No. What do you look for? — The same idea.

Assumptions: Students met must and can't for deduction at B1 in isolated sentences. Past forms with have + past participle are new, and matching listening tasks have been done once before, in Unit 2.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Everyone uses «must» for everything in the speculation stages.The three gapped prompts from the Teacher's Book go on the board and force may / might / can't. If it persists, ban «must» for one round.
«He can be at home.»Write it on the board and cross it out beside the correct «he can't be at home». Both forms in one visual, because the asymmetry is the confusing part.
The matching listening produces panic on the first hearing.The first hearing has a much easier job (who, where, what) and is checked before the matching begins. Say the sequence aloud so they know relief is coming.
«Must have be», «can't have saw».The three-box visual (MODAL + HAVE + 3rd FORM) stays on the board and you point at it instead of speaking. Keep the irregular-verb list on the desks during the writing stage.
The Critical thinkers debate about treasure runs long.Four minutes, one position per group, one speaker each — the structure is the timer. If it is going well, take the extra two minutes from the final accuracy check, not from the grammar.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
