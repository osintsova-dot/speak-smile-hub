🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Own the eight relationship phrasal verbs (get on, fall out, make up, split up…) and write a ~100-word reply to a friend's email that answers ALL the questions asked, with a greeting, advice phrases and a closing.
What is genuinely hard
Phrasal verbs are stored wrong by default: students remember the verb and drop the particle, and Russian has one word where English has verb + particle. In writing, the killer is not language — it is bookkeeping: Sam asks FOUR questions (How are you? counts!), and a skipped question sinks the whole reply.
Typical error to expect
Our plane took off... I mean took? flew? — the particle dropped or swapped (get back/get on, take off/take away): phrasal verbs stored as verb + particle chunks or not at all [A2/B1]
An email that answers two questions out of three (or misses How are you?) — the content criterion of task 35 drops instantly [national exam report, the No. 1 task-35 error]
Why the lesson is built this way
The phrasal-verb work comes first and is deliberately loud (a story-rebuild race and a call-and-response drill), because the writing half needs quiet — the lesson spends its energy budget early. Ana and Nicole's exchange is read as a reader first, mined as a writer second: the four margin notes show HOW a reply covers everything. The question-counting drill on Sam's email is the hinge of the lesson: the class discovers that How are you? is question number four — the trap that costs real students real marks. Planning answers all four in note form before anyone writes; peer review counts them again after. The reflex, installed twice, survives.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“A friend emails you four questions and you answer three — that's how most replies fail, and nobody notices they've done it. Today you learn the counting trick that makes your reply bulletproof — plus eight verbs for the whole life of a friendship: getting on, falling out, making up. By the end you'll have a finished reply on paper, checked by a real reader.”

Vocabulary · relationship phrasal verbs
pairs → class
Why: Eight verbs about friendship arrive as matched meanings plus their irregular pasts in one move — the pasts cost nothing now and save a lesson later.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 match meanings + irregular pasts; ex.2 controlled choice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 in pairs: match the eight verbs to meanings a–h — 3 minutes. Key: 1b hang out (spend a lot of time somewhere) · 2a get on (have a good relationship) · 3g get together · 4h fall out · 5d come round · 6e look after · 7f make up · 8c split up · irregular pasts: come–came, fall–fell, get–got, hang–hung, make–made, split–split.
  2. Harvest the irregular pasts onto the board next to each verb: come–CAME round, fall–FELL out, hang–HUNG out… Free grammar — take it now, thank me in the test.
  3. Call-and-response drill (TB cooler): TB cooler: a call-and-response drill — you say the verb, the class shouts the particle (get…? — ON!); you read a definition, the class shouts the phrasal verb. Sixty seconds, full volume.
  4. ex.2 solo, 3 minutes, pair check. Key: 1 get on, make up · 2 hang out · 3 split up · 4 look after · 5 get together, come round · 6 fall out.
  5. ex.3 in pairs: Yes/No questions with the verbs — Do you get on with your sister? Do you ever fall out with your friends? Two questions each way.
📺 On screen: the eight verbs and their pasts land on the board as the match resolves — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: match, drill, choose, ask. Expect make up to be «поставить макияж» for someone — enjoy the moment and anchor: make up = become friends again. Typical error: they felt out — fell/felt swap

WHAT WE DO: ex.4: rebuild the scattered ten-sentence story (A p.121 / B p.126)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: read the exchange as readers, then find the four answers

Email anatomy
pairs
Why: The Prepare-to-write box turns a nice reply into a checklist: greeting, ALL questions answered, advice phrases, closing. The question-counting drill on Sam's email installs the reflex that saves the content mark.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4: the Prepare-to-write toolkit, harvested and drilled

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3: hunt Nicole's advice phrases and her opening/closing. Key: advice phrases in Nicole's email: Remember to… · You should… · it's a good idea to…; opening Hi Ana, closing Bye for now!.
  2. Harvest the toolkit onto the board in three baskets: GREETING (How's it going? · It's great to hear from you) · ADVICE (You should… · Remember to… · It's a good idea to…) · CLOSING (I'm really looking forward to… · I can't wait to…).
  3. ex.4 fast, solo, 2 minutes, pair check. Key: 1 a good idea · 2 remember to · 3 to hear from you · 4 wait to see you again · 5 should.
  4. Mini-drill: Say «I can't wait to see you!» like you mean it. Choral, once, big.
📺 On screen: three phrase-baskets in chalk — the board is the medium and all three stay up through the writing stage, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: hunt, harvest, complete, drill. Expect looking forward to + -ing to raise a hand — confirm the pattern and move on (it is in the basket to copy). Typical error: I look forward to see you
SB p.45 ex.3–4 · board
48–54′
Planning · the four answers
solo
Why: Counting and answering Sam's four questions in note form BEFORE writing kills the number-one email error (a skipped question) at the planning stage, where it is cheap to fix.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5: count Sam's questions (the trap), then answer all of them in notes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.5, books open: Read Sam's email. How many questions does he ask? Count. Vote: three — or four? Take the vote BEFORE revealing. Key: Sam asks FOUR questions — including How are you? (the trap: How are you? counts and must be answered; most students say three).
  2. Land the trap: How are you? is a question. Skip it and your friend thinks you don't care — and an examiner counts it missing.
  3. Planning, silence, 4 minutes: four questions, four note-lines — weather? bring? gift? and how ARE you? One line each, notes not sentences.
  4. Walk and unstick: anyone stuck on the gift line gets a menu, not an answer — sweets? a mug? something from your town?
📺 On screen: the four question-lines in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: count, vote, get caught, plan. Expect most of the room to vote «three» — the collective miss is the memory hook. Typical problem: planning in full sentences — notes only, the sentences come next

🎯 Writing out · my reply
solo, silent
Why: The productive outcome in writing: a real ~100-word reply, built on a model, a plan and a checklist. Started AND finished in class.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6: write the reply — about 100 words, checklist on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Eighteen minutes. About 100 words. Four answers, the baskets are on the board, the checklist too. Go.
  2. Visible timer on. First five minutes: questions allowed in a whisper. After that — silent mode, palm down.
  3. Walk the room with a pencil: underline (don't fix) one spelling and one dropped answer per draft as you pass.
  4. Minute ten, quiet announcement: Checkpoint: count your answers. Four? Carry on. Three? You know which one is hiding.
  5. Fast finishers: swap with another fast finisher and answer ONE of Sam's questions differently — one better sentence.
  6. Your own model in reserve if anyone needs to SEE the bar: TB model answer: «Hi Sam, I'm fine, thanks. How are you? It's great that you're coming to visit Italy. It's warm here in July, so you should bring summer clothes. Remember to bring your swimming things… You should also bring some money… How about some English sweets or biscuits?… I can't wait to see you again. Francesca»
📺 On screen: visible timer counting down 18 minutes (Backup: write the end time in the corner of the board and update every five minutes by hand)
👦 Students do: write, answer all four, hit ~100 words. Expect the gift question to produce the most personality — good, that is communicative achievement. Typical error: no closing — the checklist's box five catches it at review

Why: A checklist swap turns every student into an examiner: they count each other's answered questions faster than they count their own — both sides learn.

WHAT WE DO: checklist swap: every reply gets a real examiner

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

  3. Ask for volunteers: Whose partner answered the gift question best? Read two gift answers aloud.

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«They fell out» — did they fight with fists? → no: they argued and stopped being friendly. «They made up» — friends again? → yes.
«Come round tonight!» — where do I go? → to their HOME; come round = visit someone at home.
Sam writes: How are you? What's the weather like? What should I bring? What would your family like? — how many questions must my reply answer? → FOUR. How are you? counts.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The particle drops («they fell», «we hung»)Drill call-and-response from the TB cooler: you say the verb, the class shouts the particle — get…? ON! fall…? OUT! Sixty seconds, twice in the lesson.
How are you? not counted as a questionMake the discovery theirs: let the class vote three-or-four BEFORE you reveal. The wrong vote is the memory hook.
Replies arrive without a closing (they just stop)The checklist on the board has five boxes and box five is CLOSING; peer reviewers are told to check box five first — it is the quickest catch.
Fast finishers at minute ten of the writing stageTB-style fast-finisher: swap replies and answer ONE of Sam's questions differently — one sentence, better than the original if you can.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
