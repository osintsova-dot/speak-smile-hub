🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use twelve adjectives of feeling and quality (annoyed, charming, curious, delighted, lonely, mad about, nasty, professional, rude, shy, stressful, unexpected) and the structure have something done for the things somebody else does for you: I had my hair cut.
What is genuinely hard
Have something done has no Russian equivalent. Russian says «я подстригся» — one verb, and the person who did the work disappears into it. English keeps three parts in a fixed order and the order is the meaning: have + OBJECT + PAST PARTICIPLE. Get the order wrong and you have said you did it yourself. The vocabulary has its own trap: two of the twelve words describe SITUATIONS, not people, and a candidate who writes «I was stressful» has said something quite different.
Typical error to expect
«I cut my hair yesterday» for a haircut at a hairdresser's, «I did my car repaired» — English marks the difference between doing it and paying someone: have + object + PAST PARTICIPLE, in that order. Russian has one verb for both, so the structure is learned as a pattern.
«I was stressful», «he is unexpected» — stressful and unexpected describe situations. People are stressed and surprised.
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary arrives through five real speakers arguing about celebrities, so the twelve adjectives are attached to opinions from the first minute rather than to a wordlist. They are then sorted twice — positive against negative, and people against situations — because both sorts are exactly what a lexical-choice exam item tests. The reading is six short true stories about famous people doing decent things, which is the rare celebrity text a teenager will actually finish, and it hands the grammar over ready-made: a celebrity has everything done for them. The productive stage is a celebrity-day fantasy that cannot be spoken without the structure, and the correction focus is one thing only: the past participle.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“A seventeen-year-old found a wallet with a lot of money in it. It belonged to Thor — the actor Chris Hemsworth. He gave it back on live television, and Hemsworth gave him everything inside it, and more. Today: twelve words for how people feel — and what you say when somebody else does something for you.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, marked articles returned with one comment each, and the celebrity guessing warmer

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what was the last message you sent today? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 2 minutes — the articles come back, marked on the four categories. By name, with one sentence each. Do not read any of them aloud unless the writer has agreed in advance.
  3. 1 minute — Kagan Think-Pair-Share: read your partner the ONE category comment you got and say what you would change first. Thirty seconds each way.
  4. Then the warmer, and it opens the unit in three minutes flat: teams of three choose a SECRET celebrity and write three sentences about them — where they are from, who they are married to, what they are famous for. Teams read; the class guesses.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the unit: Page one hundred and two. Unit eighteen. Five people are about to disagree with each other about famous people, and one of them is going to sound exactly like you.
📺 On screen: the marked articles handed back by name — paper only, and the papers are theirs
👦 Students do: read one piece of feedback aloud to a partner, then build and solve three celebrity riddles in teams

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and put up the three coloured boxes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Reset the wall for Unit 18: two columns, POSITIVE · NEGATIVE, and a small box to one side headed SITUATIONS, NOT PEOPLE. That box will hold exactly two words by the end of the hour, and telling them so now makes them listen for it.
  2. Then draw the three boxes and leave them empty: HAVE · OBJECT · PAST PARTICIPLE. Different colours if you have them. No explanation yet.

Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer on the twelve adjectives

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. A line down the middle of the room: POSITIVE at one end, NEGATIVE at the other, and the middle allowed but expensive — anyone standing in the middle has to justify it.
  2. You call an adjective, everybody walks. Then one person says a sentence with it about a real situation, not a definition.
  3. Eight adjectives, eight walks. On the last two, call stressful and unexpected and ask the extra question: Person or situation?
  4. Freeze and sit: Page one hundred and three. Six famous people, six true stories, and every one of them is about somebody doing something they did not have to do.
📺 On screen: the two end labels only — chalk and paper, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone commits with their body eight times and a quarter of the room produces a real sentence each round

Why: Grammar · have something done

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–4: the structure derived, then repaired, then produced

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Warmer, books closed, ninety seconds. Write the jumbled words on the board: has / Renata / cut / hair / every / her / month. Pairs build the sentence. Then two questions: Did she cut her hair? Who cut her hair? Underline has and cut.
  2. ex.1 as a class: the two sentences on the page, the actions underlined, and who does the action in each. She washes — the owner. She has her car washed — somebody at a garage, and we do not know who.
  3. Now fill the three coloured boxes on the board from that sentence: HAVE / her car / WASHED. Ask the form question directly — How do you make it? — and get the answer said by a student, not by you.
  4. Elicit three more from real life: have food delivered, have your nails painted, have a photo taken.
  5. ex.2, three minutes: six sentence halves matched. Point out that it works on collocation — which nouns go with which verbs. Answers: 1 c, 2 f, 3 d, 4 a, 5 b, 6 e.
  6. ex.3 as a class, two minutes: the second use. Read the two crime sentences and check victim. Answer: victims of crime. Add a fourth box on the board labelled BAD THINGS TOO so the class stops thinking the structure means luxury.
  7. ex.4 in mixed-ability groups, five minutes, Kagan RallyCoach: six sentences to correct. Weaker students take three or four of them — the Teacher's Book says so. Answers in full: have their car serviced · have their hair cut · had my bike stolen · could take a photo · to have some photographs taken · had some work done.
  8. Grammar reference is SB p.164 for anyone who wants it in writing.
📺 On screen: the three coloured boxes filling up, plus the fourth box for the crime meaning — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: build the rule from a jumbled sentence, match six halves, then coach a partner through six repairs

🎯 Speaking out · a day as a celebrity
pairs → class
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · a day as a celebrity

WHAT WE DO: ex.5 turned into a task that cannot be done without the structure

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two lists on the board, and everybody writes both: FIVE things I have already had done (hair cut, tooth filled, photo taken, bike repaired, phone fixed) and FIVE things I would love to have done.
  2. Five minutes, silent, individually. Rule: every line contains have + object + participle, and the participle is underlined. Model one of your own on the board first, honestly.
  3. 🎯 Kagan Timed Pair Share, two minutes each way: read your ten. The listener asks one follow-up on each list — Where? Who did it? Why that one?
  4. Then the round that makes it a lesson, six minutes: in new pairs, describe ONE DAY as a famous person, from waking up to going to bed, using at least six have-something-done sentences. The partner counts them on their fingers and says the number out loud at the end.
  5. Delayed correction, three minutes: five participles you heard wrong, on the board, repaired by the class with all three forms said aloud each time.
  6. Close with the count: Everybody in this room has just produced at least sixteen sentences in a structure that does not exist in Russian. That is not vocabulary — that is a new way of saying who did what.
📺 On screen: the two headed lists on the board with the three boxes underneath — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: write ten personal sentences, defend them in pairs, then improvise a celebrity day with a partner counting the structures

Form
have + OBJECT + past participle, in that order · any tense you like on have (has, had, is having, is going to have, may have) · the doer is optional and comes last with by.
Pron. watch
the four the Teacher's Book flags: annoyed /əˈnɔɪd/ · curious /ˈkjʊəriəs/ — three syllables, not two · professional /prəˈfeʃənəl/ · unexpected /ˌʌnɪkˈspektɪd/ with the stress on PEC. Add the one it does not flag: in have something done the stress falls on the PARTICIPLE — I had my hair CUT — which is how a listener hears that it was not you who did it
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«She has her car washed at a garage.» Who washes it? → not her. That is the whole structure in one question.
«I had my bike stolen.» Did I arrange that? → no. Same structure, and the second meaning: victims of crime.
«I was stressful yesterday» — possible? → no. The exam was stressful; I was stressed. Two of today's twelve words describe situations only.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«I had cut my hair» — the past perfect instead of the structureTwo lines on the board, one under the other, both said aloud: I HAD CUT my hair (I did it) against I HAD MY HAIR CUT (someone else did). Then the three coloured boxes. Point, do not explain.
The wrong participle: «had my bike repair», «have my photo take»The third form is the load-bearing word. Every check in this lesson is said with all three forms aloud — take, took, taken — the way the past perfect was drilled in block 6.
The twelve adjectives get used for people and situations alikeThe SITUATIONS, NOT PEOPLE box on the wall holds exactly two words all lesson. Any student who says «I was stressful» is pointed at the box, not corrected in words.
Fast finishers in the readingThe Teacher's Book task: answer the six questions from MEMORY before reading again to check. It is harder than it sounds and it keeps them in the text.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 32–38 (lexical choice — feelings and qualities are a standing field) and tasks 19–25.

Twelve adjectives of feeling look like vocabulary and behave like grammar: half of them are positive, half negative, and two of them (stressful, unexpected) describe SITUATIONS and not people, which is precisely the distinction a lexical-choice item is built on. Have something done has no Russian equivalent at all — Russian says «я постригся», one verb, and English needs three words in a fixed order, so the structure has to be taught as a pattern, not translated.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
