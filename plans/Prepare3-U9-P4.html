🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Match seven statements to three short reviews when none of the words are repeated, and write a story from three pictures — 35 words or more, every picture mentioned, joined with five linking words.
What is genuinely hard
In the reading: the right answer is always a paraphrase, so the habit of hunting for the same word actively produces wrong answers. In the writing: holding one tense from the first word to the last, and not losing the middle picture.
Typical error to expect
They pick the option that repeats a word from the text. The correct option is almost always paraphrased; the wrong ones copy the wording [exam report]
A story that starts in the past and slides into the present halfway through. One tense from the first word to the last [exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
The reading half comes first because it needs fresh heads and because the three reviews are where the vocabulary of the writing half comes from. The distractor stage after it is not optional: at this level choosing the right review is close to guessing, and saying why the other two do not fit is what turns it into reading. The story is built ORALLY before a word is written — that single move is what stops the middle picture disappearing, which is the most common way to lose marks in this task. The model answer is shown only after the peer check, because a model shown early is copied and a model shown late is a standard. Five linking words are sorted out before the writing starts, each with its own job named.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Three people, three books, seven sentences — and not one of the seven uses the same words as the review it belongs to. That is on purpose. Then you write a story: three pictures, thirty-five words, one tense. The most common way to lose marks is to forget the middle picture, so we are going to build it out loud before anybody writes anything.”

0–5′
Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a spoken round of the If game, and the bridge into the reading

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — the If game again, out loud this time (TB p.205). Groups of four. One student says a conditional sentence, the next continues from its second half, round the group twice.
  3. No writing, no preparation. If a chain breaks, the group starts a new one.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page one hundred and four. Three people, three funny books — and by the end of today you write a story of your own.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice and the two-box diagram redrawn in chalk — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: build spoken chains in fours. Expect this to be much faster than the written version last lesson and to catch the two or three students who still put will in the first half

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard the story has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write on the board, one under the other: «It didn't take her long to read the book.» and «I read it in just two days.»
  2. Same meaning. Not one word the same. That is how today's reading works — and it is why looking for the same word gives you the wrong answer.
  3. Then the second half of the lesson, in three lines: three pictures · thirty-five words · one tense. Write them up and leave them.
  4. Set the bar: A picture you write nothing about is a lost mark. Everybody's story mentions all three.
📺 On screen: the two paraphrased sentences and the three-line standard in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: read the two sentences and see that they mean the same thing. Expect somebody to say «but they're completely different» — that is the correct reaction and the whole point

8–14′
Lead-in · judging a book by its cover
pairs
Why: Three book covers and a guess. It is thirty seconds of prediction that makes the reading a check rather than a chore, and it plants the word cover before it is taught.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.104: three covers, three guesses, then read fast to check

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open at the three covers only. Do not read yet. Look at the covers and the titles. What is each book about? Two minutes in pairs.
  2. Take three guesses aloud and write them on the board — they will be checked in four minutes, which is what makes reading worth doing.
  3. Ask the TB p.205 question: Do you prefer books about serious topics or books which are funny? Thirty seconds, three answers.
  4. Then the time limit: Three minutes. Read all three. Were you right? Key: A a girl who has to help her family get some paintings back · B a boy who wants to start a rock band · C a terrible girl who has to learn to be a better person.
📺 On screen: the three book covers on the screen, enlarged, with the text hidden (Backup: the covers are printed on SB p.104 — students cover the text with a sheet of paper and look only at the pictures)
👦 Students do: guess from a cover, then check. Expect Heidi's book to be guessed wrong by almost everybody, which is the point her review actually makes. Typical problem: they start reading straight away — the covering sheet is the fix

14–26′
Reading · three funny books
solo
Why: Three teenagers reviewing three books. Multiple matching cannot be done by finding a repeated word, because the questions are all paraphrases — which is precisely the point.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.104 (track 133): seven statements matched to Natalie, Heidi or Davina

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Name the strategy before they start: TB p.205: read each question in turn and find the matching information in one of the three texts — the match will be a synonym or a paraphrase, never the same words. Do not sit on a hard question: move on and come back. And guess if you must, because nothing is lost for a wrong answer. About ten minutes is the right length for this task.
  2. Three comprehension questions first, so nobody is matching a text they have not understood (TB p.205): How did Natalie feel when she finished her book? (sad) Is Heidi's book for children or teenagers? (teenagers) What does the girl in Davina's book need to do? (become a better person).
  3. ex.2 alone, in silence, 8 minutes — the TB's recommended length for this task. Key: 1 C · 2 A · 3 C · 4 B · 5 C · 6 B · 7 A.
  4. Play track 133 afterwards while they check their own answers against the text.
  5. For your own reference, the three reviews: SB p.104, three teenagers review a funny book. A Natalie — her mum bought her Heist Society by Ally Carter for her birthday; she knew from the cover and the title that she would love it, read it in two days and was sad when it ended; it is about a girl who helps her family get some stolen paintings back, and Natalie now has all the author's other books ready for the summer. B Heidi — Matthew Meets the Man by Travis Nichols, about a 15-year-old who wants to start a rock band while every adult in his life tries to stop him; the writer is also an artist and the drawings on each page carry the story, but the cover makes it look like a book for little children, which may stop teenagers picking it up. C Davina — Firecracker by David Iserson, bought cheaply in her local shop; a funny story about a terrible girl who has to learn to be a better person. It is the first time she has tried this kind of book; she was not happy with the last few pages but enjoyed it a lot and will read more like it.
📺 On screen: track 133 in the board app and the three reviews on the screen (Backup: the reviews are printed on SB p.104 and the recording is only a second pass — read one of the three aloud yourself if you want the listening exposure)
👦 Students do: match seven statements in silence against a clock. Expect 5 out of 7. Typical error: questions 3 and 5 both go to Davina, because both are about buying and about being different — only one of them is

26–34′
Reading · why the other two are wrong
pairs → class
Why: The same move as the listening lesson, on paper: for each answer, prove it from the text and say why the other two reviewers do not fit. This is where the strategy becomes a habit.

WHAT WE DO: the distractor analysis: prove each answer and eliminate the other two reviewers

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Numbered Heads Together: each pair takes one statement and must produce two sentences — It's C because … and it isn't A because …
  2. Both sentences have to quote the review. A paraphrase in their own words is not evidence at this stage; the words on the page are.
  3. Do question 7 with the whole class on the board, side by side: didn't take her long against I read it in just two days. Then ask them to find the pair for question 4 themselves (the pictures inside were excellent / the clever drawings on each page help the story along).
  4. Say the rule they take away and have three students repeat it: The right answer says it differently. The wrong answers say it in the same words.
📺 On screen: the three reviews back on the screen with the seven statements beside them, one at a time (Backup: everything is on SB p.104 — pairs read out their evidence line from the book and you write the paraphrase pairs on the board)
👦 Students do: prove and eliminate. Expect this to take longer than the matching itself, which is correct. Typical error: «because it says so» — ask which words, every time

Vocabulary · words about books
pairs → class
Why: Nine words that let a teenager say something about a book beyond «good». They are learnt from definitions and used within four minutes in questions about the students' own reading.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.104: nine words found in the reviews, then used about their own reading

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed, elicit three (TB p.205): Books have hundreds of … (pages) Your ___ is what you think about a book. (opinion) The name of a book is its ___. (title).
  2. Books open: underline the nine words in the three reviews, then match them to the definitions, 4 minutes. Key: 1 end · 2 cover · 3 title · 4 drawings · 5 shelf · 6 pages · 7 author · 8 opinion · 9 chapter.
  3. ex.2, five questions to complete and then ask each other, 4 minutes. Key: 1 opinion(s) · 2 drawings · 3 title, cover (in any order) · 4 pages · 5 end.
  4. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with the nine words on cards, 2 minutes, four trades each.
  5. Fast finishers: TB p.202: fast finishers write Kat's reply to Amber's message — Amber, I'm glad you liked the book! It sounds great but I'm getting ready for my exam. Can I borrow it in the summer? Kat. TB p.205: fast finishers invent a chapter heading for each of the three books and the class guesses which book it belongs to.
📺 On screen: the nine definitions on the screen, revealed one at a time (Backup: they are printed on SB p.104 — cover them with a ruler and slide it down; the trading cards are printed in advance)
👦 Students do: underline, match, then ask each other. Expect drawings and cover to be the two that are already known and chapter to be the one nobody has. Typical error: author confused with writer — they are the same, say so and move on

Move break
standing
Why: A stirrer that also forces one English sentence. Teenagers who have been sitting for forty minutes need to stand before the grammar block, or the grammar block dies.

WHAT WE DO: two minutes of movement before the listening and the writing

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. Shake out your hands. Now: one book each, out loud, round the room — the title only, in any language. No repeats. Go.
  2. Sit down on a clap and pens down — the next stage is ears only.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: stand and name one book each. Expect the room to be surprised by how many they can name. Typical error: two students name the same book — let it go, it is a break

46–56′
Listening · the reading competition
solo → pairs
Why: Right/wrong statements with the wrong ones corrected — the sub-skill is holding what was actually said apart from what sounds plausible. Rephrasing before listening is taught here as a strategy in its own right.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.105 (track 134): a teacher talks about a competition, then eight right/wrong statements

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 first: Is he talking about a new book, a competition, a website or a writer? Play track 134 once. Key: He is talking about a competition and a website..
  2. Before the second listening, the strategy: TB p.206: before the second listening, have the class rephrase the statements in their own words — Only young children can do the competition. The person who reads the most books wins. Rephrasing before listening is a strategy that works in reading too, and it is worth naming as one.
  3. Demonstrate the correction move on the board (TB p.206): The teacher has organised this competition. — wrong, and the correction is «it's from a website», not just «no».
  4. Play track 134 again. Eight statements, and every wrong one has to be repaired. Key: 1 ✗ — it is open to anyone aged 12–15 · 2 ✓ · 3 ✗ — you will be able to borrow all the books · 4 ✗ — the instructions are on the website · 5 ✓ · 6 ✓ · 7 ✓ · 8 ✗ — it is written by writers and book companies.
  5. Check with Kagan Showdown — tick or cross on the mini-whiteboard, all boards up at once, and the repair said out loud by whoever raised a cross.
  6. Name the trap once at the end: Where marks go: statement 8. The word teenagers is said out loud in the recording, just not about the blog — a classic case of a heard word that is not an answer.
📺 On screen: track 134 in the board app (Backup: the audioscript is on TB p.303 — read it yourself, twice, at natural speed; the eight statements are printed in the students' own book)
👦 Students do: listen twice and repair the wrong statements. Expect 5 or 6 out of 8, and the repairs to be much harder than the ticks. Typical error: statement 8 — the word teenagers is in the recording, just not about the blog

Prepare to write · linking words
class → pairs
Why: The model is not a text to copy but a source of joins. Five linking words, each with a different job, sorted out before anybody writes a story — which is what stops five sentences all starting «Then».

WHAT WE DO: GET READY p.105: the worked example about Tom and the bus, and five linking words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Look at the three pictures of Tom at the bus stop. Describe each one. Who? Where? What is happening? Two minutes, whole class.
  2. Then the three sentences to put in order. The correct order is c, a, b.
  3. Now the five linking words, each with its job named. TB p.206, five questions to ask before they fill the gaps: Which word gives a reason? (because) Which word joins parts of a sentence? (and) Which word says what happens because of something else? (so) Which word shows a contrast? (but) Which word says that something bad happened? (unfortunately).
  4. The gap-fill that follows, in pairs, 3 minutes. Key: 1 because · 2 so (and is also possible) · 3 but · 4 Unfortunately · 5 and.
  5. Write the five words on the board in a row and leave them there for the writing stage: so · unfortunately · and · but · because.
  6. And the structural rule, in one line: TB p.206: the way to organise it is three sections, one per picture. Then check the finished text for grammar, vocabulary, spelling and punctuation — in that order, and separately.
📺 On screen: the three pictures and the three jumbled sentences on the screen (Backup: everything is printed on SB p.105 — work from the book and write the five linking words on the board, which is where they need to be for the next stage anyway)
👦 Students do: order the sentences, then sort out five joins. Expect so and because to be swapped by two or three students. Typical error: unfortunately put in the middle of a clause — show them it starts a sentence

64–78′
🎯 Writing out · the three-picture story
class → solo, silent
Why: The productive outcome: a story built orally, then written alone against the clock. Built first, because a story that is planned out loud does not lose its middle picture.

WHAT WE DO: PLAN and WRITE p.105: the story on SB p.139, built out loud first, then written alone

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Open SB p.139 — the three pictures they actually write about. Build the story ORALLY with the four TB p.206 questions, no writing at all, 4 minutes: SB p.139, the pictures for the story they actually write: Ali goes to the library, chooses far too many books, drops them all on the floor, and a friend helps her pick them up. TB p.206 says to elicit the vocabulary first with four questions: Where is Ali? (in the library) What did she want to do? (take out lots of books) What happened to them? (they fell on the floor) How did her friend help? (she picked them up).
  2. Two minutes on the past endings while the story is still spoken: wanted /ɪd/ · helped /t/ · carried /d/. Say each, class repeats, then all three in one breath.
  3. Point at the five linking words on the board and set the target: At least three of these five in your story.
  4. WRITE: 7 minutes, silent mode, alone. Something about every picture. Past tense from the first word to the last. Thirty-five words or more.
  5. Two minutes before the end: Underline every verb. Is every one of them past? Count the pictures. Three?
📺 On screen: the three pictures from SB p.139 on the screen and a 7-minute timer (Backup: the pictures are printed on SB p.139 — keep the book open at that page and time it on the wall clock, writing «3 minutes left» on the board rather than saying it)
👦 Students do: build the story out loud, then write in silence. Expect 40–60 words from most. Typical error: the librarian disappears from the middle picture — the numbered check at the end is where they catch it themselves

78–86′
Improve · swap and repair
pairs → class
Why: Peer repair against a three-item checklist, and only then the model. Showing the model too early turns writing into copying; showing it after the check turns it into a standard.

WHAT WE DO: IMPROVE p.105: peer check against three items, and only then the model answer

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap papers. Kagan RallyCoach with a fixed checklist: (1) is there something about all three pictures? (2) is every verb past? (3) how many linking words — write the number in the margin.
  2. The rule of the stage: You may not rewrite your partner's story. You may only ask a question about it.
  3. Only NOW show the model answer: Model answer, TB p.206: «Yesterday, Ali went to the library because she wanted to take out some books to read in the holidays. She chose lots of books and unfortunately she couldn't carry them all, so they fell onto the floor. The librarian was angry but Ali's friend came to help her pick them all up again.»
  4. One question about the model, to the class: How many linking words are in it? (three: because, unfortunately, so — plus but.) And which sentence covers the middle picture?
  5. Students add one linking word and one adjective to their own story in a different colour. That is the whole improvement.
  6. Say the trap once, at the end, and leave it: Where marks go: the middle picture disappears, and the tense slides from past to present halfway down. Both are caught by the same two-minute check at the end — underline every verb, count the pictures.
📺 On screen: the model answer on the screen, revealed only at this point (Backup: write the model answer on the board while the pairs are checking and cover it with a sheet of paper until they finish)
👦 Students do: check a partner's story against three items, then improve their own. Expect the linking-word count to be two and the model to have four. Typical error: they correct spelling instead of asking about content — the rule of the stage is the answer

Structure
Yesterday, Ali went to the library because she wanted to take out some books. · She chose lots of books and unfortunately she couldn't carry them all, so they fell onto the floor. · The librarian was angry but Ali's friend came to help her.
Form
past simple throughout the story; linking words so · unfortunately · and · but · because, each with its own job. 35 words or more means «or more» is fine.
Pron. watch
the regular past endings inside the writing stage, two minutes, no more: wanted /ɪd/ · helped /t/ · carried /d/. Plus the stress in unˈfortunately, which is five syllables and gets flattened.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Write 35 words or more.» Is 30 enough? → no. Is 60 a problem? → no: «or more» means more is fine.
Three pictures. How many of them must be in the story? → all three. A picture with nothing about it is a lost mark.
«Who says it didn't take her long to read the book?» Will the review contain the words «didn't take long»? → almost certainly not. It will say something like «in just two days».
Which linking word says that something bad happened? → unfortunately.
Which one gives a reason? → because. And which one gives the result? → so.
The story starts «Yesterday». What tense is the last sentence in? → the past, still.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
They match by finding the same word and get three answers wrongPredictable, and it is the lesson. After the check, take question 7 and put the two phrasings side by side on the board: didn't take her long — I read it in just two days. Then ask them to find the pair for question 4 themselves.
The story loses the middle pictureThis is why the story is built out loud first. If it still happens, the two-minute check at the end catches it: Count the pictures in your story. Three? Make it a numbered check, not a general instruction.
The tense slides into the present halfway downTwo colours. They underline every verb in their own draft, and anything that is not past goes in the second colour. Nothing else needs saying.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
