🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use thirteen words for the built and the natural world (facilities, health centre, historic buildings, modern architecture, air conditioning, street lights, open spaces, pollution, ruins, wildlife, valley, seasons, bugs) and read two parallel texts for detail, deciding which of two people each statement belongs to.
What is genuinely hard
Most of these are two-word phrases with one main stress and no logic to reason from: air conditioning is not conditioned air, facilities is not what a Russian speaker expects from «facility», and open spaces is a fixed pair. The reading is hard in a specific way — the two texts are ABOUT the same thing (where I live), so the answer to «who mentions this?» is decided by one detail, not by the topic.
Typical error to expect
«I went to cinema», «she is doctor», «in city where I live» — Russian has no articles at all, so they are not forgotten, they were never there. Every English noun needs a decision: a, the or nothing — and the decision is made about a hundred times in a hundred-word email.
Words that arrive wrong: «facilities» pronounced with the stress on the first syllable, «nature» used where the English word is wildlife, and «ruins» treated as a singular.
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary arrives inside a personality quiz, which is the only reason a class of sixteen-year-olds will read twelve target words closely: they are reading to find out what kind of person they are. The words are then sorted into built and natural, which is the concept behind the whole unit, and heard in three teenagers describing where they live. The reading is two Australians — a girl in Sydney who catches a ferry to school, and a boy born on a cattle station the size of a small country who did not go to school until he was twelve — and the parallel-text format is exactly what makes proof-underlining necessary. The productive stage turns the words back on the students' own town.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“One of today's teenagers lives on a farm of seven thousand square kilometres and didn't go to school until he was twelve — he studied by radio and internet. Today: the English for what a place is made of, and two people who both think they live in the best one.”

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that forces a choice and a target word

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two ends of the room: CITY and COUNTRY.
  2. You read a statement and everybody walks: I would rather have good facilities than open spaces. · I could live somewhere with no street lights. · Pollution is the worst thing about cities. · I would miss the wildlife.

  4. Four statements, four walks, eight sentences. Then freeze and sit: Page eighty-seven. Two Australians. One of them lives on a farm bigger than some countries.

WHAT WE DO: the three discussion questions from the page, with the wall in reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the three questions: Would you prefer to live where Harry lives or where Maddie lives? Why do most young people prefer to live in cities? What things make a place enjoyable to live in? — pairs first, then the floor
  2. Kagan RallyRobin first, two minutes: partners alternate single reasons, no speeches.
  3. Then groups of four, four minutes, with one rule: every turn uses a word from the wall. The wall is right there and it is meant to be used.
  4. Bring it to the floor for three minutes and go for the disagreement, not the consensus: ask the strongest argument AGAINST the majority answer to question 2.
  5. Add the class's own vote from the opening back in: Eleven of you said city. Would Harry change your mind?
📺 On screen: the word wall and the opening vote still on the board — nothing new is displayed
👦 Students do: speak in strict alternation first, then argue in fours with a compulsory target word in every turn

WHAT WE DO: a productive task that cannot be done without the thirteen words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The task, on the board: Describe the place where you live to somebody who has never been there. Six sentences, six different words from the wall — three from BUILT and three from NATURAL.
  2. Four minutes to write, alone and silent. Norilsk makes this task better, not worse: the words for what is missing are on the wall too.
  3. Kagan Timed Pair Share, 90 seconds each way: the listener counts the words on their fingers and asks ONE follow-up question at the end.
  4. Change partners once and repeat. The second telling is the better English, which is why it is done twice.
  5. Four to the floor. Then the closing question, thirty seconds in pairs: What is the ONE thing your town needs that it hasn't got? Collect the answers on the board as a list — it is the unit's own data.
  6. Deferred correction: five errors from your monitoring, unattributed, repaired by the class in the last minute.

Structure
There are plenty of open spaces to be in. · There are no street lights and no pollution. · The city has excellent facilities. · It's the best of both worlds.
Form
two-word noun phrases with ONE stress: AIR conditioning, STREET lights, HEALTH centre, Open spaces · plenty of / quite a lot of + uncountable or plural noun · there is / there are with these nouns, which is how a place gets described.
Pron. watch
the compound phrases carry ONE main stress and the class will give them two: AIR conditioning · STREET lights · HEALTH centre · MODern architecture · hisTORic buildings · Open spaces · faCILities /fəˈsɪlətiz/, which is also the hardest of the twelve to say · and the article the has two pronunciations that nobody teaches: /ðə/ before a consonant, /ðiː/ before a vowel — the city, but /ðiː/ open spaces
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Facilities» — is a park a facility? → yes: buildings, equipment and services provided for a purpose. Is a mountain? → no, that is the natural world.
«Ruins» — one building or the broken parts of one? → the damaged parts that are left. Can I say a ruin? → yes, but the phrase you will meet is the ruins.
Harry says there are no street lights where he lives. Is that good or bad, in his opinion? → good — you can see a million stars. That is what a which-person question is made of.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The quiz becomes a personality chat and the words are lostThe quiz has a job: every option contains a target word, and the class reads them all before they answer. Two minutes for the quiz, two for the score, and then the words go on the wall — the timer, not your voice, does the enforcing.
Stress on the wrong part of the two-word phrasesEvery phrase goes on the wall with the stressed part in capitals, and every phrase is clapped once by the whole class as it is written. Thirty seconds each time, and it is the difference between sounding B1 and sounding beginner.
Which-person questions answered from the topicThe proof rule from block 7 holds: no letter without the underlined words in the text. Both texts are short enough to re-read, so there is no excuse.
«I've never been anywhere, I have nothing to say»The productive task is about the street they actually live on, not about travel — and the wall gives them the words. Anyone genuinely stuck describes the school instead.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 10 (matching headings to seven short texts) and tasks 32–38 (lexical choice).

Where you live is one of the standing topics of the Russian paper: it prints in the reading section, it is a speaking card, and it is an essay table almost every year. The twelve words that arrive today — facilities, open spaces, historic buildings, air conditioning, pollution, wildlife — are the difference between a candidate who says «there are many interesting places» and one who names them. The reading half trains the heading reflex: decide what the text is ABOUT, not which words it repeats.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
