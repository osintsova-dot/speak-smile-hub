🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Take part in a real discussion in threes: state an opinion, support it with an example, and react to what the other people say, using the Speaking bank phrases for adding emphasis (There's no doubt in my mind that… · I'm totally convinced that… · You can't deny that… · I really DO think that…) and giving examples (For instance… · Take …, for example · You only have to think of… · Just to give you an idea…).
What is genuinely hard
Teenagers at B2 can produce an opinion; what they cannot do is support it and react to somebody else's. Three students in a group usually produce three monologues in sequence — which is precisely what both the Cambridge and the Russian oral examiner mark down. The second difficulty is silence in the middle of a discussion: when a student has nothing to say they say nothing, instead of asking a question.
Typical error to expect
«I think it is good. It is interesting.» — an opinion with no example under it is a two-mark answer in both the oral and the essay. Every claim in this unit is followed by For instance… / Just to give you an idea… / You only have to think of…
Three monologues instead of a discussion. Nobody refers to anybody. The fix is mechanical and it works: the second speaker may not start until they have said whether they agree with the first.
Why the lesson is built this way
The article is short and genuinely arguable — film awards, and the claim that judges are too often old, white and male. The model discussion is heard TWICE with two different jobs: first for the arguments and the examples under them, then purely to hunt the Speaking bank phrases, which is the only way functional language gets noticed rather than read. Then the phrases are drilled for stress (the whole point of «I really DO think» is the stress on DO), prepared in note form, and used in two rounds of threes so that everybody speaks twice. Delayed correction comes at the end, from what you actually heard, not from a list.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“At the Sundance Festival recently, women film-makers took the top prizes. The person in charge of the voting was a famous female director. Coincidence? Today: you argue — in English, in threes, with examples — and we find out whether you can change anybody's mind.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, lesson-3 reporting verbs recycled, and four real examples collected for today's discussion

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: One sentence: what was the last thing you watched or listened to? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 2 minutes — rewind, woven into today. Kagan Think-Pair-Share: Report one thing you read or heard yesterday — use claimed, admitted, complained or warned. Thirty seconds each way. Lesson 3's verbs, alive, before they cool.
  3. 1 minute — the hook. Name a film that won a big award and did not deserve it. Or one that deserved it and got nothing. Take four titles, write them on the board, do not judge them. They become examples later.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page ninety-two. Are film awards fair? By the end of today you will have argued about it twice, in English, in threes.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2: a short opinion piece read for the argument, not for detail

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.2, three minutes, silent: read the article. One job — What exactly is the complaint?
  2. The argument in one line, elicited not given: there are fewer minority actors nominated for the top prizes, few women win the top film-making prize, and the reason may be that the judges and voters are often old, white and male — so they are not representative of the audience.
  3. Then the reaction, four minutes in pairs: Does this information surprise you? Why or why not? Insist on specifics — Which part surprised you? Name it.
  4. Take three reactions to the floor. Add the prompts from the Teacher's Book if the discussion is thin: Should judges deliberately give awards to women and non-white film-makers? Should half the judges be women? Should some judges be young? Do film awards actually matter at all?
  5. Keep the last question visible — it is the one that produces the best English.
📺 On screen: the four Teacher's Book prompts on the board for anyone who runs dry — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: read a short argument, then react to it specifically in pairs and to the class

Move break · the opinion line-up
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Sixteen-year-olds who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that turns opinions into a physical line

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. A line down the middle of the room: one end AGREE COMPLETELY, the other DISAGREE COMPLETELY, and everyone stands where they actually are.
  2. Statement one: Film awards are basically fair. Everyone places themselves. Then Kagan Mix-Pair-Share: turn to the person nearest you and say why you are standing there. Thirty seconds.
  3. Two more statements — Awards change what films get made. · Nobody actually cares who wins. Re-place, re-pair, re-explain.
  4. The rule that makes it work: you may not stand in the same place as last time without saying why.
  5. Freeze and sit: Now listen to two people your age arguing about exactly this.
📺 On screen: the three statements read aloud — no technology, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: every student physically commits three times and explains their position aloud three times

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: what are their arguments, and what examples do they put under them?

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the two questions before playing: Do the boy and the girl generally agree? What EXAMPLES do they use to support their arguments?
  2. Track 40, first play, whole. With a weaker class, do exactly what the Teacher's Book suggests: play it twice — the first time only for «do they agree?», the second for the examples.
  3. Build the answer on the board as a two-column table, argument on the left, example on the right. That table is the whole lesson in one image: award ceremonies ignore women → many women direct, few win; male directors are in the news, women film-makers are not · the situation is changing → at Sundance recently women took the top prizes · the business needs more diverse judges → the person in charge of voting at Sundance was a famous female director; most Academy voters are white and male.
  4. Ask the diagnostic question aloud: How many of their arguments have NO example under them? Not one of the three. Say why that matters.
  5. Kagan RallyRobin, ninety seconds: in pairs, take turns naming one argument and one example from the table, from memory, alternating.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4: hunt the Speaking bank phrases in the same recording, then drill the stress

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4: play track 40 again with ONE job — tick the Speaking bank phrases you actually hear. Books open at the Speaking bank box.
  2. Answers, and they use nearly all of them: Don't forget that… · There's no doubt in my mind that… · I really do think that… · I'm totally convinced that… · You can't deny that… · It's time that… · For instance,… · Look at… · You only have to think of… · …such as… · Just to give you an idea…
  3. Every phrase that was heard goes onto the wall strip, written by the student who caught it.
  4. Now the pronunciation, and it is not decoration — it is the meaning. Drill the emphatic do three times, chorally and then individually: I really DO think that… · I really DO want to see it. · I DO hope the film won't be slow. Say it flat first, deliberately, and ask what is lost.
  5. Then drill the four longest phrases for contour, twice each, at speed. Thirty seconds of choral work here saves the whole discussion stage.
  6. Grammar note, twenty seconds: It's time that… takes a PAST tense — it's time they changed the rules.
📺 On screen: the phrase strip on the wall, filling with phrases students caught themselves. Backup: if the audio is down, read eight of the phrases aloud inside invented sentences and have the class spot and classify them
👦 Students do: listen a third time with a single hunting job, then drill stress chorally and individually

Discussion · first round, in threes
groups of three
Why: Discussion · first round, in threes

WHAT WE DO: ex.5: the article itself argued, with the phrases in hand

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of three, counters out. Eight minutes total, and the topic is the article: Discuss your own opinions about the article in exercise 2. Give examples.
  2. Before they start, repeat the two rules and the counter system. Then the ICQ: Can you give an opinion without an example? — No. What do you do before you add your point? — React to the last one.
  3. Five minutes of discussion, timer visible. You circulate with a pen and paper collecting: two good uses of a phrase (with names) and three broken sentences (without names). That paper is the delayed correction later.
  4. Stop at five minutes, then two minutes of reflection in the same threes: How many phrases did your group actually use? Tick them on the strip.
  5. One minute of feedback to the floor: name the two students you heard using a phrase well and say exactly what they did. Public, specific, thirty seconds — it doubles uptake in round two.
📺 On screen: the phrase strip and the two rules, visible from every seat — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: all three students in every group speak; the counters make silence and monologue equally impossible

WHAT WE DO: ex.6: notes and examples for the three discussion topics, in English only

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.6, six minutes, silent, individually: for each of the three topics decide your opinion and note TWO arguments with an example under each. The three topics: Superhero films are ruining the cinema because they tend to be childish and silly. · To really enjoy a film you have to watch it in the cinema, not on a phone or tablet. · Film adaptations are never as good as the original book.
  2. Write the Teacher's Book planning questions on the board: Do I agree or disagree? Why? What examples can I give to support my opinion?
  3. The rule, and check it while circulating: notes, not sentences, and in English. A student writing full sentences will read them aloud later and the discussion dies.
  4. Four minutes in pairs, Kagan Timed Pair Share: try out ONE of your three topics on a partner for ninety seconds each way. It is a rehearsal, not the performance — say so.
  5. The third topic is the one to push on: adaptations. Everyone in the room has an example, and the vocabulary from lesson 1 (plot, screenplay, ending, character) fits it exactly.

🎯 Practice makes perfect · the real thing
groups of three → class
Why: 🎯 Practice makes perfect · the real thing

WHAT WE DO: ex.7a–7b: two full discussions in new threes, then the whole class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 🎯 New groups of three — different from round one, deliberately. Counters redistributed.
  2. Round A, five minutes: topics 1 and 2. Same two rules, timer visible, you circulate and collect. Do not intervene while they speak — write it down instead.
  3. Round B, five minutes, and rotate one person per group so everybody meets somebody new: topic 3, adaptations. This is the topic where the unit vocabulary should surface, and it will.
  4. ex.7b, three minutes, whole class: Did any group have similar ideas? Did anybody CHANGE somebody's mind? Take the two best exchanges to the floor and make the students replay them, briefly.
  5. Delayed correction, three minutes, from your collection sheet: three broken sentences on the board, unattributed, repaired by the class. Then the two good ones, WITH names, and say exactly what made them good.
  6. Re-take the opening vote — Are film awards fair? — and compare the two numbers on the board. If anything moved, that is the argument working, and it is worth naming.
📺 On screen: the two vote counts side by side on the board, and the unattributed sentences for repair — chalk and paper only
👦 Students do: every student holds two five-minute discussions in two different groups, and hears their own language repaired at the end

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class

  • add emphasis with six phrases, stressing them correctly

Structure
There's no doubt in my mind that the judges need to change. · You can't deny that things are improving. · I really DO think that awards matter. · Just to give you an idea, at Sundance the top prizes went to women. · It's time that the academy changed.
Form
emphatic do/does/did + bare infinitive, with the stress ON the auxiliary · It's time + subject + PAST tense (It's time they changed) · What about the case of …? · Take …, for example.
Pron. watch
the whole meaning of the emphatic structure lives in the stress: I really DO think that… · I really DO want to see it. Say it flat and it means nothing · the phrases themselves have a fixed contour — there's no DOUBT in my mind · I'm TOtally convinced · you can't deNY that · just to give you an iDEA
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I really do think that…» — do I doubt it? → no, the opposite: do makes it stronger. Which word do I stress? → do. Say it wrong and it disappears.
«It's time that the academy changed.» Has it changed? → no. Is this about the past? → no — the past tense here means «overdue».
ICQ before the discussion: Before you give your own opinion, what must you do? — Say whether you agree with the person before.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
One student dominates, two go quietThree counters each (paper squares, buttons, anything). You spend one every time you speak. When yours are gone you may only ask questions. It fixes the problem in one round without a word of scolding.
«I have no opinion about film awards»Legitimate, and useful. Give them the alternative role out loud: Then your job is to ask the hardest question you can think of. Nobody sits out.
The phrases stay on the page and never enter the speechThe strip on the wall with ticks after each round. Uptake has to be visible or it does not happen. Two minutes at the end of each round is enough.
Russian creeping in during preparationPreparation is in NOTES, not sentences, and notes are in English only — that is the rule and it is easy to check while circulating.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 39 and task 40 (the oral part: a discussion and an opinion turn) and, on paper, task 38 — the essay, which is built out of the same arguments.

A Russian oral answer loses marks for exactly the two things this lesson drills: an opinion with no example under it, and a turn that does not react to what the other person said. The Speaking bank phrases — I'm totally convinced that…, You can't deny that…, Just to give you an idea… — are the cheapest possible way to lift a flat answer into the top band, and they cost thirty seconds each to learn.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
