🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Talk about money precisely with fifteen B2 words (allowance, afford, purchase, receipt, refund, cashpoint, swipe, get into debt, budget, tax, value for money, bargain, the sales) and five idioms (snap up a bargain, cost an arm and a leg, tighten your belt, throw money down the drain), and read a 1,200-word article for specific information: ten questions, ten answers, each traceable to a line.
What is genuinely hard
Money vocabulary is a collocation problem, not a meaning problem: students know «money» and then say «I have no possibility to buy it» instead of «I can't afford it». Add two mechanical traps — money is uncountable (Russian «деньги» is plural) and receipt has a silent p that half the class will pronounce.
Typical error to expect
«monies», «a money», «I have many money» — money is uncountable: much money, a lot of money, an amount of money. Russian «деньги» is plural, which is the whole source of the error.
«I have no possibility to buy it» — a direct calque. English has one verb for it: I can't afford it. Teach it as a chunk and drill it three times, because it appears in every exam letter about money.
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary arrives inside a Culture exchange text about what American teenagers actually do with money — real figures, so the words are read as information rather than as a list. The reading is then a genuinely surprising article (Sweden going cashless, Peter the Great taxing beards, the English window tax), which gives the class something to argue about in the last stage: is a cashless country a good idea?
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
In some Swedish shops you cannot pay with cash at all — they won't take it. In Russia in 1698 you had to pay a tax to keep your beard, and you carried a copper token to prove you had paid. Today you get the fifteen words you need to talk about money like an adult, and then you read about both of those things and decide what you think.

WHAT WE DO: greeting at the door, then a comparison round on last unit's grammar

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

pairs → whole class
Why: Two minutes of competition wakes a December class faster than anything else, and both tasks are inside the topic rather than beside it.

WHAT WE DO: two fast competitive tasks that open the topic and show you what the class already has

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. Write eight countries on the board: Ireland · the US · Scotland · Finland · Japan · Switzerland · Mexico · the Vatican City. Pairs write the currency for each; hands up when you have all eight. (Euro · dollar · pound · euro · yen · Swiss franc · peso · euro — the Vatican is the one that surprises them.)
  2. SB p.58 exercise 1: two minutes, pairs list every kind of shop they can name. Who has more than ten? More than fifteen? The winning pair reads its list; the others tick off matches and then add anything the winners missed.
  3. Do not correct pronunciation yet. This stage is for energy and for diagnosis — you now know what the group already has.
📺 On screen: the eight countries on the board. Backup: dictate them; the stage is board-and-voice only.
👦 Students do: work in pairs against another pair, calling out and ticking off.

Vocabulary in context · the money words
desks
Why: Fifteen words in one text with real statistics beats fifteen words in a list, because the class reads for the figures and acquires the vocabulary on the way.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.58 exercises 2 and 3 (tracks 22 and 23) — the words, then the Culture exchange text about American teenagers

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 2: go through the box together first — elicit or explain each item, then play track 22. Drill receipt /rɪˈsiːt/ hard: write it, cross out the p in front of them, and have the class say it four times.
  2. Two culture notes worth thirty seconds each: ATM is American, cashpoint and cash machine are British. A bill in American English is a banknote — and the bill in a restaurant is the check.
  3. Exercise 3 — the Culture exchange text (track 23), seven minutes. Answers: the Culture exchange text (track 23): a spending habits · b spend, on · c afford · d purchases · e Value · f allowance · g cash · h payments · i swipe · j coins · k get into debt · l account · m tax · n budget. The figures in the text: $2,600 a year, 19% on clothes, 32% get an allowance for chores, 34% fear debt most, only 33% have ever made a budget.
  4. Mixed ability: for a weaker group, tell them which words belong to a–f and which to g–n, jumbled. For a stronger group, cover the box and do it as an open cloze first.
  5. Fast finishers: write these numbers on the board — 2,600 · 19 · 32 · 34 · 33 — and ask what each one refers to in the text.
  6. Check the figures with the whole class at the end; they are what make the words stick.
📺 On screen: recording — tracks 22 and 23 in the board app, both cued before the lesson. Backup: the class audio script is printed in the Teacher's Book (TB p.123); read the items aloud yourself at normal speed.
👦 Students do: listen, complete, compare in pairs, then hunt for what five numbers in the text refer to.

30–37′
Stirrer · the idiom auction
open floor
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.58 exercise 5 on the feet: five money idioms placed on a sensible–reckless line

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. Two ends of the room: SENSIBLE and RECKLESS.
  2. You call an idiom (tighten your belt! spend a fortune! snap up a bargain! throw money down the drain! cost an arm and a leg!); students walk to the end they believe in and must justify it in one sentence when pointed at.
  3. Meanings to confirm as you go: 1 snap up a bargain — find something good value and buy it immediately · 2 cost an arm and a leg — be very expensive · 3 spend a fortune — spend a lot · 4 tighten your belt — spend carefully when you have little · 5 throw money down the drain — waste money.
  4. Finish with the picture behind two of them — snap up is the bite of a crocodile: fast. Tighten your belt is what happens when there is no money for food. Images survive translation.
  5. Drill the rhythm of two idioms in chorus before they sit down.
📺 On screen: the two wall labels (SENSIBLE / RECKLESS) put up before the lesson. Backup: point at two walls and name them aloud — no materials needed.
👦 Students do: walk to a wall on every item, and justify the choice aloud when pointed at.

Why: Question-word first is the single most transferable reading instruction at B2, and it takes ten seconds to give.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.59 exercises 2–3 (track 24) — the two photos predicted, then ten questions answered

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 2, ninety seconds: look at the two photos and predict. (Photo 1: a cashless shop in Sweden. Photo 2: a seventeenth-century man having his beard cut off because he refuses to pay the tax on it.) Take guesses; confirm nothing yet.
  2. Before they read, give the one instruction that makes this task work: look at the QUESTION WORD first. Question 1 says Why — you are looking for a reason. Question 2 says How — you are looking for a method.
  3. Eight minutes, individually, with the recording (track 24) running the first time if your group needs the support. Answers: track 24, ten questions: 1 it was the first country in Europe to use banknotes · 2 anyone over seven can have a debit card · 3 a mobile payment app created by the six biggest Swedish banks · 4 because you can pay electronically almost everywhere · 5 for: fewer robberies, no theft at cashpoints, safer staff; against: a flat battery, hackers, companies tracking your spending, and it is easier to overspend · 6 tax on what you earn and tax on what you buy · 7 to make people adopt a Western style and show loyalty — a copper token proved you had paid the beard tax · 8 rich men grew longer beards to show status, because longer beards cost more · 9 bricking up fireplaces, building fewer windows, using bigger bricks · 10 a TV licence is a tax you pay for owning a television; it funds the BBC and keeps it advert-free.
  4. Check in fours with Numbered Heads Together — you call the question number and a student number, and that student answers for the group.
📺 On screen: recording — track 24 in the board app if you use it. Backup: the text is in the book and the task works perfectly as silent reading; the audio is support, not the source.
👦 Students do: predict from photos, then scan for ten specific answers, then answer for the group when their number is called.

Reading 2 · eight words from context + the surprise round
pairs
Why: Meaning-from-context is the vocabulary skill that transfers to an unseen text, and the ranking task forces a second pass through the article for content rather than language.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.59 exercise 4 and the Teacher's Book extra activity

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 4 in pairs, five minutes: work out eight expressions from context — no dictionaries. Answers: adopt = start to use · do away with = stop using · as long as = on condition that · run out = have none left · keep track of = have the information you need about · copper = a red-brown metal · stature = status in society · dodge = avoid.
  2. Then the extra activity, three minutes: find three facts in this text that surprised you most. Rank them. Pairs first, then two pairs share.
  3. Take one culture note back to them if it comes up: the window tax made people brick up their windows, and that is where the English phrase «daylight robbery» comes from.
  4. Board any three of the eight expressions you want recycled in the next stage — most usefully as long as, run out and keep track of.
📺 On screen: the three recyclable expressions written up. Backup: no display dependency here.
👦 Students do: negotiate eight meanings in pairs, then rank three surprising facts and defend the ranking.

🎯 Critical thinkers · cash or a phone?
groups of four
Why: The productive outcome of the lesson: the new words used to argue a real position, with the vocabulary requirement made explicit so that it is not optional.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.59 exercise 5 — a structured argument that uses the unit's vocabulary under pressure

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board two columns: REASONS TO USE CASH and REASONS TO PAY WITH A PHONE. Everyone copies them and writes two ideas under each, alone, in three minutes.
  2. Round Robin in fours: one reason each, round the group, no repeats, twice round.
  3. Then take a position: each group must agree ONE sentence beginning In our opinion, … and one group member reads it out.
  4. Push for the vocabulary explicitly: use three words from page 58 in your group sentence — I'm listening for them.
  5. Model the level you want: In my opinion it's better to pay by phone. It's quicker and you don't end up with a pocket full of coins. It's true that companies can see what you buy, but that doesn't worry me much.
  6. Correct nothing during the discussion; collect three errors for the next stage.
📺 On screen: the two columns on the board. Backup: nothing to fail — spoken stage.
👦 Students do: make notes alone, contribute in turn in fours, then agree and read out a group position.

70–80′
Personalisation + delayed correction
pairs → whole class
Why: Delayed correction lands when the class does the fixing, and the two errors this topic always produces are worth naming in writing.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.58 exercise 6 «Use it … don't lose it», then the three errors you collected

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 6 in Timed Pair Share: sixty seconds each, and the rule is that yes and no are not answers — every answer needs an example or a reason.
  2. Let two or three students ask YOU the questions afterwards. A teacher who answers a money question honestly buys a lot of attention.
  3. Board the three errors from the last stage, anonymised. Thirty seconds in pairs to fix them, then take the corrections.
  4. Almost certainly one of them will be «many money» or «I have no possibility». Fix both explicitly and write much money · a lot of money · I can't afford it on the board.
📺 On screen: the three errors and the corrected chunks on the board. Backup: no equipment.
👦 Students do: answer personal questions with reasons, then fix three anonymised sentences in pairs.

Word-stress workout · the ten hardest items
desks
Why: Pronunciation of high-frequency vocabulary is a listening skill as much as a speaking one: a word you cannot say is a word you do not hear.

WHAT WE DO: a short accuracy stage on pronunciation, because half these words are unsayable at first sight

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write ten words on the board with the stressed syllable blank: receipt · purchase · allowance · cashpoint · refund · bargain · budget · account · payment · debt.
  2. Pairs mark the stress and circle any silent letters. Two minutes.
  3. Say each word once; the class repeats twice, then you mark the board. The two that always break: receipt (silent p) and debt (silent b).
  4. Finish with three sentences said in chorus at speed: Can I have a receipt? · I can't afford it. · It cost an arm and a leg.
📺 On screen: the ten words on the board. Backup: dictate them; two minutes.
👦 Students do: mark stress in pairs, then repeat chorally and in rows.

  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will be better able to talk about spending and saving using B2 money vocabulary and idioms, in the context of teenage spending and a cashless society.
  • Subsidiary. Reading for specific information using question words; guessing meaning from context; word stress and silent letters in high-frequency money words.

Structure
I can't afford it. · It's good value for money. · She got into debt. · It cost an arm and a leg. · As long as you have a card, you don't need cash.
Form
money is uncountable: much money, a lot of money, an amount of money · afford + noun / afford to + infinitive (usually with can/can't) · spend money ON something · pay FOR something · get INTO debt · idioms are fixed: no changes to the words.
Pron. watch
two traps and one rhythm. receipt is /rɪˈsiːt/ — the p is silent and half the class will say it; purchase is /ˈpɜːtʃəs/ with a weak second syllable, not «purCHASE». The rhythm is the idioms: cost an ARM and a LEG · THROW money down the DRAIN — two beats carry each one, and the words between them almost disappear
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
afford: «I can't afford it» — do I want it? — Maybe. Do I have the money? — No.
value for money: Is it cheap? — Not necessarily. Is it worth what it costs? — Yes.
tighten your belt: Do I have a lot of money? — No. Am I spending carefully? — Yes.
ICQ before the reading: Which do you read first — the text or the question? How many minutes?

Assumptions: Students have basic money vocabulary from B1 (buy, sell, cheap, expensive, save) and can read a long article with a clear purpose. Idioms and financial collocations are new, and so is the uncountability of «money» as a productive problem.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«Many money», «I have no possibility to buy it».Both are calques and both are predictable. Write the correct chunks on the board in the delayed-correction stage and drill I can't afford it three times — it appears in every exam letter about money.
The article is long and the ten questions take too much time.Question words first, and let them work in pairs from question six onwards if the clock is tight. The last four questions are about the beard tax and the window tax — the most memorable half, so do not cut it.
Money is a sensitive subject in some families.Every task is about teenagers in general, Sweden or history — never about this family's income. Keep the personalisation stage on habits (do you save? do you shop around?) and not on amounts.
Idioms are copied but not used.The auction makes each student say one aloud with a justification, and the group-position stage requires three unit words in one sentence. Usage is built into the tasks, not hoped for.
«Receipt» comes back as /rɪˈsept/ for the rest of the year.Cross out the p on the board the first time and again in the stress workout. Two exposures in one lesson fixes it for most of the group.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 10–18 (reading) and tasks 32–36 (lexical choice, where money collocations live).

Money is a standing topic of the Russian paper — pocket money, part-time jobs, saving and spending come up in the reading section, in the email of task 37 and in the oral. What a candidate lacks is not ideas but collocation: afford, get into debt, value for money, allowance. Those are marks in tasks 32–36, where the four options are all real words and only one collocates.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
