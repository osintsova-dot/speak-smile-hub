🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use the nine illness-and-injury verbs (break, catch, hurt, injure, feel/get better…) with the right partners — and write a magazine article of about 100 words with a title, an introduction, answers to every question and a conclusion.
What is genuinely hard
The verbs look interchangeable and are not: you CATCH a cold but BREAK a leg; hurt works two ways (my leg hurts / I hurt my leg) where Russian uses two unrelated verbs. And in the writing — answering ALL the questions: the cheapest marks anywhere are lost to a skipped question.
Typical error to expect
My leg is ache · I have sore in my throat — ache used as an adjective and sore used as a noun; the frames are I've got a headache / My throat is sore [A2/B1]
A one-word reply («Flu.», «Yes.») does not count as an answer at all: an answer needs a verb in it [national exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
The verbs are sorted by what they can carry (a cold · sick · your leg) — the grid IS the grammar, and the recorded dialogues plus the book's mime pairwork make the verbs physical before the quiet half. The writing half runs on the block's proven model-first shape: the model arrives SCRAMBLED and the class rebuilds it, which teaches intro→answers→conclusion better than any diagram; then the second notice (the healthy-diet one) gets planned with the TB's sentence openers and written to the end IN CLASS, because an article finished at home is an article written by somebody else. Yesterday's will/going to is compulsory in the article — the grammar earns its keep the very next day.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Magazines pay people to do what you'll do today. A real notice, a real 100-word article with your name on it — about food, health and what you're going to change this year. And first: why English has NINE verbs for being ill, and which ones your leg needs.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a three-sentence error clinic from lesson 1, and the bridge into the verbs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

  2. 3 minutes — error clinic from the future interview. Three anonymous sentences from your lesson-1 log on the board (a will/going-to swap, a dropped be, an I will to…): pairs fix all three — Kagan RallyRobin, then hands up.

  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the lesson: Today, two halves: loud — nine verbs for being ill; quiet — you write an article a magazine would print.
📺 On screen: three faulty sentences in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: fix the sentences in pairs and give the reason. Expect the dropped-be one to fall fastest — praise the deciding-question reflex

Why: Nine verbs sorted by what they can carry (a cold · sick · your leg) — the grid is the grammar. The two-correct-answers format forces reading all three options, not grabbing the first plausible one.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 the grid + ex.2 two-correct-answers

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 in pairs, 3 minutes: which of the nine verbs go with a cold? sick? your leg? Key: which verbs go with: 1 a cold — catch/get/have · 2 sick — feel/get/be · 3 your leg — break/cut/hurt/injure. TB notes: sick is an adjective; catch never goes with better; hurt = injure OR be painful
  2. Concept checks from the plan header — the two jobs of hurt and the catchable things, cold-called.
  3. ex.2 solo, 4 minutes, pair check — TWO answers are correct each time, find both. Key: choose TWO correct: 1 B, C · 2 A, C · 3 A, B · 4 A, C · 5 A, C · 6 B, C (example 0: feeling/getting better).
  4. Kagan Showdown, four calls: you name the noun (flu! · your thumb! · better!), teams write every verb that fits and show on three — most correct verbs wins the call.
📺 On screen: calls by voice, answers on mini-whiteboards — voice and boards are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: sort, choose both answers, fire verb sets. Expect catch+better to be tried (it fails — TB notes it explicitly); expect delight that hurt fits almost everywhere. Typical error: I broke a cold — let the grid answer it

Why: The model arrives in the wrong order on purpose: rebuilding it teaches the shape (intro → answers → conclusion) better than any diagram — the students DO the structure before they are told it.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–5: the notice, the scrambled model, the box, the title

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1–2 — read the ARTICLES WANTED notice: Three questions. Count them. Every one needs an answer. The notice: ARTICLES WANTED! — Doing sport is healthy, but routines can be hard to follow. Why is sport important? What sports are you going to do this holiday? How will you make sure you do them regularly? Write an article answering these questions. We will publish the most interesting articles.
  2. ex.3 — the model in the wrong order: The student article arrives with its three paragraphs SCRAMBLED (A goal-setting · B people say they have no time · C why sport matters + the triathlon plan): the class rebuilds the order, then mines it — «There's a triathlon in my town… I'm going to compete in it! · That way we will be able to encourage each other.» Both target forms, in a model they have just enjoyed. Pairs rebuild: which paragraph opens?
  3. ex.4 — the Prepare-to-write box mapped onto the paragraphs. Key: Prepare-to-write match: 1 B (intro) · 2 C (main) · 3 A (conclusion).
  4. ex.5 — choose the best title and defend it. Key: best title: 2 «How to stay fit and healthy» — discuss why 1 and 3 only cover one paragraph each.
  5. Mine the model for the grammar: Find me one going to and one will. Why is each one there? (the triathlon plan · the encourage-each-other prediction)
📺 On screen: the notice and the model are on SB p.67 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: count the questions, rebuild the order, map the box, vote the title. Expect B to be misplaced first (it reads like an opening) — the fix teaches the intro's real job. Typical problem: title chosen by taste — the rule is «does it cover ALL of it?»

Planning · notes
solo → pairs
Why: Three questions from the notice and three sentence openers stop the blank-page freeze: nobody starts writing without knowing what each paragraph is for.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6: the healthy-diet notice, notes with the TB openers

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the second notice — the task: ex.6–7: the SECOND notice (a health-and-fitness magazine): What should a healthy diet include? How healthy is your diet? How can you avoid unhealthy food? → notes, then write about 100 words with be going to and will for decisions and predictions, plus advice language (It's a good idea to… · Remember to… · You should…).
  2. The TB openers go on the board: TB mixed-ability sentence openers: A healthy diet should include… · I think my diet is… · I'm going to… / I'll try to…
  3. Three minutes of silent notes: one line per notice question + a title idea. Not sentences — notes.
  4. Kagan Think-Pair-Share, one minute: tell your partner your three answers; steal one good idea shamelessly — say so: Stealing ideas is planning. Stealing sentences is copying.
📺 On screen: the openers and the three notice questions in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: note, share, steal an idea. Expect «my diet is bad» honesty — welcome it: honest articles are the readable ones. Typical problem: notes written as full sentences — stop it now or the writing stage starts finished and flat

🎯 Writing out · my article
solo, silent mode
Why: The productive outcome in writing: a real 100-word article with a title, built on a model, a plan and a checklist. Started AND finished in class.

WHAT WE DO: about 100 words: title, three paragraphs, every question answered, both futures inside

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The checklist goes up before the first word: title · intro · ALL three questions answered · conclusion · one going to + one will · advice language · ~100 words.
  2. Eighteen minutes of silent writing. You walk, read over shoulders, point at the checklist — no talking, no spelling service: Circle it, carry on, we fix it in review.
  3. Time calls in chalk: 10 · 5 · 2 minutes.
  4. Fast finishers: add ONE more concrete example with a reason — or take two of the reading's leftover detail questions from p.65.
  5. Two minutes before the end: Read your own last sentence. Does it close the article or just stop it?
📺 On screen: the checklist in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed; nothing else goes up while the room writes
👦 Students do: write to the end in silence. Expect 80–110 words from most; expect the conclusion to be the weak wall — the last-sentence call exists for it. Typical error: a question answered with one bare line — the names-and-reasons bar applies

70–80′
Peer review + polish
pairs
Why: A checklist swap turns every student into an editor: they catch each other's missing answers and flat titles faster than they catch their own — both sides learn.

WHAT WE DO: checklist swap: walls first, paint second

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap notebooks. Round 1 — the walls: tick each notice question you can FIND an answer to; a missing tick goes back to the author now, and one sentence gets added on the spot.
  2. Round 2 — the paint: Kagan RallyCoach: find one going to, one will, one advice phrase; circle one spelling you doubt (cough and stomach are the usual suspects).
  3. Authors take their work back: three minutes of repair.
  4. The reviewer writes one honest compliment line under the article — a real one, about content.
📺 On screen: the two review rounds numbered in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: check walls, check paint, repair, compliment. Expect at least a third of articles to gain a missing answer — that is the stage paying for itself. Typical problem: reviews that only hunt spelling — the rounds are ordered against exactly that

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«My leg hurts» vs «I hurt my leg» — same verb, two jobs: which one is the pain speaking, which one the accident? → first pain, second accident.
«I caught a cold» — did I run after it? → no: catch = get an illness from somewhere. Can I catch a broken leg? → no — you break it.
«Answer these questions» in the notice — how many questions must the article answer? → ALL of them. A skipped question is a lost paragraph.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
hurt/injure treated as free synonymsThe grid stage names the split: injure needs an object (I injured my ankle); hurt can also just BE the pain (my ankle hurts). The dialogue of ex.3 shows both jobs in four lines.
The article answers two questions out of threeThe checklist rules: tick each notice question as you answer it. Peer review checks the ticks BEFORE the polish — a missing answer costs a paragraph, not a comma.
Ideas stall on the healthy-diet noticeThe TB openers go up before writing starts: TB mixed-ability sentence openers: A healthy diet should include… · I think my diet is… · I'm going to… / I'll try to… — three sentence starts are enough runway for anyone.
Fast writers finish at 60 wordsThe bar is names-and-reasons: not «eat fruit» but WHICH fruit, WHEN, WHY. One example pushed deep beats three listed.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
