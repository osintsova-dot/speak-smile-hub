🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Choose correctly between pay, charge and cost (verbs AND nouns) by naming the subject — and write a ~100-word story with a compulsory first line, interesting verbs and time adverbs.
What is genuinely hard
Russian covers the three verbs with one frame («заплатил/стоило/берут»), so the subject is the invisible decision: products cost, sellers charge, customers pay. And in writing, the hard part is not language — it is architecture: stories arrive as one long breathless sentence without a plan.
Typical error to expect
The ticket paid £20 / I costed for the shoes — one Russian frame («заплатил/стоило») spread over three English verbs with three different subjects [A2/B1]
receipt (silent p!) · delicious · disgusting · juicy — the block's misspelling traps; a misspelt word scores nothing, in a class test and later in the exam
Why the lesson is built this way
The verb work comes first and is deliberately loud (a Showdown on subjects), because the writing half needs quiet — the lesson spends its energy budget early. Jamie's story is read as a story first (the twist is worth £4.99), mined as a model second: verbs upgraded, time adverbs harvested — so when students write, every tool has been touched in the last half hour. The three compulsory first lines are a gift, not a constraint: two of them open with a past continuous, quietly recycling block 3. Peer review against the same checklist closes the loop. This is the block's writing outcome — protect the eighteen quiet minutes.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Jamie sent back everything he bought except one pair of socks — and won a competition because of it. Today you read his story, steal his tricks, and write your own: one hundred words, a first line the book gives you, and a twist at the end if you can manage one. A real reader reads it before you leave.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts. The rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a three-sentence repair clinic from the mingle, and the bridge into the three verbs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — error clinic from the mingle. Three anonymous sentences from your lesson-2 log on the board (a participle slip, a been/gone swap, a bare yes). Kagan RallyRobin in pairs: find and fix all three, then hands up.
  3. Repair together fast; one choral Yes, I have! for the road.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page fifty-six, bottom half. Three verbs, one meaning in Russian, three subjects in English — and then you write a story with a twist.
📺 On screen: three faulty sentences in chalk — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: hunt the errors in pairs and fix them. Expect the been/gone swap to be found last — the arrows on the side board help

Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the two-part aim and the standard the story must reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the aim as two lines: 1. pay, charge or cost — by SUBJECT. 2. A story: 100 words, a shape, a twist if you dare.
  2. Show the story checklist skeleton on the board (it becomes the peer review list later): beginning-middle-end · interesting verbs (not just get and go) · 3+ time adverbs · a title · spelling checked.
  3. Set the bar: A story is finished when a READER understands it — your partner is the reader today.

👦 Students do: listen; one student reads the checklist aloud. Typical problem — «time adverbs» draws blank faces: promise a harvest from Jamie's story at 38′

Vocabulary · pay, charge, cost
pairs → class
Why: Three verbs, three subjects — products cost, sellers charge, customers pay. The rule is a table, not a feeling; the CCQs make the table stick.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: six examples → six definitions, and the three-subject table

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 in pairs, 4 minutes: match the definitions. Key: a Charge (noun, given) · b Pay (noun) · c cost of (noun) · d cost (verb) · e charge (verb) · f pay (verb).
  2. Build the table on the board as the answers land: Three verbs, three subjects: PRODUCTS cost an amount · SELLERS charge an amount · CUSTOMERS pay sellers. As nouns: the pay = money for a job · the cost (of) = what you spend · a charge = what a business asks.
  3. Concept checks from the plan header — the jacket, the museum, free of charge — cold-called by name.
  4. Drill the two hard forms by ear: charged /tʃɑːdʒd/ — one syllable · charges /ˈtʃɑːdʒɪz/ — two.
📺 On screen: the three-row table in chalk — the board is the medium and it stays up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: match, build the table, answer the CCQs. Expect the nouns (the pay, a charge) to be the surprise — the verbs feel familiar. Typical error: the ticket paid £20
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare4_Unit9_Vocabulary2_Pay_Charge_Cost_A5
A5 glue-in sheet — WHO PAYS WHAT?: three verbs, three subjects, one café bill — PAY (the customer gives money: pay £10 for the meal · How much did you pay?) · CHARGE (the seller asks for money: The café charged us £10. · How much did they charge?) · COST (the thing has a price: The meal cost £10. · How much did it cost?) — under the summary line PERSON PAYS · SHOP CHARGES · THING COSTS
  • Glue the sheet in — the subject chooses the verb, mark all three.
  • Say the summary line aloud until it is automatic.
  • Describe one purchase with all three verbs in three sentences.
  • Note the pattern: pay FOR the meal, but the meal COST £10 — no for.
When: ~5 min at the start of the “Vocabulary · pay, charge, cost” stagePrep: 1 × A5 per pupil. Scissors, glue sticks, pencils.

Practice · who is the subject?
pairs → teams
Why: Controlled practice with the subject named out loud every time — the transferable step. The Showdown round forces every team to commit in writing.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 controlled choice + a subject Showdown

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.2 solo against the clock (3 minutes), pair check with the reason said aloud. Key: 1 charge · 2 paid · 3 cost · 4 pay · 5 cost · 6 charge.
  2. Kagan Showdown on mini-whiteboards, six calls — you name a subject, teams write the verb: the delivery company! (charges) · these boots! (cost) · you, at the checkout! (pay) · the café, for extra cheese! (charges)…
  3. ex.3 pairwork from p.122 if the pace allows: items 1–3 verbs, 4–6 nouns.
📺 On screen: calls by voice, answers on mini-whiteboards — voice and boards are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: choose with reasons, then commit at speed. Expect cost/charge to be the last confusion standing — the table row is the pointer. Typical error: they cost us £2 for delivery

28–38′
Model story · Jamie
solo → pairs
Why: The model is read as a reader first (what is the twist worth — £4.99!), then mined as a writer: verbs, time adverbs, shape. Students steal from a text they have already enjoyed.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: read Jamie's story as readers first

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set a gist hook before reading: Jamie hates shopping for clothes. By the end of his story he's WON something. Read and find out what — and how much it was worth. 3 minutes silent reading.
  2. Kagan Think-Pair-Share: the twist is £4.99 — is that a happy ending or a joke? Both answers are defensible; make them defend.
  3. Walk the shape together: beginning (hates shopping → tries online), middle (the competition, the socks), end (the email, £4.99) — three fingers, three parts.
📺 On screen: Jamie's story is in every book on p.57 — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: read for the twist, argue its worth, map the shape. Expect the £4.99 line to get a laugh — that laugh is the lesson about endings. Typical problem: reading to translate every word — the hook question pulls them through

38–46′
Story anatomy
pairs
Why: Interesting verbs and time adverbs — the Prepare to write box turns a nice story into a checklist a 13-year-old can actually follow.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–5: upgrade the verbs, harvest the glue

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4 as a race, 2 minutes: four boring meanings, four interesting verbs from the story. Key: 1 enter · 2 looked up · 3 sent back · 4 noticed — «looked up», «sent back», «noticed» beat «found», «returned», «saw».
  2. ex.5: find the time adverbs, then choose. Key: 1 as soon as · 2 later · 3 while · 4 suddenly · 5 First · 6 When.
  3. Harvest onto the board into the STORY GLUE basket: first, then, while, when, suddenly, soon, a few days later, a few weeks after that.
  4. Mini-drill: Say «suddenly» like something actually happened. Choral, dramatic, once.
📺 On screen: the STORY GLUE basket in chalk — the board is the medium and the basket stays up through the writing stage, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: race, sort, harvest. Expect while/when to need one extra example (while + longer action — block 3's rule, still alive). Typical error: calling suddenly an adjective
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare4_Unit9_Writing_Story_A5
A5 writing card — WRITE A STORY, BUILD THE TENSION (topic: a shopping problem): the four-point PLAN (where you were · who was with you · what went wrong · how it ended), the model story annotated in the margin (opening · background · problem · action · ending), a USEFUL LANGUAGE strip (Last… · while / when · Suddenly · Luckily · In the end) and a CHECK list (past tenses · sequence words · clear ending · spelling and punctuation)
  • Glue the card in on the page where the story will be written.
  • Mark the five labels in the model before writing your own.
  • Write the story: the background in the past continuous, the problem in the past simple.
  • Suddenly and Luckily are compulsory — that is what builds the tension.
When: ~6 min at the start of the “Story anatomy” stagePrep: 1 × A5 per pupil. Scissors, glue sticks, pencils.
SB p.57 ex.4–5 · board
46–54′
Planning · notes
solo
Why: Three first lines to choose from and four planning questions stop the blank-page freeze: nobody starts writing without knowing where the story goes.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6: choose a first line, answer four questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Give the task exactly as the book does: Your story MUST begin with ONE of the three lines on the page — the department-store bag, Chloe and the special offer, or the security guard.
  2. Four planning questions on the board: Where are you? What goes wrong — or right? What are the 2–3 main events? How does it end — and how do you FEEL? Four answers, one line each, 5 minutes, silence.
  3. Show your plan to your partner for 60 seconds — one question or one idea from the partner, no more.
  4. Walk and unstick: anyone with an empty line gets a menu, not an answer — a mistake at the checkout? a wrong parcel? a famous customer?
📺 On screen: the four planning questions in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: choose a line, plan in four lines, exchange one idea. Expect the security-guard line to be the popular one — fine, the TB model uses it too and stays in your pocket
SB p.57 ex.6 · notebooks
54–72′
🎯 Writing out · my story
solo, silent
Why: The productive outcome in writing: a real 100-word story with a compulsory first line, built on a model, a plan and a checklist. Started AND finished in class.

WHAT WE DO: ex.7: write the story — about 100 words, checklist on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Eighteen minutes. About 100 words — 80 is the floor. The first line is already written for you. Checklist is on the board. Go.
  2. Visible timer on. First five minutes: questions allowed in a whisper. After that — silent mode, palm down.
  3. Walk the room with a pencil: underline (don't fix) one spelling and one verb-upgrade opportunity per draft as you pass.
  4. Minute twelve, quiet announcement: Time for a title. Steal Jamie's shape if you like.
  5. Fast finishers: swap with another fast finisher and write an alternative two-sentence ending for their story.
  6. Your own model in reserve if anyone needs to SEE the bar: TB model answer (the security-guard opening): «While I was leaving the shop, the security guard stopped me. Everyone was looking at me and I felt very embarrassed… ‘Have you bought anything here today?’ … Finally I found the receipt in my purse. I was so happy I almost hugged the guard!»
📺 On screen: visible timer counting down 18 minutes (Backup: write the end time in the corner of the board and update every five minutes by hand)
👦 Students do: write, hit the floor of 80 words, title the story. Expect the middle to sag around minute eight — point silently at the STORY GLUE basket. Typical error: get and go doing all the work — the underline pencil catches it

72–80′
Peer review + polish
pairs
Why: A checklist swap turns every student into an editor: they catch each other's tense slips and misspellings faster than they catch their own — both sides learn.

WHAT WE DO: checklist swap: every story gets a real reader

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap notebooks. Kagan RallyCoach against the board checklist: tick the five points, circle ONE spelling doubt, write ONE compliment word at the bottom (funny! clever! wow!).
  2. Two minutes back with your own draft: fix what your reader found.
📺 On screen: the checklist is already on the board from minute 5 — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: review, compliment, polish. Expect reviewers to be stricter than you are — channel it: circles, not crossings-out. Typical find: a missing title — cheap fix, instant win

Ten seconds of fame
class
Why: Two or three stories get a real audience — the cheapest motivation for next time's writing, and a natural close to the quiet half.

WHAT WE DO: two or three stories meet a real audience

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ask for volunteers — or draft the two compliments you liked best while walking: Whose partner's story deserves ten seconds of fame?
  2. The AUTHOR's partner reads the best two lines aloud — the author enjoys; the class reacts with one word each.
  3. If energy is high, thirty seconds of TB cooler — chain story: an opening sentence on the board; the class builds the story one sentence per student around the room.
📺 On screen: read aloud from the notebooks — paper and voice are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: listen, react, applaud. Expect shy authors to glow when the PARTNER reads — that is why the partner reads

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«The jacket paid £40» — can a jacket pay? → no: customers pay. The jacket COST £40.
«The museum charges £5» — who gets the money? → the museum: sellers charge, visitors pay.
«Free of charge» — who pays? → nobody: there is no charge at all.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
pay/charge/cost collapse into one verbThe subject question is the tool, used every single time: WHO is the subject — the thing, the seller or the customer? The three-row table stays on the board all lesson.
Stories arrive as one endless sentenceThe plan is compulsory and checked BEFORE writing starts: four answers, four lines. No plan — no pen.
Fast finishers at minute ten of the writing stageTB fast-finisher: swap stories and write an alternative ENDING for your partner's story — two sentences, same characters.
Weak writers freeze at 100 wordsThe floor is 80 words and the model is open on the desk: copying the SHAPE of Jamie's story is allowed and said out loud to be allowed.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
