🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use ten words that describe how you learn a language — look up, guess, translate, spell, meaning, mistakes, topic, list, articles, exercises — and find specific numbers and names in a long factual text.
What is genuinely hard
The listening. Five separate conversations, three options each, and every option is mentioned out loud in the recording — in question 4 all three options contain the same language. Recognising a word is worth nothing; the question decides.
Typical error to expect
A word from the option was said in the recording → they tick it. A matching word is not a correct answer; meaning decides [exam report]
My mother learns me English instead of teaches — learn = you learn it yourself [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The unit opens on the students' own experience rather than on the book: the English words already inside their language, then a quiz about how they themselves learn. The vocabulary is embedded in that quiz, so the ten words are used to answer real questions before they are ever tested. The listening is placed before the reading because it is the hardest thing in the lesson and needs the freshest attention, and it gets its own orientation stage — one question per item, asked before the recording starts, which is the difference between three marks and five. The reading is a map, not a story: the task is to find, not to understand every word, and that is worth saying out loud to a class that has been taught to read every syllable. The lesson ends by turning the world map into the class's own map, which costs three minutes and makes the text theirs.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“There are about seven thousand languages in the world. One country — one — has eight hundred and thirty-two of them. Meanwhile the language you are sitting in now is third, and two thirds of the people who speak it learned it the way you are learning it. Today: how you learn, and how big the thing you are learning actually is.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a three-round rewind of Unit 15, and the bridge into Unit 16

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — Unit 15 rewind, and the last time it is asked for. Three rounds on the board: One — a hobby you have done for more than a year. Two — something you have wanted since you were small. Three — finish «I always enjoy myself when …».
  3. One answer per round from three different students. The interview language is one lesson old and this is the last time it is revisited before the block test.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page ninety. New unit — and this one is about the thing you are doing right now.
📺 On screen: the three prompts in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: produce one sentence per round. Expect the for/since choice to be automatic now — if it is not, that is the diagnosis for the review lesson. Typical problem: they want to keep telling interview stories; take two and stop

Lesson frame

Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the two halves of the lesson and the standard for the listening

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write one number on the board, very large, and nothing else: 7,000. Guesses. What is that? Take four guesses, confirm nothing.
  2. Then the two halves: 1 How do YOU learn a language? 2 How many languages are there to learn?
  3. The standard for the hard part of the lesson, said now so it can be pointed at later: In the listening today, everything you can see in all three options will be said out loud. Hearing a word is not an answer.
  4. Ask them to write one honest number in the margin: how many words of English they think they know. It comes back at the end.
📺 On screen: the number 7,000 and the two halves of the lesson in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: guess at the number and write their own. Expect guesses about population or distance. Typical problem: somebody knows the answer already — ask them to keep it for forty minutes and they usually will

Lead-in · English around us
groups of 4
Why: Starting from the English already inside their own language makes the topic theirs and surfaces the question the unit is actually about: why do languages borrow?

WHAT WE DO: the About you box p.90, opened with the TB p.177 warmer about borrowed words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.177: groups get two minutes to write down English words that have moved into their own language. Count them, then ask the two questions that make it a lesson: Why has our language taken these words? Is that a good thing?
  2. Count the words each group found and put the winning number on the board. Then the two questions that make it a lesson rather than a game: Why has our language taken these words? Is that a good thing?
  3. Then the About you box itself: How do you feel about learning English? Is it a fun hobby? Is it boring and not very useful? Is it important for your future?
  4. Kagan Think-Pair-Share, one round, then two groups report. Take the honest answers, including the negative ones — the quiz in fifteen minutes works much better if nobody has pretended.
📺 On screen: the borrowed words collected onto the board as the groups call them out — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: list English words in their own language, then answer the About you questions. Expect a long list from computing and sport. Typical problem: they list words that went the other way — worth thirty seconds, because it is the same point in reverse

14–26′
Vocabulary · language learning
solo → pairs
Why: Ten words about the thing they do three times a week. The words are learnt inside a quiz, so meaning is checked by use rather than by translation.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.90: complete the quiz with the ten words in the box

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed, two quick meaning checks (TB p.177): How do you spell «dog»? (D-O-G — so spell is that) and You do this when you think you know but you are not sure. (guess).
  2. Books open. The ten words are read aloud once and the class says the category: All of these are about …? (learning a language).
  3. Then model how a gap is solved rather than guessed: Look at gap one. After «read» we need a …? (a noun) Which noun in the box makes sense here? (articles).
  4. Solo, 5 minutes, all ten gaps. Key: 1 articles · 2 look, up · 3 guess · 4 translate · 5 meaning · 6 list · 7 exercises · 8 spell · 9 mistakes · 10 topic.
  5. Pre-teach one word the quiz needs and nobody has: abroad — in a foreign country.
  6. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with definition cards, 3 minutes, five trades each.
  7. Fast finishers: TB p.177: fast finishers invent a fifth option, E, for each quiz question — to play computer games. They write them on the board and the class says whether anybody actually does that.
📺 On screen: the ten words and the quiz on the screen so the class can watch gap one being solved (Backup: everything is printed on SB p.90 — read the ten words aloud, write them on the board as a word bank, and solve gap one there)
👦 Students do: complete ten gaps, then trade definitions. Expect 8 of 10 solo. Typical errors: look up split across two gaps (it is one answer in two places), and meaning confused with mistakes

26–34′
The learner quiz
solo → pairs
Why: The quiz result is a genuine piece of self-knowledge, and it is the only stage of the year where students are asked to describe HOW they learn rather than what.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2–3 p.90: actually do the quiz, score it on SB p.137, then compare

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Solo, 3 minutes: one answer per question, and it has to be honest rather than flattering.
  2. Before any score is read out, read the kindest band aloud first, from SB p.137: «English probably isn't your favourite subject, but if you work hard, you can be good at it.» That takes the sting out of the whole thing.
  3. Then the scoring: SB p.90, «What kind of language learner are you?» — four questions with four options each: when you use English outside the classroom · what you do with a new word · the best way to learn vocabulary · which statement about learning you agree with. The scoring is on SB p.137: A = 4, B = 3, C = 2, D = 1. 12–16 «a very serious learner — but remember you can have fun too» · 8–11 «you enjoy learning English and you are not afraid of mistakes» · 4–7 «English probably isn't your favourite subject, but study a little and often and you'll see the difference».
  4. Kagan RallyCoach: compare results in pairs and answer one question — Do you agree with what it says about you?
  5. One whole-class question to close it, and it is the useful one: What is one thing on this quiz that you do not do — and could?
📺 On screen: the three score bands on the screen, revealed lowest first (Backup: they are printed on SB p.137 — read the three bands aloud in the same order and have students add up their own scores in the margin)
👦 Students do: answer four questions honestly, score themselves, then compare. Expect most of the class in the 8–11 band. Typical problem: somebody scores 4 and announces it loudly — the pre-read band is what makes that survivable

Listening · five conversations
solo → pairs
Why: Five short conversations, three options each — the densest listening format of the course. The orientation questions before the recording are what make it doable.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–5 p.90 (track 117): five short conversations, three options each

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Reading time first, 90 seconds, silent: five questions and fifteen options, before anything is played.
  2. Then one orientation question per item, taken straight from TB p.177: TB p.177, one orientation question per item before you press play: What language is Danny learning? (Spanish) If the teacher said «the present perfect», would the answer be A, B or C? (A) What problem does the boy have? (he hasn't done his homework) Which language does Yumi definitely speak? (Swedish — it is in all three options) What could the news be about?
  3. Say the warning from the lesson frame once more, pointing at question 4: All three of these say Swedish. So Swedish tells you nothing.
  4. Play track 117 straight through. Pens down for thirty seconds afterwards, then play it again — first time to answer, second time to check.
  5. Kagan Showdown: A, B or C on the mini-whiteboard, all five items in a row, boards up together. Key: 1 B · 2 B · 3 A · 4 C · 5 A.
  6. Then the strategy, named once and written on the board: TB p.177: listen for the words that signal a contrast or the important bit — however in question 2, How do you know? in question 5. And use the questions before the recording: question 4 tells you Swedish is in all three options, so Swedish proves nothing.
  7. And the trap: Where marks go: question 4. All three options contain Swedish, so a student who is matching words instead of listening for meaning has a one-in-three chance and nothing more.
📺 On screen: track 117 in the board app with the five questions on the screen (Backup: the audioscript is on TB p.301 — read the five conversations aloud yourself, twice, changing your voice for each speaker; the questions are printed in the students' own book)
👦 Students do: listen twice under quiet conditions, then reveal together. Expect 3 of 5 first time and 4 of 5 after the second play. Typical error: question 3 — the boy gives two reasons and the first one is the wrong one

Reading · four questions first
pairs
Why: Four questions asked before the text is opened. Curiosity is a better reason to read than an instruction, and the answers are genuinely surprising.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.92: four questions asked and guessed BEFORE the text is opened

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The four questions, on the board, before anybody looks at p.92: How many languages are there in the world? What European language family does Polish belong to? Which Asian language has the most speakers? What are some common second languages in Africa?
  2. Pairs guess all four, 3 minutes, writing the guesses down. Guesses that are written down get checked; guesses that are spoken get forgotten.
  3. Collect the range for question 1 only — the lowest guess and the highest — and put both on the board next to the 7,000 from the lesson frame, still uncovered.
  4. Now open p.92 with a genuinely short time limit: Four minutes. Four answers. This is a map, not a story — you are looking for numbers, not reading sentences.
  5. Key: 1 7,000 · 2 Slavic · 3 Chinese · 4 English, French, Portuguese.
📺 On screen: the four questions in chalk with the guesses written next to them — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed; the text itself is printed in the students' own book
👦 Students do: guess four answers, then scan to check. Expect the guess for question 1 to be wildly low, usually a few hundred. Typical problem: they start reading the map from the top and run out of time — the four-minute limit is what prevents it

54–68′
Reading · the language map
solo → pairs
Why: A text made almost entirely of numbers and names, which is exactly why it is easy to read and hard to read carefully. The task is to find, not to understand every word.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.92 (track 118): five questions that each need a number or a name

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. For your own orientation before they open the page: SB p.92, a world map with one paragraph per continent. There are about 7,000 languages in the world; similar ones are grouped into families, and many of the smallest have no writing at all, so when the last speaker dies the language dies too. Europe — 284 languages: Romance (Spanish, Portuguese, Italian), Slavic (Russian, Polish, Czech), Germanic (English). English is the third largest language in the world: 335 million first-language speakers and 505 million second-language speakers. Basque, spoken in Spain and France, belongs to no family at all. Asia — 2,303 languages; Chinese has a billion speakers, more than any other language; Hindi is fourth and Arabic fifth. The Americas — 1,060 languages; Spanish is second in the world with 406 million speakers; Portuguese in Brazil, English and French in Canada, and native languages such as Mam in Mexico and Guatemala. Africa — 2,146 languages, and probably the continent where human language began; many people speak English, French or Portuguese as well as their own. Australia and Oceania — 1,311 languages; Papua New Guinea alone has 832, more than any other country, while some Aboriginal languages now have one or two speakers left.
  2. Five questions, solo, 6 minutes. The rule is the same as in lesson 1: Answer, then underline the line. No line, no answer.
  3. Key: 1 Chinese — a billion speakers; Spanish — 406 million; English — 335 million · 2 Asia, with 2,303 languages · 3 Papua New Guinea, with 832 languages · 4 It does not belong to any language family. · 5 They have become very small and have very few speakers..
  4. Play track 118 once while they check, so they hear the numbers said aloud — that is the rehearsal for next lesson's vocabulary and it costs nothing here.
  5. Kagan Numbered Heads Together to collect: Question three — number fours, boards up!
  6. One question that is worth thirty seconds because it is the point of the whole text (TB p.181): What information here surprised you most?
  7. Background for you, not for them: TB p.181: modern humans evolved in Africa about 200,000 years ago and spread out roughly 50,000 years ago, developing language as they went. There is no direct evidence of early speech — the earliest written records are Sumerian tablets from about 3000 BC — so what we know comes from archaeology, from cave art and from studying remote communities in places like Papua New Guinea. Migration is what makes language families: Romance, Slavic and Turkic all follow settlement patterns. Globalisation is now running the other way, and smaller languages are dying as new generations move to English.
📺 On screen: track 118 in the board app with the world map on the screen (Backup: the map is printed on SB p.92 and the recording adds nothing that is not on the page — read the continent paragraphs aloud yourself, or simply skip the listen-and-check step, which costs the lesson nothing)
👦 Students do: answer five questions and prove each one. Expect question 1 (three languages and three numbers) to be the slow one. Typical error: they give English as the biggest language, because it is the one on the page in front of them — the text says Chinese, and the number proves it

🎯 One fact each
whole class
Why: The productive outcome: every student says one fact from the map out loud, in a full sentence, with a number in it. Twelve facts, twelve speakers, four minutes.

WHAT WE DO: the productive outcome: every student says one fact from the map, out loud, with a number in it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set it up as a rule rather than as a task: One fact each. Round the room. It must be a full sentence and it must contain a number. No repeats — if somebody takes yours, find another.
  2. Model two yourself so the shape is unmistakable: Papua New Guinea has eight hundred and thirty-two languages. Asia has two thousand three hundred and three.
  3. Give them ninety seconds to find and write their own before anybody speaks — that is what stops the last five students being stranded.
  4. Round the room. Say each number back, louder and correctly, rather than correcting it — that is the whole pronunciation teaching of the stage.
  5. Then the second half of the task, in pairs: Close the book. How many of the twelve facts can you still say? Kagan RallyCoach, two minutes.
📺 On screen: the numbers written on the board as they are said, so the class sees the figure and hears the words at the same time — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: produce one factual sentence each, then recall as many as possible. Expect twelve different facts and about seven remembered. Typical error: the numbers are read digit by digit — say the whole number back and the class copies you

Talking points · your languages
groups of 4
Why: The class turns the world map into their own map. It costs three minutes and it is what makes the text about them.

WHAT WE DO: the Talking points box p.92: turn the world map into this class's own map

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The three questions from the box: What languages do you speak? What languages are you studying? What languages do your parents speak?
  2. Groups of four, three minutes, then one number per group to the board: how many languages does your group of four contain?
  3. Add them up as a class and write the class total under the 7,000. Seven thousand in the world. And how many in this room?
  4. Two questions to finish, both from TB p.181 and both genuinely open: Do you think there will be more or fewer languages in the future? Could another language replace English as the international one?
  5. Take three answers, do not resolve it, and stop. It is a discussion, not a conclusion.
📺 On screen: the class's own language total written on the board under the 7,000 from the lesson frame — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: pool their languages and argue about the future. Expect the class total to surprise them, especially if anybody has a grandparent with another language. Typical error: they count English twice — worth one clarification and no more

  • say what kind of language learner they are and why

Structure
I look words up in a dictionary. · I try to guess what it means. · I translate it into Russian. · Making mistakes is part of learning. · The topic of the lesson was interesting.
Form
look up is separable: look it up, never look up it. translate takes into, not on. learn is what you do yourself, teach is what somebody does to you.
Pron. watch
the phrasal verb keeps its stress on the particle: look it UP. And the large numbers of the reading: seven THOUsand · a BILlion · three hundred and thirty-five MILlion.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Look it up in a dictionary.» Can I say «look up it»? → no. When the object is a pronoun it goes in the middle.
«My mother learns me English.» Who is the teacher in that sentence? → nobody. learn is what you do yourself; the mother teaches.
Question 4 of the listening: all three options contain Swedish. So does hearing «Swedish» help? → no, not at all. The question is which languages she speaks WELL.
«English is the third largest language.» Third by what? → by number of speakers. The text gives two different numbers for English, and only one of them is first-language.

The present perfect and past simple both appear in the recordings and in the map text, but the contrast between them is not taught until next lesson — do not open it today, even if a student asks.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
