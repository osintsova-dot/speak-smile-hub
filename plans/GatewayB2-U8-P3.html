🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Complete nine gapped notes from a five-minute radio programme, writing only what is heard and spelling it correctly, and then produce the two reporting-passive structures: the impersonal It is said / was reported / has been claimed that … and the personal He is known to have written … / They are believed to be ….
What is genuinely hard
The listening is hard for a reason that is not vocabulary: the notes paraphrase the recording, so a student waiting for the exact words of the note hears nothing. Two of the nine gaps are numbers said fast (nearly 220 kilometres per hour, 1891) and one is a date said in the British order. The grammar is hard in one specific way: students fuse the two structures into He is said that he is, and for past events they write to had or to have die.
Typical error to expect
«It is said that people believe …» → «People say that …» is fine, but the reverse trips them: It is said that needs the that, and He is said to be needs the infinitive with to. Two structures, two shapes, and students mix them into He is said that he is.
«is believed to had died», «was said to have die» — for a past event the form is to have + third form, always have, never has or had, and always the third form after it.
Why the lesson is built this way
The listening and the grammar are the same language, which is why the book puts them on one spread and why this plan does not treat them as two lessons. The recording is saturated with the target structure — what is said to have been the worst natural disaster in the history of the US, it's been claimed that between 6,000 and 12,000 people died, Cline is known to have written a newspaper article, emergency plans are believed to be just as important — so the grammar block opens by mining a text the class has already understood. The order is therefore: hear it, understand it, then notice it.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
On the eighth of September 1900 a hurricane hit an island city in Texas. Between six and twelve thousand people died — it is still the worst natural disaster in American history. The head of the local weather office had published an article nine years earlier laughing at the idea that a hurricane could seriously damage Galveston, and partly because of him the city decided not to build a seawall. Cuba knew where the storm was going. Washington did not listen. Notice how I told you all that: «it is claimed», «it is known», «it is believed». That is today's grammar, and it exists because sometimes nobody is certain.

WHAT WE DO: greeting at the door, then three passive sentences on a disaster

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — the last time the weather changed your plans. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — rewind, yesterday's passive, aimed at today's topic. On the board: Three sentences about a disaster you know: one present passive, one past passive, one with a modal.
  3. RallyRobin in pairs, then three read aloud. Correct only the auxiliary and the participle — nothing else.
  4. If was build or a missing be appears, write the correction silently on the board next to the sentence and carry on. Yesterday's chant is still fresh; it needs one reminder, not a re-teach.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): You can say what was done. Today: how to say what people SAY was done, when nobody is completely sure. Page 102.
📺 On screen: the prompt on the board and any corrections written beside it. Backup: dictate the prompt.
👦 Students do: produce three passive sentences each in pairs, then three read to the class.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.102 exercise 1 and the Teacher's Book warmer — activating the topic and predicting the content

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Start with the Teacher's Book warmer, two minutes, books closed: List all the natural disasters you can think of. Now — which FAMOUS ones? Where? When? What damage? Take four or five and push each one for the three details.
  2. Books open at page 102. The photograph is a wooden house pushed off its foundations, people standing in front of it, clearly a hundred years old.
  3. Board three headings — WHERE? · WHEN? · WHAT HAPPENED? — and run exercise 1 in pairs, three minutes. Push them to justify from the photograph: the children's clothes, the material of the house, the absence of cars.
  4. Collect ideas under the three headings without confirming anything. The class will guess America and roughly the right century, and almost nobody will guess a hurricane.
  5. Hold all of that. We are about to find out.
📺 On screen: the three headings filled in on the board as the class guesses. Backup: the photograph is in their own book — nothing to project.
👦 Students do: list famous disasters, then speculate about a photograph in pairs and justify from visual evidence.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.102 exercise 2 with track 45 — one hearing for the whole picture, nothing written

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Before you press play, say the instruction that protects the stage: Pens down. Nothing to write. You are only checking the three things on the board.
  2. Play track 45 once, straight through, no pauses. It runs about five minutes: a presenter and a weather expert, Max Davis.
  3. Immediately after, thirty seconds in pairs, then take the answers against the board: the photograph was taken in Galveston, Texas, in 1900; a category four hurricane hit the island city on 8 September and killed somewhere between six and twelve thousand people.
  4. One question to check that the argument, not just the facts, landed: Why was it so important? What did they learn from it? (Two things: the Weather Bureau needed better communication, and politics had to be kept out of weather prediction — Washington had blocked the Cuban reports.)
  5. Ask what surprised them. It is usually the number of dead, or the fact that Cline broke the rules and issued a warning without authorisation.
📺 On screen: track 45 played once from the class audio. Backup: if the audio fails, read the class audio script aloud from the Teacher's Book, printed page 131 (PDF 133) — it is a two-voice dialogue and you can do both parts, or give one part to a strong student to read. Listening 2 · nine notes, and the strategy that fills them
desks → pairs
Why: Note completion is a strategy skill before it is a listening skill, and the strategy is teachable in four minutes. Playing back the two failed gaps in isolation is worth more than a third full hearing.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.102 exercise 3 with track 45 again — the Exam tip taught explicitly, then applied

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the Exam tip on the page, then convert it into five instructions on the board, because this is the strategy the class will use all year: (1) read the incomplete notes BEFORE you listen; (2) look at the words either side of each gap and decide what KIND of word is missing — a number, a name, a noun, a verb; (3) one to three words per gap, no more; (4) write what you HEAR, not a synonym; (5) spelling is marked.
  2. Two minutes, silent: read the nine gapped sentences and write the expected word class next to each gap. Take three or four aloud — gap c and gap i are clearly years, gap f is clearly a number.
  3. Play track 45 again, straight through. Then ninety seconds to finish, then compare in pairs before you check.
  4. Answers: track 45, note completion: a Great Storm · b Service · c 1891 · d seawall · e 4th of September · f nearly 220 · g politics · h (mass) evacuation · i 2005. The programme is about the Galveston hurricane of 8 September 1900 — a category four storm that destroyed 3,600 buildings and killed between 6,000 and 12,000 people.
  5. Play back the two gaps the class got wrong, pausing on the exact line. It is usually f (the winds are thought to have reached 135 miles per hour — that's nearly 220 kilometres per hour: the note wants the kilometres) and h (emergency plans for mass evacuation).
  6. Name the trap out loud: Gap f is the one everybody loses. The recording says both numbers. The note says «kilometres». Read the note.
📺 On screen: track 45 played a second time, plus the five-point strategy on the board. Backup: if the audio fails, read the Teacher's Book audio script (printed p.131) aloud twice at natural pace, pausing nowhere the first time and at the nine answer points the second time.
👦 Students do: predict the word class for nine gaps, listen and complete, compare in pairs, then hear the two hardest gaps again in isolation.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.102 exercise 4 — a short argued discussion before the grammar block

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board the question exactly as the book has it: How much importance should we give to natural disasters that happen a long way from our own home? What makes you say that?
  2. Ninety seconds alone, notes only, both sides. Then Round Robin in fours, one idea each, twice round.
  3. Feed the two Teacher's Book prompts to any group that stalls: Which places are more likely to experience natural disasters? Do we have a responsibility to help victims?
  4. Take two positions from the room and then the follow-up question that makes it sharper: Are events that happen a long way from our country less important than events in our own? Why? Why not?
  5. Correct nothing. Collect two errors for the wrap-up.
📺 On screen: the question and the follow-up on the board. Backup: nothing to fail — spoken stage.
👦 Students do: make notes on both sides, contribute in turn in fours, defend a position to the class.

WHAT WE DO: four minutes on their feet that walks straight into the target structure

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two lines facing each other. Whisper one sentence to the first person in each line: Scientists have discovered that the volcano will erupt again in nine years.
  2. It passes down the line by whisper. The last person says it aloud. It will be wrong, and that is the joke and the point.
  3. Now the language move: So what do we actually know? Nothing. We only know what people SAY. How do we say that in English? Take attempts — somebody will produce people say that ….
  4. Write the transformation on the board and leave it: People think natural disasters are becoming more frequent. → It is thought that natural disasters are becoming more frequent.
  5. Sit them down on that sentence.
📺 On screen: the two versions of the sentence written on the board and left up. Backup: none needed — voice and board.
👦 Students do: pass a rumour down a line by whisper, then watch it become the target structure.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.102 Grammar in context 2, exercises 1 and 2 — the first of the two structures

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 1: four sentences on the page (It was reported that the hurricane was heading towards New England and three more), three questions under them. Pairs, three minutes.
  2. Answers: a the impersonal pronoun it · b to talk about general thoughts and beliefs, when who thinks them is not important · c the tense of be shows whether the belief is past, present or general. Other verbs that work the same way: believe, know, think, say, expect, claim, report
  3. Board the verb list, because they will need it for the rest of the unit: believe · know · think · say · expect · claim · report.
  4. One transformation together on the board: They expected that the hurricane would lose strength gradually → It was expected that the hurricane would lose strength gradually.
  5. Say the register point once, because it explains why they have never used it: This is formal English. It is how news reports and academic texts are written. You would not say it to a friend.
  6. Exercise 2, six transformations, seven minutes, individually then compared in pairs. Answers: 2 It is thought that it's a myth · 3 It is known that the Ancient Greeks already knew the Earth was spherical · 4 It has been claimed that the world is the same shape as a frisbee · 5 It was reported that a telescope had been used to check the atmosphere on Uranus · 6 It is expected that a new rocket will be sent up one of these days · 7 It is claimed that there are members of the Flat Earth Society all around the world.
  7. Do not accept a version without that. It is the commonest omission and it is the difference between a mark and no mark in the unit test.
📺 On screen: the seven reporting verbs and the worked transformation on the board. Backup: no technology needed.
👦 Students do: analyse four model sentences, then transform six of their own and compare in pairs.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.103 exercises 3 and 4 — the second structure, and the form that always breaks

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 3: four sentences from the recording (The disaster is said to be the worst in US history, Cline is known to have written a newspaper article and two more), five True/False statements under them. Pairs, four minutes. Answers: a True · b True · c True · d True · e True. The two shapes to put on the board: subject + be + believed/known + to + infinitive (present or general) and subject + be + believed/known + to have + past participle (past)
  2. Board the two shapes side by side and leave them up, exactly as the Teacher's Book does: subject + be + believed/known/said + TO + infinitive — present or generally true — and subject + be + believed/known/said + TO HAVE + past participle — past.
  3. Two examples underneath: The number of extreme weather events is expected to increase. · Alexander the Great is known to have died in Babylon. Have them copy both shapes and both examples.
  4. Exercise 4, eight rewritings. Do number 1 together, aloud, and name the decision: «They reported that most people HAD LEFT» — past event, so «to HAVE left», not «to leave».
  5. Write the memory aid on the board and leave it: Now or always → to go. Finished in the past → to have gone.
  6. Seven minutes, in pairs. Answers: 1 Most people were reported to have left the city before the eruption · 2 Krakatoa is believed to have been the worst volcanic eruption ever · 3 At first the Earth wasn't known to go around the Sun · 4 Some tourists are said to enjoy visiting the sites of natural disasters · 5 Natural disasters are believed to be becoming more frequent · 6 Some animals are known to be able to hear things humans can't · 7 Flamingos are believed to have flown away before the tsunami · 8 Global warming is claimed to be responsible for many natural disasters.
  7. Mixed ability from the Teacher's Book: for a stronger group, ask them to write the active version of each of their eight sentences and read two of them out.
📺 On screen: the two structural shapes and the memory aid written on the board and left up. Backup: dictate both shapes; the stage is board-and-book only.
👦 Students do: judge five statements about form, then rewrite eight sentences choosing between the present and the perfect infinitive.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.103 exercises 5, 6 and 7 — the structures in a text, then produced freely

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 5, the text THE RESCUE DOG WHO BECAME A NATIONAL HERO — Frida, the Mexican Navy labrador who searched the debris after the 2017 Mexico City earthquake. Ask them to predict from the photograph and title first; take ideas without confirming.
  2. Eleven choices a–k, individually, four minutes, then compared in pairs. Answers: «The rescue dog who became a national hero» (Frida, Mexico): a have become · b It is · c Frida · d It · e that photos of Frida have · f is · g are reported · h have become · i reported · j Rescue dogs are known · k say.
  3. Take one fact from the text back to the class because it will stay with them: Frida is credited with saving twelve people and finding fifty-three bodies. People wanted her face on the 500-peso banknote. She retired in 2019.
  4. Exercise 6, six sentence stems completed logically (People from my country are said … · It's well known … · In the past, it was believed … · Last week in the news it was reported … · It's often wrongly claimed … · I've never been known …). Four minutes, alone.
  5. Mixed ability: for less confident students, elicit the next word for each stem before they start (1 to · 2 that · 3 that · 4 that · 5 that · 6 to). That single scaffold removes most of the failure.
  6. Exercise 7, Timed Pair Share, three minutes: read your six to a partner and say whether you agree with theirs. The disagreements are the interesting part — take two to the whole class.

WHAT WE DO: seven minutes on the listening discipline that earns marks in the Russian paper

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Say who it is for: This is for the people taking the EGE in May. Two minutes.
  2. Tasks 3 to 9 of the Russian listening are seven questions on one long interview. Same discipline as page 102: read first, decide what kind of information you need, listen for the IDEA, not for the word.
  3. Then the two rules that actually lose the marks, written on the board: (1) Write what you hear. Do not improve it, do not translate it, do not use a synonym. (2) Spelling counts. «Evacuation» with one c is zero.
  4. Do a thirty-second live demonstration with gap f from today's task: play or read the line the winds are thought to have reached 135 miles per hour — that's nearly 220 kilometres per hour and ask what goes in the note. Half the room will still say 135. The note says kilometres. The recording gives you both. It is not a listening problem — you did not read the note.
  5. Close it: The three minutes before the recording starts are worth more than the recording itself.

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will be better able to report unverified information using the impersonal passive (it is said that …) and the personal passive with an infinitive (he is known to have …), in the context of a historical disaster and a rescue dog.
  • Subsidiary. Listening to a five-minute radio programme for specific information and completing gapped notes with one to three words; discussing responsibility towards distant disasters.
  • Personal. Play the two failed gaps back in isolation rather than playing the whole recording a third time. Precision beats repetition and it saves four minutes.
By the end, students can…
  • complete gapped notes from a long recording without writing synonyms
  • predict the word class of a gap before listening
  • report general belief with it is said / thought / claimed that …
  • report a past event with is believed to have + past participle
  • choose between the present and the perfect infinitive correctly

Structure
It is said that dolphins can communicate. · It was reported that the hurricane was heading towards New England. · Dolphins are said to be able to communicate. · Cline is known to have written a newspaper article in 1891. · Krakatoa is believed to have been the worst eruption ever.
Form
structure one: It + be + past participle of believe / know / think / say / expect / claim / report + that + clause — the that is not optional in writing · structure two: subject + be + the same participle + to + infinitive for the present or a general truth, and + to have + past participle for a past event · the tense of be shows whether the belief itself is past, present or general · both are formal register.
Pron. watch
the reporting passive is one connected blur: it is said that → /ɪtɪzˈsedðət/, is believed to have been → /ɪzbɪˈliːvdtəvbɪn/. Note also two participles that break the class: thought /θɔːt/ (one syllable, no /g/) and claimed /kleɪmd/ (the -ed is /d/, never /ɪd/)
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
it is said that: «It is said that the storm was the worst ever» — do we know who says it? — No. Does it matter? — No. Is it certain? — No.
present vs perfect infinitive: «He is known to have died in Babylon» — is he dying now? — No, it's finished. And «the number is expected to increase» — has it increased yet? — No, it's the future or generally true.
register: Would you say «it is claimed that» to your friend about football? — No. Where would you read it? — In the news, in an article.
ICQ before the note completion: Do you read the notes before or after the recording? How many words maximum? Do you write what you hear or your own word?

Assumptions: Students can form the passive in all tenses after lesson 2 and have met reported speech in Unit 7. They have done gapped-note listening at B1 but with shorter recordings and less paraphrase. The reporting passive is entirely new as production and will be recognised only vaguely.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 1–9 (listening — the note-completion done today is the shape of tasks 3–9) and tasks 19–25.

Today's listening is a radio programme with gapped notes: read the gaps first, predict the word class, write only what you hear, spell it right. That is exactly the discipline the Russian listening section rewards, and spelling really is marked. The grammar half — it is said that …, he is believed to have … — is the register the exam's academic texts are written in, so it pays twice: in tasks 19–25 and in comprehension.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
