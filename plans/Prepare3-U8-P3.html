🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use the collocations that talk about enjoying yourself — have a great time, spend time doing, be glad, enjoy yourself, a fun day out — and interview somebody about an unusual hobby with at least five questions.
What is genuinely hard
These are chunks, not words. Every one of them breaks in a different place: enjoy needs an object, fun and funny are not the same word, and spend time is followed by ‑ing. Learning the individual words does not help at all.
Typical error to expect
We had a funny time instead of a fun time: funny = makes you laugh, fun = enjoyable. Unit 15 teaches both in the same word box [A2/B1]
I enjoyed with nothing after it, or I enjoyed me — enjoy needs an object: enjoy yourself, enjoy the party [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary is taken from a text they have already read, so the first task is finding, not learning — five phrases that were under their nose yesterday. Personalisation comes immediately and doubles as rehearsal: the six half-sentences of ex.3 are the raw material of the interview at the end. The listening sits in the middle deliberately: Libby is a real teenager talking about a genuinely strange hobby, which is exactly the register the students are about to produce, and her first answer is a for two years that ties yesterday's grammar into today's speaking. The last third is one long productive task built in three steps — build the questions, rehearse with a coach, perform — because a performance without a rehearsal is just reading aloud. Correction is delayed to the very end and kept anonymous.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Libby is fifteen and she keeps sixty thousand bees in her garden. She has done it for two years. In an hour, one of you is going to be somebody with a hobby that strange, and the other one is going to be the journalist who has to get five good questions out. Choose your side now.”

0–5′
Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a fast trading round on for and since, and the bridge into the vocabulary of enjoying yourself

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — rewind on the grammar, out loud. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with cards that say only a time expression: 2019 · two hours · I was five · a long time · Monday · ages. The partner must say FOR or SINCE and then use it in a true sentence.
  3. Two trades each, then sit. It costs three minutes and it is the only revision yesterday's grammar needs before it is used for real today.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page eighty-nine. Yesterday you asked how long. Today: was it any good?
📺 On screen: printed time-expression cards — the cards in their hands are the medium, so no backup is needed; if the set has gone missing, read the expressions out and students answer chorally
👦 Students do: trade cards and produce a true sentence each time. Expect the choice to be automatic now and the sentence to be the slow part. Typical problem: ages again — it is the one nobody keeps

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the outcome of the lesson first, because everything else is preparation for it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the outcome on the board before anything else: By ten past eleven, two of you are at the front of this room. One of you keeps bees. The other one is from a newspaper.
  2. Under it, the standard: Five questions minimum. No question begins with «Do you like».
  3. Say the language aim in one line: today's words are all about enjoying yourself, and every one of them is two or three words long — the mistake is never the word, it is the chunk.
  4. Two sentences on the board as a taster of exactly that: We had a fun day. and We had a funny day. Same day? Let them argue for twenty seconds.
📺 On screen: the outcome, the standard and the two taster sentences in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: read the outcome and argue about fun and funny. Expect the two sentences to split the room, which is the intended effect. Typical problem: someone dreads performing — say now that the interview is rehearsed twice before anybody stands up

14–26′
Vocabulary · having fun
pairs → class
Why: Collocations, not words: the mistake is never fun, it is have a funny time. They are learnt as whole chunks or they are not learnt at all.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.89: find the phrases in yesterday's blog, then complete seven sentences

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed first (TB p.174): write I liked it. and This is good. on the board and ask for more interesting ways of saying the same thing. Take whatever comes.
  2. Books open, ex.1: the phrase table, and one instruction — go back to Lucy's blog on p.88 and underline the ones that are actually in it. 3 minutes. Key: the phrases that are in the blog: a really fun hobby · I'm so glad · We have a great time · It's a fantastic feeling · It's such a great way.
  3. That is five phrases they read yesterday without noticing, which is worth saying out loud: You have already read all five of these.
  4. Go through the table itself: have + fun / a great time / a laugh · spend time + with a friend / doing a hobby · be or feel + glad / happy · enjoy + yourself / an activity · a fun, brilliant, exciting, fantastic + feeling / day out / hobby.
  5. ex.2 in pairs, 4 minutes: seven sentences, the correct form of have, spend, be or enjoy. Key: 1 had · 2 Spending · 3 enjoys · 4 spent, was · 5 have · 6 was/is, was/am · 7 enjoyed.
  6. Two CCQs on the two that break: «I enjoyed.» Finished sentence? «We had a funny time.» Is that what you meant?
📺 On screen: the collocation table on the screen so the whole class builds the same phrase at the same time (Backup: it is printed on SB p.89 — copy the four verbs onto the board as four columns and let students come up and write the endings into them)
👦 Students do: find five phrases in yesterday's text, then complete seven sentences. Expect ex.2 items 4 and 6 (two gaps each) to be the slow ones. Typical error: I enjoyed at the party — enjoy takes the object directly, with no preposition

26–34′
Vocabulary · your own good times
solo → pairs
Why: Six half-sentences finished about their own lives, then compared. It is a personalisation stage that doubles as a rehearsal for the interview at the end.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.89: six half-sentences finished so that they are true for you

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Model two yourself, out loud and specifically (TB p.174 gives the shape): It was a fantastic feeling when I finished the ten-kilometre run last weekend. My idea of a fun day out is a long walk and somebody else cooking.
  2. Solo, 4 minutes, all six. Specific beats general: a party is weak, my cousin's party in August is an answer.
  3. Kagan Timed Pair Share, ninety seconds each way. The listener must ask one follow-up question per sentence — Who were you with? What time did you finish?
  4. Take three of the best to the class. Say explicitly what has just happened: Those follow-up questions are the interview. You have just done the hard part.
📺 On screen: the six sentence beginnings copied onto the board so nobody keeps turning the page — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: finish six personal sentences and answer follow-ups. Expect the follow-up questions to be better than they were in lesson 1, because they now have question shapes. Typical error: general answers — push once for a detail and the whole class raises its level

34–45′
Listening · Libby the beekeeper
solo → pairs
Why: A real teenager with a strange hobby, talking the way the students are about to. The right/wrong task keeps the listening honest; the how long question ties it to the grammar.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.89 (track 116): a girl talks about beekeeping — gist first, then seven right/wrong statements

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Point at the photo at the bottom of the page and pre-teach one word (TB p.174): hive — the box a colony of bees lives in. Ask one question before playing: Why would anybody want sixty thousand bees?
  2. Play track 116. Task one only, two questions: A girl called Libby talks about her hobby — beekeeping. First: who is she talking to, and how long has she done it? Then: seven right/wrong statements. Key: She is speaking to her class. She has done her hobby for two years..
  3. Stop on the second answer and point at it: Question 1 of the first task is the how long question of this whole unit, and it is answered with for two years. Point at it after the check: the grammar is not an exercise, it is how people actually say things.
  4. Read the seven statements aloud, then play track 116 again. Key: 1 ✗ · 2 ✗ · 3 ✓ · 4 ✓ · 5 ✓ · 6 ✗ · 7 ✗.
  5. Kagan Numbered Heads Together to check: Statement four — number twos, boards up!
  6. Name the trap once: Where marks go: statements 6 and 7 — students hear the words lots of bees and honey and tick without listening to what Libby says about them.
  7. One closing question that bridges into the last third (TB p.174): How would Libby finish «It was a fantastic feeling when …»?
📺 On screen: track 116 in the board app with the seven statements on the screen (Backup: the audioscript is on TB p.301 — read Libby's monologue aloud yourself, twice, at natural speed; the statements are printed in the students' own book)
👦 Students do: listen twice and mark seven statements. Expect 5 of 7. Typical error: statements 6 and 7 — the words honey and lots of bees are both said, and students tick on the word rather than on what Libby says about it

Move break
standing
Why: A stirrer that also forces one English sentence. Teenagers who have been sitting for forty minutes need to stand before the second half, or the second half dies.

WHAT WE DO: ninety seconds of movement and a switch into the speaking half

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. Find somebody you have not spoken to today. Tell them one true thing, in one sentence, that begins «I really enjoy…». Then sit down.
  2. Nothing is collected and nothing is corrected — the point is to get everybody's voice working before the interviews.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: cross the room and produce one sentence each. Expect the room to be standing and talking within ten seconds. Typical error: I really enjoy to play — leave it, the correction slot is at the end

47–56′
Interview · building the questions
pairs
Why: Questions are built before roles are chosen, so that nobody spends four minutes deciding what their hobby is and no minutes on the language.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.89: choose the hobby, choose the roles, and write five questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Put the eight hobbies on the board before pairs are formed: dog training · fencing · jewellery making · remote-controlled vehicles — or a hobby of their own, plus beekeeping, geocaching, baking bread and collecting old coins.
  2. Assign roles the easy way, and do it deliberately: TB p.174: let the weaker student in the pair be the one who ANSWERS — answering is easier than asking. Collect unusual hobbies on the board first so that no pair spends four minutes choosing. Send them back to the flow chart in ex.6 on SB p.87 for question shapes, and remind them about the weak forms they did last lesson.
  3. The seven question starters from the book go on the board and stay there: Tell me about… · How long have you…? · When did you…? · How do you feel about…? · Why did you…? · How did you get the idea to…? · Are you glad you…?
  4. Pairs write five questions, 5 minutes. The one rule from the lesson frame: No question begins with «Do you like». Every one of these seven starters is better.
  5. Walk and fix broken questions NOW — this is the last moment where a correction helps rather than interrupts. The commonest repair is How long time → How long have you.
📺 On screen: the eight hobbies and the seven question starters in chalk — the board is the medium here and both lists stay up until the performances are over, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: choose a hobby and write five questions. Expect the questions to need one repair each. Typical error: all five questions in the present simple — point at the starters, which are mostly present perfect and past simple

56–66′
Interview · rehearsal
pairs → fours
Why: A rehearsal with a coach is the difference between a performance and a reading. This is also the only stage where you can still fix a broken question.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.89: run the interview twice, once with a coach listening

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. First run, in the pair, 3 minutes. Nothing is written; they just do it once to find out where it breaks.
  2. Then join pairs into fours. Kagan RallyCoach: pair A performs, pair B has exactly two jobs — count the questions, and note one answer that was too short.
  3. Feedback inside the four, thirty seconds, using one sentence each: You asked four questions, not five. / Question three got one word.
  4. Swap and repeat, 3 minutes.
  5. The instruction that raises the whole level: An answer of one sentence is not an answer. Two sentences minimum, and one of them says why.
📺 On screen: the count-the-questions and too-short-answer checklist written on the board as two lines — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: rehearse twice and coach another pair. Expect the second run to be noticeably longer and calmer. Typical error: the interviewee answers everything with a single sentence — the coach's second job is exactly this
SB p.89 Speaking ex.2 · board
66–80′
🎯 Interviews on stage
front of the class
Why: The productive outcome: a real audience, a real listening task for everybody else, and language that has to survive being heard.

WHAT WE DO: the performances: ninety seconds a pair, with a listening task for everybody else

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the audience task before the first pair stands up, otherwise the audience is not an audience: Everybody else: write down ONE fact from each interview. At the end I will ask you for somebody else's fact, not your own.
  2. Ninety seconds per pair, timed visibly. Raise a hand at sixty seconds so nobody is cut off mid-sentence.
  3. Say nothing at all during a performance. Log every error silently on your correction sheet with the student's initial — you will need it in six minutes and you will not use the initial out loud.
  4. After each pair, one question from the audience, not from you.
  5. At the end, Kagan Numbered Heads Together on the listening task: Number threes — one fact about the beekeeper. Number ones — one fact about the fencer.
📺 On screen: a visible timer for the ninety seconds and the audience task written on the board (Backup: use the wall clock and call the time yourself; the audience task is on the board anyway and the interviews need no technology at all)
👦 Students do: perform for ninety seconds and take notes on everybody else. Expect the audience task to keep the room genuinely quiet, which is the only thing that does. Typical error: the journalist reads the five questions off the page without looking up — worth one whole-class reminder before the second pair

80–86′
Delayed correction
class
Why: Errors logged silently during the performances are worked on afterwards, anonymously. Correcting during a performance kills the performance and teaches nothing.

WHAT WE DO: six errors from the performances, anonymous, repaired by the class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Six sentences on the board, exactly as they were said, with no names and no tone of voice. Choose them: two collocation errors, two question forms, one for/since and one third person.
  2. Six sentences. All of them were said in this room in the last quarter of an hour. Repair them in pairs. Two minutes.
  3. Take the repairs one at a time and write the correct version next to the wrong one in a second colour. Never say who produced it — the rule is absolute and the students know it.
  4. Finish with the one that matters most for the block, and let the class say the rule: «I enjoyed» — what is missing?
  5. Say the honest thing about what just happened: Every one of these came out of a conversation that worked. That is what a mistake in this room is.
📺 On screen: the six sentences in chalk with space beside each for the repair — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair six sentences in pairs. Expect the collocation errors to be the hardest to spot, because they sound fine. Typical error: they correct the vocabulary and miss the missing auxiliary in the question

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can talk about enjoying themselves using whole collocations and conduct and answer a five-question interview about a hobby.
  • Subsidiary. Listening for gist and then for detail in a monologue; asking follow-up questions; performing in front of an audience with a listening task.
  • Personal. Delay every correction to the last stage. An interview stopped in the middle for a missing ‑s is not an interview any more, and the students who most need to speak are the ones who stop first.
By the end, students can…
  • say they had a good time using four different phrases
  • ask at least five questions about somebody's hobby
  • answer a question about a hobby in more than one sentence
  • listen to a classmate's interview and report one fact from it

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«We had a funny time.» Is that what you mean? → probably not. funny means it made you laugh; fun means you enjoyed it.
«I enjoyed.» Is that a sentence? → no. Enjoy needs something after it: yourself, the party, playing football.
«I spend time to play the guitar.» What is wrong? → after spend time the verb takes ‑ing: spend time playing.
In the interview, if your partner answers with one word, what do you do? → ask another question. A journalist with a one-word answer has failed, not the interviewee.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Nobody can think of an unusual hobby and four minutes disappearDo not let them choose from nothing. The board gets a list before the pairs are formed: the four from the book (dog training, fencing, jewellery making, remote-controlled vehicles) plus four you add (beekeeping, geocaching, baking bread, collecting old coins). Choosing from eight takes ten seconds.
The strong student takes the journalist role in every pairReverse it deliberately, as TB p.174 advises: the weaker student ANSWERS, because answering is easier than asking. Say it as a casual instruction, not as a differentiation: Whoever is nearest the window is the journalist this time.
The performances run over and the correction slot disappearsTime them visibly: ninety seconds per interview, a hand raised at sixty. Six pairs is nine minutes. If the class is bigger, half the pairs perform and the other half perform next lesson — the correction slot is not the thing to cut.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
