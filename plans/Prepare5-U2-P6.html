🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Read a long opinion text and answer five four-option questions about detail, attitude and the text as a whole (B1 Reading Part 3), proving every answer from an underlined line and rejecting the other three options out loud.
What is genuinely hard
Four options instead of three means the wrong ones are built with more care: each contains something the writer really says, twisted by one word. The last question — which message would he send? — asks about the whole text, and students answer it from the last paragraph they happen to remember.
Typical error to expect
Choosing the option that repeats the text's words — option A in question 1 mentions many nationalities, which is in the text, and is still wrong.
Answering the whole-text question from one paragraph — question 5 can only be answered by holding the entire article in your head.
Why the lesson is built this way
The papers come back at the start, so the lesson opens with three minutes of real feedback and then changes gear. The exam task is taught as a fixed three-step procedure, demonstrated by you on question 1 with all four options killed aloud, then done under a clock. The autopsy is the heart of the lesson and it is a pair task with a written outcome, using the Teacher's Book's own reasons. The productive stage turns the task inside out: pairs write their own four-option question on the same text, which is the fastest way to see how a distractor is manufactured.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Five questions, four options each, one long text. Nineteen of those twenty options are wrong — and each one is wrong for a reason you can name. Today we learn to name them.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, marked papers back with specific feedback, and the switch into the exam page

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what's the best thing about today so far? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — the papers come back. Hand back both tests with section totals written on them. One specific positive sentence about the class (The weather vocabulary was the strongest section — nobody lost a mark on earthquake or lightning) and one specific instruction for block 3.
  3. Sixty seconds in pairs: find ONE mistake on your paper that you can explain to your partner. Not all of them — one.
  4. Take two explanations aloud. If the same error appears twice, put it on the board and repair it as a class.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): change gear: Papers away. Page one hundred and twenty-two. One long text, five questions, four options each — and a method that works every time.
📺 On screen: the two class results summarised in two lines on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: look at their own paper with a job rather than a mark to feel. Explaining one error aloud beats reading twenty red marks

Lesson frame

Why: Exam technique is only technique if it is a fixed procedure the students can name, and it has to be the same three steps every exam lesson of the year.

WHAT WE DO: name the task and put up the three-step method

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the method and leave it up all lesson: 1 READ the question. 2 UNDERLINE the line that answers it. 3 KILL the other three options — say why each one fails.
  2. Say the shape in 30 seconds: read the text, dissect one question together, do four alone against the clock, then take the wrong options apart.
  3. Set the bar: Today, «I think it's B» is not an answer. «It's B, line four, and A is wrong because…» is an answer.
📺 On screen: the three-step method on the board — it stays up all lesson, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: copy the three steps into their notebooks under the strategy card from block 1

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: gist reading under a clock, plus the four words that unlock it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the scene in one sentence: «Travelling with a backpack» by Andy Robinson — why the people you meet backpacking are more interesting than your friends at home, why short friendships can be as strong as long ones, how to pack light (old clothes you throw away, shampoo from the hostel, a waterproof backpack), and why backpackers are good for small places and kind to the environment.
  2. Pre-teach exactly four words on the board: hostel, destination, travel light, waterproof. Ninety seconds, no more.
  3. ex.1: three minutes, silent, whole text, one question only — does the writer enjoy backpacking? Clock visible.
  4. Kagan Think-Pair-Share, sixty seconds: what are the four things he likes about it? Collect four headlines on the board — the people, the friendships, the packing, the places. Those four headlines are the map for the questions.
📺 On screen: the four pre-taught words and the four headlines on the board. Backup: the article is on the page and needs nothing else
👦 Students do: read against a clock, then build the map of the text in pairs

Question 1 dissected

Why: Question 1 dissected

WHAT WE DO: ex.2: the highlighted evidence, and all four options killed aloud

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the exam tip aloud: Read the question and then the text. Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer. Then check that the other three options are definitely wrong.
  2. Question 1 is printed with the evidence already highlighted in the text — use that. Read the question, read the highlighted lines, and take the answer from the class.
  3. Now the important part, and do it slowly: question 1 — D. A is wrong because he says he has spent time with people of MANY nationalities; B is wrong because he never says sharing is difficult, only that you may be unlucky; C is wrong because he enjoys visiting sights WITH them, but never says they know more
  4. Write the three rejection reasons on the board in the students' own words. This is the model for everything they will do for the rest of the lesson.
  5. Pairs, ninety seconds: which single word in each wrong option makes it wrong? (one of a few particular countries · difficult · know more). Name it: One word. That's all a distractor ever is.
📺 On screen: question 1, its three rejections and the killer words on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: watch one question taken apart completely, then find the single word that breaks each wrong option

34–38′
Move break · true, twisted, invented
standing, three walls
Why: Move break · true, twisted, invented

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that teaches distractor logic physically

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three signs on three walls: TRUE · TWISTED · INVENTED.
  2. Read out eight statements about the backpacking text. Everyone walks to the wall they believe: it is exactly what the text says, it is the text's words with one thing changed, or it never appeared at all.
  3. One reason from one student after each statement, then move on. Do not linger.
  4. Freeze and sit: Every wrong option in every exam you'll ever take is TWISTED, not INVENTED. That's why they look right.
📺 On screen: three A4 signs and eight statements prepared in advance — nothing to fail
👦 Students do: move, decide, justify. The three-way sort makes the design of a distractor visible

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: four questions, eight minutes, no help

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Eight minutes, silent, individual, clock on the board. Say the honest timing: In the real paper this whole task takes about twelve minutes. We're doing four questions in eight so there's time to take them apart afterwards.
  2. The requirement is unchanged and it is written on the board: every answer underlined in the text.
  3. Do not help. Circulate silently and note who is underlining and who is not — that is the diagnostic you actually need.
  4. At seven minutes: One minute. Question five is about the whole text — if you're stuck, skim all four paragraphs again.
📺 On screen: the clock and the underlining rule on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: work alone under exam conditions with a visible clock

48–66′
The autopsy · nineteen wrong options
pairs → class
Why: The autopsy · nineteen wrong options

WHAT WE DO: every rejected option explained, out loud, by the students

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. First, check the four answers only — letters, no discussion: 2 C, 3 A, 4 B, 5 C. Let the room react.
  2. Then the real work. Assign one question per pair (four pairs minimum, double up in a big group). Their job: write ONE sentence for EACH wrong option saying why it fails.
  3. Six minutes. Circulate with the Teacher's Book reasons in hand and feed them only to pairs that stall.
  4. Each pair presents its question. The class marks the explanation with thumbs: convincing or not convincing.
  5. Spend the longest on question 5, the whole-text one. Line up the four messages and match each to the paragraph it contradicts — luxury hotels, a bag he can't lift, hiring a car. That is the clearest demonstration of a whole-text question in the book.
  6. Close with a count: Twenty options, one right. You have just explained nineteen of them.
📺 On screen: the four questions and their rejection sentences built up on the board as pairs present — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: each pair owns a question and teaches it. Nobody sits through four explanations delivered by the teacher
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE tasks 10–18 (the long text). Explaining why three options fail is the single most transferable reading habit in the whole year.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: after the pairs present, put two Russian-format questions on the same text on the board and give five minutes. Take the time from the write-your-own-question stage.

pairs → class
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · write your own question

WHAT WE DO: students manufacture an exam question with a working distractor

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. In pairs, write ONE new four-option question about the same text. Rules on the board: one option is true and answers the question · one is TWISTED (the text's words, one thing changed) · one is true but answers a different question · one is invented.
  2. Six minutes. Circulate and push on the twisted option — that is the one that teaches.
  3. Swap questions with another pair. Answer theirs, then say which option was the twisted one and what the twist was.
  4. Take the three best questions and put them to the whole class. Vote on the most convincing distractor of the day.
  5. Close the loop out loud: Somebody is paid to do exactly this to you in June. Now you know how they do it.
📺 On screen: the four-option recipe on the board while they write — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: manufacture a question and a distractor, then solve another pair's. Everyone writes and everyone reads for evidence

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.» If you can't underline it, do you have an answer? → no, you have a guess.
Option A says «people of many nationalities» and so does the text. Does that make it right? → no. Shared words are the trap.
Question 5 asks which message he would send. Which part of the text answers it? → all of it. That is what makes it the hardest question.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students answer from general impressionNo letter is accepted without an underlined line, and the requirement is enforced from question 1 onwards, including when the answer is right.
The text is long and the weaker readers stallThe first reading has one simple question (does he enjoy backpacking?) and a three-minute clock. Comprehension of the whole argument is built by the questions, not demanded before them.
The autopsy becomes the teacher talkingEach pair owns one question and writes one sentence explaining one wrong option. You hold the Teacher's Book reasons and feed them only to pairs that stall.
«Is this the Russian exam?»One honest sentence — the same skill, differently packaged — and move on. The detail lives in the note at the foot of this plan, for you, not for the lesson.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 10–18 (the long reading text with multiple-choice questions) — the closest match in the whole Russian paper to today's task.

Reading Part 3 and the Russian exam's long-text section test the same three things: detail, global meaning, and the writer's attitude. The method taught today transfers line for line — underline the part of the text that gives the answer, then prove the other three options wrong. Students who cannot say why B is wrong have not answered the question, they have guessed it.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
