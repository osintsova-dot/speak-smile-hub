🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Answer personal questions at the length the question asks for — one or two sentences for most, three for a «Tell me about …» — and never in a single word.
What is genuinely hard
Not the language. The instinct. A nervous fifteen-year-old answers Do you like reading? with No and stops, and in that moment the examiner has heard no English at all. Producing more than was asked for feels rude, and it has to be taught as a rule rather than as advice.
Typical error to expect
A one-word reply («Karate.», «Yes.») does not count as an answer at all: an answer needs a verb in it [national exam report]
«I don't know» / «I haven't got a favourite» and full stop — a bare negative is marked as an unanswered point; it needs development: …but I like chess [exam report]
Why you like it? / What you do after school? — the missing auxiliary do/does [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
This is the one lesson of the block where the exam format IS the topic, so it is framed from the first minute as «one skill, two wrappings» and the children are told plainly that some of them will sit a Russian exam and some will not. The teaching sequence is deliberately not «here are the tips»: the class sorts twelve questions by the length of answer they need, which is the only move in this part that actually transfers, and then judges five real answers, three of which are bad. Hearing why an answer fails stops them producing it. The recorded answers come after that judgement, as a model bank rather than as a test. The pair practice rotates twice, because the second interview is always visibly better and that improvement is the evidence the lesson worked. The last third opens the Russian version of the same thing on the screen, live, so the transfer is experienced and not promised — and the honest limitation of the school app is stated in the plan rather than discovered at 68 minutes.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“One skill, two wrappings. In about an hour you will do a Cambridge task and then the Russian version of the same task, and you will see that only the packaging changed. One rule for today, and it is the whole lesson: an answer of one word is not a wrong answer. It is no answer.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, three minutes of collective feedback on the block test, and the bridge into the exam lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — test feedback, not a post-mortem. Hand the papers back face down. On the board, the three errors that cost the class the most marks, no names. Turn your paper over. Find one of these three in your own work. That is your line for today.
  3. Nothing else about the test today. Scores are not read out, and the five-mistake rewrite is the homework from last lesson, not a new task.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page one hundred and thirty-three. Today is the one lesson in this block about exams — and about the fact that there are two of them.
📺 On screen: the three most expensive errors written in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: take their papers back and find one of the three in their own work. Expect relief that no score is announced. Typical problem: somebody wants to argue a mark — one line, «see me after», and move on

WHAT WE DO: the map of the lesson: the same three minutes of talking, asked two different ways

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Draw two boxes on the board with an arrow between them. In the left one: An examiner asks you about your school and your free time. Three to four minutes. In the right one, the same words. One of these is British. One is Russian. Which is which? They cannot tell — that is the point.
  2. Say the honest sentence, once, and do not soften it: Some of you will take a Russian exam in two years. Some of you won't. Today's skill is for both of you, because it is the same skill.
  3. Then the promise that pays off at 68 minutes: At the end of the lesson we will open the Russian version on the screen and you will see how similar it is.
  4. And the thing that lowers the temperature immediately: TB p.258: this part has already been met in the Student's Book on pages 13, 31, 93, 97 and 119 — that is Unit 1, Unit 3, Unit 16, Review 4 and Review 5. Say that out loud: it is not a new task, it is the fifth time.
  5. The plan of the lesson in three lines on the board: sort the questions → judge the answers → do it, twice, and then in Russian.
📺 On screen: the two boxes and the three-line plan in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: compare the two boxes and fail to tell them apart. Expect one student to ask which exam they will take — answer factually, in one sentence, and move on. Typical problem: anxiety; the «fifth time, not the first time» line is what removes it

Speaking Part 1 · the format
class
Why: The format is two sentences long, so it gets two minutes. What deserves the time is the difference between a question that wants a short answer and one that wants a long one.

WHAT WE DO: the exam information and tips boxes p.133, and the board demonstration that matters

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The format in two lines: «There are questions about you. You need to talk to the examiner and answer the questions you are asked.» Twelve questions are printed; the real part lasts three to four minutes.
  2. The four tips from the book's own box, one line each: listen carefully — you and your partner get questions on different topics · you may ask the examiner to say the question again · when the examiner says Tell me about …, answer in two or three sentences · this part takes three to four minutes
  3. Then the demonstration that does the actual teaching (TB p.258): TB p.258, before the book is opened: write two questions on the board — How old will you be on your next birthday? and What do you usually do at the weekends? — and ask which needs a short answer and which needs a long one. That single comparison is the whole lesson in thirty seconds.
  4. Take answers for thirty seconds before confirming. The first question wants a number; the second wants a description.
  5. Write the conclusion on the board and leave it up for the whole lesson: The question tells you how long the answer is. Read the question.
  6. One CCQ to close the stage: «Do you like reading?» — is «No» an answer?
📺 On screen: the two board questions and the conclusion in chalk — the board is the medium here and the conclusion stays up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: compare two questions and work out the difference. Expect them to get it in one, which is fine — the point is that it is now written down. Typical problem: they think «longer» means «harder»; it means «two more sentences»

18–28′
Short answer or long answer
pairs → class
Why: Sorting twelve questions by the length of answer they need is the single most useful move in this part, and it takes ten minutes to learn for good.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.133: sort all twelve questions by the length of answer they need

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the twelve questions aloud once, at examiner speed, so they hear them before they read them: SB p.133 ex.1: What's your name? · Where do you come from? · What's your best subject at school? · Do you like studying science? · What do you wear to school? · How much homework do you get? · Tell me something about your favourite teacher. · How much free time do you have? · Who do you spend your free time with? · Do you play computer games? · Do you like reading? · Tell me something about a hobby you enjoy.
  2. Pairs, 5 minutes: mark each question S for short or L for long. Kagan Think-Pair-Share — think alone for one minute first, then compare.
  3. Reveal and confirm: TB p.258: questions 7 and 12 — the two Tell me about … questions — are the ones that need a long answer. Aim for three sentences. Everything else can be answered in one or two, but never in one word.
  4. Ask the follow-up that makes it useful rather than a sorting exercise: What do questions seven and twelve have in common? (both begin «Tell me something about»).
  5. Then the second half of the point, which is the one they will forget: The short ones are short. They are not one word.
  6. Collect with Kagan Showdown: S or L on the mini-whiteboard, all twelve in a row, boards up together.
📺 On screen: the twelve questions on the screen so the class marks the same one at the same time (Backup: they are printed on SB p.133 — number a column on the board 1 to 12 and write S or L against each as the class calls it out)
👦 Students do: sort twelve questions and justify the two long ones. Expect 10 of 12 agreed immediately and questions 3 and 11 to be argued about. Typical error: they mark everything L to be safe — point at question 1, which is a name

28–40′
Five answers taken apart
pairs → class
Why: Five real answers, three of them bad, judged by the students themselves. Hearing why an answer fails is what stops them producing it.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.133: match five real answers to their questions, then judge them

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The five answers are read aloud, deadpan, without comment: «No, I don't.» · «No, not really. I find it quite difficult.» · «Just my normal clothes. I don't have to wear a uniform.» · «She's nice. I like her.» · «I haven't got any hobbies.»
  2. Pairs, 4 minutes: match each answer to a question from ex.1, then decide — good answer or not so good, and why.
  3. Kagan RallyCoach: the partner may only accept a verdict with a reason attached.
  4. Reveal and go through them one at a time: SB p.133 ex.2, why each printed answer is good or not (TB p.258): a «No, I don't.» — an answer to a do you question like 4, 10 or 11, and not good enough: you have to say why. b «No, not really. I find it quite difficult.» — the same kind of question answered properly. c «Just my normal clothes. I don't have to wear a uniform.» — question 5, good, because it gives plenty of detail. d «She's nice. I like her.» — question 7, too short: say why. e «I haven't got any hobbies.» — question 12, the worst answer on the page: it lets the examiner hear none of your English. Even «I just go online» is better — or make something up.
  5. Spend the longest on e, because it is the one that matters: «I haven't got any hobbies.» It is true, it is polite, it is grammatically perfect — and it is the worst answer on this page. Why?
  6. Let the class answer that themselves. The sentence you are fishing for is: the examiner heard no English.
📺 On screen: the five answers on the screen, revealed one at a time as the class judges them (Backup: they are printed on SB p.133 — copy the five onto the board before the lesson and uncover them one by one with a sheet of paper)
👦 Students do: match five answers and judge each one. Expect answer c to be identified as good immediately and answer d to be argued about. Typical error: they judge on politeness rather than on quantity of English — that distinction is the stage

40–45′
What counts as a good answer
class
Why: Four criteria, written down and left on the board, so that the pair practice has a standard to be measured against rather than a vague «say more».

WHAT WE DO: the four criteria, written down, before any pair practice happens

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ask the question before giving the answer (TB p.258): What makes a good answer to a question? Take four suggestions.
  2. Then put the book's four on the board, and leave them there for the rest of the lesson: TB p.258, what makes a good answer: not too short · plenty of detail · correct grammar · interesting vocabulary.
  3. Turn them into a checklist the students can actually use on each other: Long enough? Any detail? Any mistakes? Any interesting words?
  4. Two minutes on intonation, because it is invisible and it costs marks: say She's nice twice — once with a falling tone (finished) and once with a rising one (unfinished). The class copies both.
  5. If you stop on a rise, the examiner thinks you gave up.
📺 On screen: the four criteria in chalk as a numbered checklist — the board is the medium here and they stay up for the rest of the lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: suggest criteria, then copy the four. Expect «correct grammar» to be their first suggestion and «interesting vocabulary» to be nobody's. Typical problem: they think detail means length; one example fixes it

🎯 Ask and answer, rotating
pairs, twice
Why: The productive outcome: every student is interviewed twice by two different partners, so the second attempt is visibly better than the first.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 p.133: take turns asking and answering, then change partner and do it again

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Round one, 4 minutes: A is the examiner and asks questions 1–6, B answers. Then swap for questions 7–12. Kagan Timed Pair Share.
  2. The examiner has one extra job and it is the whole reason for the rotation: Tick the four criteria on the board after every answer. Say nothing while your partner is talking.
  3. Thirty seconds of feedback inside the pair, one sentence each: Your answer to question eight was one word.
  4. Round two, new partner, 4 minutes, the same twelve questions. It is deliberately the same set — the improvement is the point, not the novelty.
  5. Then say what just happened, because they will not notice it themselves: Hands up if your second time was better than your first. Nearly every hand goes up.
  6. Two students demonstrate at the front for one minute. The class ticks the four criteria for them.
📺 On screen: the four criteria and the twelve questions both still on the board so neither partner has to look down (Backup: the questions are printed on SB p.133 — keep the book open between the two students and keep only the four criteria on the board)
👦 Students do: interview each other twice with different partners. Expect the second round to be visibly longer and calmer. Typical error: the examiner corrects mid-answer — the «say nothing while your partner is talking» rule is what stops it

🎯 The OGE version
class, on the screen
Why: The transfer stage: the same skill in the Russian wrapper, experienced rather than promised. Six recorded questions, forty seconds each, no partner and no second chance.

WHAT WE DO: the same skill in the Russian wrapper: six recorded questions, forty seconds each

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Open the school trainer on the screen and be straight about what you are opening. ⚠️ The trainer has no speaking module that can be sent as a homework link — its speaking tasks live only inside the full mock at #/mock, which sits behind the teacher's own access. So this stage runs on your login, not theirs, and it is a demonstration rather than a task they take home.
  2. Open #/mock, choose any variant, and go to speaking task 2. Read out what the screen says the rules are: six questions, forty seconds for each, the recording plays once and there is no partner in the room.
  3. Say the comparison plainly, in one breath: The Russian counterpart is speaking task 2: six questions from a recorded interviewer, forty seconds to answer each, six marks. The differences are worth naming honestly: the questions come from a recording, so you cannot ask for them again; there is no partner in the room; and the clock is visible. Everything else — a one-word answer scores zero, the answer must contain a verb, the tense of the answer must match the tense of the question — is identical.
  4. Do questions 1 and 2 together as a whole class, out loud, with the timer running on the screen. The forty seconds is the shock — it is much longer than they expect and it is what makes a one-word answer visibly absurd.
  5. Questions 3–6 in pairs, one screen or one phone between two, 4 minutes.
  6. Name the two real differences and nothing more: the questions come from a recording so you cannot ask again, and there is no partner. Everything else you did in the last twenty minutes.
📺 On screen: the trainer open on the screen at #/mock, speaking task 2, on the teacher's own login (Backup: print the speaking card in advance from the teacher mode of the app; if there is no sound at all, read the six questions aloud yourself with a phone timer set to forty seconds — the format survives completely without the recording, because the recording is only a voice reading questions)
👦 Students do: do two questions with you and four in pairs, against a forty-second clock. Expect them to be surprised by how long forty seconds is, which is exactly the aim. Typical error: they stop at fifteen seconds and look up — say «keep going» and they find two more sentences every time

Transfer · write the interviewer's questions
pairs
Why: Writing the questions is what proves the format is understood. A student who can build the task can survive the task.

WHAT WE DO: the transfer task: they build the exam instead of sitting it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The task in one line: You are the examiner. Write six questions for a student you have never met. Two of them must be «Tell me about» questions.
  2. Pairs, 4 minutes. The constraint does the teaching — the two long questions cannot be written without understanding what makes a question long.
  3. Swap the six questions with another pair and answer theirs, cold, one round only.
  4. Two pairs read their best question to the class, and the class votes on which one would get the most English out of a candidate.
  5. Close the loop with the sentence the whole lesson has been building to: A good question is one that makes it hard to answer in one word.
📺 On screen: the two required question types written on the board as a checklist — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write six examiner questions and answer somebody else's. Expect the «Tell me about» questions to be the good ones. Typical error: six yes/no questions in a row — the two-long-questions constraint is what prevents it

Structure
No, not really. I find it quite difficult. · Just my normal clothes — I don't have to wear a uniform. · She's nice. She explains things twice and she never shouts. · I play the guitar. I've played it for two years and I practise most evenings.
Form
the answer's tense matches the question's tense · a do you question takes a short answer PLUS a reason · a Tell me about question takes three sentences · never answer with the bare word.
Pron. watch
the falling intonation of a finished answer, and the rising one of an unfinished one. Students who stop on a rise sound as if they gave up; two minutes of copying you is enough to fix it.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Do you like reading?» — is «No» an answer? → no. It is a word. The answer is «No, not really — I find it quite difficult.»
«Tell me something about your favourite teacher.» How many sentences? → three. That is what «tell me about» means in this exam.
«I haven't got any hobbies.» Why is that the worst answer on the page? → because the examiner hears no English at all. Even an invented hobby scores more.
In the Russian version, can you ask for the question again? → no. It comes from a recording and it is said once. That is the only real difference that matters.

The differences are worth naming honestly: the questions come from a recording, so you cannot ask for them again; there is no partner in the room; and the clock is visible. Everything else — a one-word answer scores zero, the answer must contain a verb, the tense of the answer must match the tense of the question — is identical.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
