🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Mark their own Cambridge Reading and Listening papers against the official keys, classify every loss as a slip, a gap or a misreading, and leave with three named, fixable lines plus a map of where each part of the paper lands in the Russian exam.
What is genuinely hard
A returned paper teaches almost nothing on its own, because a mark is a verdict and a verdict cannot be acted on. What can be acted on is a classification: this one I knew and wrote badly, this one I have never known, this one I read wrongly. Three problems with three different cures — and teenagers do not make the distinction unless they are made to. On this paper there is a fourth category nobody expects: marks lost on the SHEET, in boxes, spelling and capital letters, by students whose English was right.
Typical error to expect
«I was just careless». Sometimes true, usually a way of avoiding the second category. Today's rule: a slip is something you can correct in five seconds without looking anything up. If you cannot, it is a gap and it goes in the log.
Reading the key and moving on. An answer that has been read is not an answer that has been understood — every wrong item gets a WHY written beside it, in any language.
Why the lesson is built this way
The students mark, because the person holding the pen is the person learning. Papers come back unmarked with the official key beside them, section by section, with a stop after each block for the items the ROOM lost — those are the only ones worth reteaching. The listening is replayed with the audioscript in hand, which is the most effective quarter of an hour in any review lesson, because it converts «I didn't hear it» into «I heard it and I did not recognise it». The answer sheets get four minutes of their own, because on this paper they cost real marks. The writing homework is marked against the Cambridge criteria and the published sample answer. And the lesson ends with the EGE map, which the class builds by standing up and putting their own errors on it.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“You get your paper back with nothing written on it and the official Cambridge key beside it. You are the examiner today. By the end you will have three lines — not a score, three lines — and they are the only thing you can actually do something about in August.”

Opening · the papers come back unmarked
door → desks
Why: Predicting before marking makes the marking active, and the distance between the prediction and the result is the fastest route to an honest self-assessment.

WHAT WE DO: the mock returned with nothing written on it and the official key beside it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. At the door: nothing is collected. Tonight's writing sheet stays face down on the desk until minute fifty-six, and its author is the person who marks it. Check as you go past that everybody actually has it; anyone who has not written it does it tonight and hands both drafts in together.
  2. 2 minutes — the papers back, by name, face down, and with nothing written on them. Say that clearly as you hand them out, because they will look for a number and not find one.
  3. 1 minute — Kagan RallyCoach: in pairs, thirty seconds each way, before anything is opened — which part do you THINK you did best on, and which worst? Write both guesses at the top of the paper.
  4. The gap between those two guesses and the reality is often the most useful thing in the lesson, and it gets checked at the end of the marking.
  5. Bridge (30 seconds): No marks. The official key is on the desk. You are the examiner today.
📺 On screen: no technology in the opening — the papers and the printed keys only, and the papers are theirs
👦 Students do: take back an unmarked paper, keep their own writing sheet face down and commit to two public predictions about their own performance

Lesson frame
board
Why: The classification is the whole method of the lesson and it has to exist before the first item is marked, or everything gets called carelessness.

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and put up the classification that runs the lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three headed columns on the board: SLIP (I can fix it in five seconds, unaided) · GAP (I still do not know it) · MISREAD (I knew it and answered a different question).
  2. Add a fourth column and say why it is separate: SHEET — right on the question paper, wrong or missing on the answer sheet. On a Cambridge paper that column is never empty and it has nothing to do with English.
  3. The rule that stops «I was just careless» being a hiding place: if you cannot fix it in five seconds without looking anything up, it is not a slip.
  4. Say the shape in thirty seconds: mark it yourself, classify every loss, listen again with the script in your hand, then three lines you can revise from in August.
  5. The honest framing, because somebody needs to hear it: This is a real examination paper, sat with no support and no practice on the sheet. It is built to find holes. Finding eight holes in May is good news.

👦 Students do: copy the four column headings into the margin of their own paper

Why: Marking · reading Parts 1–3

WHAT WE DO: fifteen items marked against the official key, then the ones the ROOM lost

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Marking rule on the board: tick, or write the correct answer AND one letter in the margin — S, G, M or SH.
  2. Six minutes, individually and silently, reading Parts 1 to 3. The key is on the desk, one per pair, and it does not move.
  3. Spot-check three papers as you walk, cheerfully and at random. You said you would.
  4. Then the only part worth class time: Hands up for anyone who lost question 9. Find the items the ROOM lost rather than the ones individuals lost — usually three or four across the three parts.
  5. Reteach exactly those, and nothing else, for five minutes, using Kagan Numbered Heads Together so the explanation comes from a student who got it right. Part 2 is the usual casualty: all three requirements have to match, not the interesting one.
  6. Two minutes: everybody counts their G items so far and writes the number in a circle at the top of the page.
📺 On screen: the class-wide wrong items listed on the board as they are found — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: mark fifteen of their own items, classify every loss with a letter, then explain a shared error to the room

WHAT WE DO: seventeen more items, and the two parts that decide the score

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Five minutes, individually and silently, Parts 4 to 6, same marking rule.
  2. Then stop on the two that always hurt, three minutes each. Part 4 (five sentences removed, eight offered): the decision is made by reference words — this, they, but, so — and by the sentence AFTER the gap as much as the one before it.
  3. Part 6, the open cloze, and it is the most useful three minutes of the stage: took · between · It / This / That · than · have / had · much. Every one is grammar known since Unit 3. Ask the question that matters — Did you not know it, or did you not go back and read the whole sentence?
  4. Run both with Kagan Showdown: read the gap again, everybody writes the answer, everybody reveals together. Forty seconds each and it tells you whether the reteaching worked.
  5. Two minutes: add the G count for Parts 4 to 6 to the circle at the top. That circled number, not the score, is what the rest of the lesson works on.
📺 On screen: the two parts written up with one corrected example each — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: mark seventeen more items, then reveal a corrected answer simultaneously with the whole room, twice

Move break · say it correctly, standing
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies — and in an examination week that is truer, not less true.

WHAT WE DO: four minutes between two heavy stages

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everybody stands. You read one of the items the class got wrong — the WRONG version.
  2. The whole room says the correct version out loud, together. Then one student says why the wrong one was tempting.
  3. Six items, six choruses, and the open cloze gaps are the best of them because they are single words: took place · between our team and · far more experienced than.
  4. Saying a correct form aloud in unison is not a drill for its own sake; it is the only moment in the lesson that puts the right version in the mouth rather than on the page.
  5. Freeze and sit: Now the listening — and this time you have the script.

The listening, with the script in your hand
individual → pairs → class
Why: The listening, with the script in your hand

WHAT WE DO: the most effective quarter of an hour in any review lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand out the audioscript, one per pair, face down. Mark the listening first: five minutes, individually, against the official key, same four letters in the margin.
  2. Ninety seconds on Part 1 first, because it is the cheapest fix in the lesson and you saw it happen on Monday: items 2 and 3 are both decided by the LAST line. Three sports are named but only one was tried today; the bike is broken and then Dad says he fixed it at the weekend. One rule on the board: Do not choose while the conversation is still running.
  3. Play Part 2 again, whole, papers open, script still face down. Just listen — no writing.
  4. NOW turn the script over. Four minutes, in pairs: find the exact line that decides questions 8 and 9 and underline it.
  5. Those two are worth the whole stage. Question 8: the answer is that the STAFF know a lot — «they've chosen the right people to work there», and the word «staff» is never said. Question 9: the stem says they AGREE, so one voice is not enough — she has learnt from the diagrams and he says they have detail and labels.
  6. Write the transferable rule on the board and leave it there for the last two lessons: The stem is a PARAPHRASE. If the words match what you heard, suspect it.
  7. Then two minutes on Part 3, which loses marks for reasons that are not listening at all: Tuesday needs a capital letter, 17th October is accepted in four written forms, and A-L-T-I-D-R-E-W has to be copied letter by letter as it is spelled.
📺 On screen: PREPARE_Listening_Part 02.mp3 replayed, with the printed audioscript in their hands. Backup: if the audio will not play, the script alone does most of the work — read the two decisive stretches aloud yourself and run the underlining task unchanged
👦 Students do: mark twenty-five items, listen once more without writing, then find and underline the lines that decide the two hardest questions
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE tasks 2 and 3–9 (the listening section). Every stem on this paper is a paraphrase and the option that repeats the spoken words is the trap. Hearing WHY a distractor worked, with the script in hand, transfers to the Russian paper completely — and questions 8 and 9 are the clearest examples the year has produced.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: after the underlining task, give one three-item Russian-format extract for five minutes. Take the time from the writing stage, which survives on six minutes because the sample answer goes home with them.

WHAT WE DO: the marks that were earned on the paper and lost on the sheet

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Both answer sheets side by side with both question papers. Four minutes, in pairs, one question each: Find every place where the paper is right and the sheet is wrong.
  2. The four things that turn up every time, and they should be counted out loud: an empty box · two boxes shaded for one question · a word written in the wrong gap in Part 3 or Part 6 · a lower-case letter where a capital was needed.
  3. Everybody writes the number of SHEET losses at the top of the page, next to the circle. In a normal group it is between one and four marks per student.
  4. Say the sentence that makes it stick, and say it once: Those marks were yours. You knew the English. You lost them to a box.
  5. One rule for the future, written on the board: In the reading, the answer goes on the sheet the moment you decide it. In the listening, on the paper first — the recording gives you six minutes to copy.

Why: The writing, against the Cambridge criteria

WHAT WE DO: the paper that cannot be marked with a key

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Writing sheets face up at last. The four Cambridge criteria go on the board with one question each: C content (are all four of Jessie's notes answered?) · CA communicative achievement (does it sound like an email to a friend?) · O organisation (paragraphs, and do they join up?) · L language (range, and accuracy).
  2. Two minutes, silent and alone: write the four letters in your own margin and put a tick or a question mark against each. Nobody has marked this before you, and that is deliberate — the person who diagnoses the text is the person who improves it.
  3. Then the Cambridge sample answer for question 1, one per pair. Three minutes: find the four notes from Jessie's email in it and underline where each is answered. Every one is there, in order, in about a hundred words — that is what «uses all the notes» looks like.
  4. Three minutes on their own text with the same pen: mark where each of the four notes is answered. Anyone who cannot find one has just diagnosed their own C.
  5. Everybody stands for the next three minutes — it is the twenty-fourth minute of sitting and the last stages are the demanding ones. Kagan Timed Pair Share on your feet, ninety seconds each way: read your partner's email and say ONE sentence of praise tied to a letter and ONE question tied to a letter.
  6. Say what happens next, and it answers the obvious question: I have not marked these and I am not going to. What I mark is the second draft. That is tonight's homework and it is the only piece of the week that gets a comment from me.
📺 On screen: the four Cambridge criteria on the board with one sentence each — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: grade their own email against the four criteria, check it against the published sample answer, then stand and hear a partner read it

🎯 The error log · three lines
individual → pairs → teacher
Why: 🎯 The error log · three lines

WHAT WE DO: the stage the whole lesson exists for

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The format goes on the board and there is no negotiation about it: TOPIC + WHAT I DO WRONG + ONE EXAMPLE FROM MY OWN PAPER.
  2. Do one on the board yourself, from a real anonymous paper: Open cloze: I write two words where the paper asks for one — I wrote «a lot of» for gap 32 instead of «much».
  3. 🎯 Six minutes, silent, individually: three lines, on a card, with their name on it. Three — not five and not one. Walk the room and reject anything that says «grammar» or «listening».
  4. Kagan RallyCoach, three minutes: read your three to a partner, whose only job is one question — Could you revise that from what you have written? If the answer is no, it gets rewritten.
  5. Collect the cards, photograph them and give them straight back. Say what they are for, twice: This goes inside the cover of your notebook. In August you revise from these three lines and nothing else. They are also what next lesson is built from.
  6. Thirty seconds on the two guesses at the top of each paper: hands up, whose best-part guess was right? Whose worst? A good-humoured ending to a demanding stage.

👦 Students do: write three specific, revisable lines about their own work and defend each one to a partner

The EGE map · built standing, out of their own errors
board → standing
Why: The exam has been a footnote all year, on purpose. It is made explicit once, at the point where there is real evidence to attach it to, and the class puts the evidence there itself rather than copying a list.

WHAT WE DO: eight minutes of the year's exam work, finally made explicit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. This is the one moment in the year when the Russian exam is named to the whole class, and it is named because there is now evidence on the desk to attach it to.
  2. Put the twelve rows on the board in advance, one line each: reading Parts 1–6, listening Parts 1–4, writing Parts 1–2, speaking. Beside each, its Russian address. Read them out once, fast, with no lecture.
  3. Then everybody stands with their error card: put one sticky note (or a chalk mark) against the row where YOUR first log line belongs. Ninety seconds and the whole class is on its feet.
  4. Look at the board together for one minute. The rows with five marks against them are the group's summer syllabus, and everyone can see it without a single name being said.
  5. Two facts worth naming while they are standing there: reading Part 4 is almost exactly EGE task 11, and listening Part 3 has no Russian equivalent at all — the Russian paper never makes you write what you hear, which is why spelling gets punished later instead.
  6. Say the honest sentence, exactly as it has been said all year: Not everybody in this room will sit that exam. If you will, this board tells you which parts of today matter most. If you will not, nothing changes — the English is the same. Then stop.
📺 On screen: the twelve-row map on the board with the class's own sticky notes on it — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: stand up and place their own first error line on the map, then read the class's summer syllabus off the board

Wrap-up + homework
desks → door
Why: Self-assessment against the aim they heard at the start, so the lesson closes the loop it opened — and in these four lessons the loop is the year, not the hour.

WHAT WE DO: self-check against today's aim, then the homework said once and written on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Back to the aim: Thumbs up if there are three lines on your card and you could revise from all three of them.
  2. Exit at the door: each student reads me line one of their log. Out loud, once. That is the whole exit ticket.
  3. Homework said once and written up: the email rewritten, about 100 words, with the first version stapled behind it. Twenty minutes, and it is the only marked homework of the week. Plus five minutes copying the three lines onto your notebook cover.
  4. Say what is next: Next lesson is built out of these cards — and then we sit the speaking test, the whole thing, with you in the examiner's chair.

Writing Part 1, second draft
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can mark their own work against an official key, classify each loss as a slip, a gap or a misreading, and write a three-line error log specific enough to revise from.
  • Subsidiary. Hearing a distractor with the script in hand; explaining why a rejected option was attractive; checking a written text against the four Cambridge criteria; using an answer sheet without losing marks on it.
  • Personal. You have the papers and your own notes from watching them work. Use the notes, not the totals — the student who scored well but never checked anything needs a different sentence from the one who ran out of time in Part 2.
By the end, students can…
  • mark a paper honestly against an official key
  • say the difference between a slip and a gap in their own work
  • explain why a listening distractor was attractive
  • name three specific things to revise, not «grammar»

In question 3 the lift is out (Mum has the car), the bus is out (one an hour), the bike is broken — and then Dad says he fixed it at the weekend. The map · paper section → EGE taskReading Part 1 · five short texts EGE task 10. Not the same wrapper, but the same question: why does this text exist and what does the writer want? A candidate who reads for facts instead of purpose loses both. · Reading Part 2 · matching people to texts EGE task 10. Almost the same machine — several short descriptions, more options than answers, extra options printed specifically to be rejected. All the requirements have to match, and that rule is identical in both papers. · Reading Part 3 · long text, five questions EGE tasks 12–18. Four options, one gap, all four words in the same field. Collocation decides it, not meaning. · Reading Part 6 · open cloze, one word EGE tasks 19–25. The Russian paper gives you the word to change and Cambridge gives you nothing at all, which makes this the harder version of the same knowledge: articles, prepositions, auxiliaries, comparatives, pronouns. · Listening Parts 1 and 4 · multiple choice EGE tasks 3–9. Three options instead of three, an interview in Part 4 exactly as in the Russian paper. The option that repeats the words you heard is the one to suspect. · Listening Part 2 · opinion and agreement EGE task 2 and tasks 3–9. Every stem is a paraphrase and two of the six ask what the speakers AGREE about, which is the same discipline the Russian true-false-not-stated task demands: one voice is not enough, and «not stated» is a real answer. · Listening Part 3 · write the answer in the gap No EGE equivalent — and that is why it is useful. The Russian listening paper never asks a candidate to write, so nothing else in the year measures whether they can spell what they have understood. Those marks come back in tasks 26–31 (word formation) and in the writing, where a misspelled known word is a lost mark in both exams. · Writing Part 1 · the email with four notes EGE task 37. Nearly the same task: an email from a friend, points that must all be answered, about a hundred words. Two differences worth naming — the Russian task wants 100 to 140 words and it wants three questions asked back. · Writing Part 2 · article or story EGE task 38. Different genre, same marking logic: does it answer the task, is it organised, is the language varied and accurate. The Russian task adds an opinion, an objection and a table of figures; the story has no Russian equivalent at all and is written for pleasure. · Speaking Parts 1–4 The EGE oral section. The Russian oral has no paired discussion, so the transfer is in the habits, not the format: answer in two parts (answer, then reason or example), keep going when the first idea runs out, and ask for a repeat instead of answering the wrong question.
Marks and what to reportReading 32 marks in 45 minutes · Listening 25 marks in 40 minutes of audio · Writing two pieces of about 100 words in 45 minutes · Speaking 12–17 minutes for a pair. Cambridge does not publish a raw-score conversion for a sample test, so do not invent a Cambridge grade from it: report percentages per paper and per part, which is what the review lesson needs anyway.
Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Somebody marks themselves generouslySpot-check three papers during the stage, at random and cheerfully, and say in advance that you will. The point is not policing: a generous mark is a wasted lesson and they should be told so in those words.
«I got a bad score, so I am bad at English»Head it off in the frame: this is a real B1 examination paper sat with no support and no practice on the sheet, and it is built to find holes. Finding eight holes in May is good news; finding them in a real centre in June is not. Report percentages per part, never a Cambridge grade — a sample test has no published conversion.
The error log says «vocabulary»Reject it and give the format: TOPIC + WHAT I DO WRONG + ONE EXAMPLE FROM MY OWN PAPER. «Listening Part 3: I write what I think I heard instead of what was spelled — I wrote ALTIDREW as ALTIDRU.» Three of those and August has a shape.
Time runs shortCut the writing stage to six minutes (they have the sample answer to work from at home) and protect the error log and the EGE map. The log is the only thing that leaves the room with them; the map is said once a year.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Everything. Each part of the Cambridge paper gets its Russian address on the board today: reading Part 4 next to task 11, reading Part 6 next to tasks 19–25, listening Part 2 next to tasks 2 and 3–9, the email next to task 37.

The review lesson is where the year's exam work is finally made explicit, because now there is evidence on the desk to attach it to. Students who are not sitting the Russian exam lose nothing from hearing the map; students who are get something they can revise from over the summer, built out of their own marked paper rather than out of a list.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
