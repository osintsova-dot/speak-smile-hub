🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Sit the Reading and Use of English paper of a genuine B2 First for Schools practice exam — seven parts, 52 questions, one hour and fifteen minutes — to the official clock, with the official rubrics, and with no help of any kind.
What is genuinely hard
The English on this paper is not new; three other things are. The length: seventy-five unbroken minutes is longer than anything this class has sat, and the last twenty are where the marks are lost. The arithmetic: 52 marks in 75 minutes is eighty-five seconds a mark, and the paper does not care which part was interesting. The procedure: answers go on a separate sheet, in capitals for Parts 2, 3 and 4, and this paper gives no transfer time at the end at all.
Typical error to expect
Spending fifteen minutes on Part 5. The novel extract feels solvable so candidates stay in it, and then Part 7 — ten matching questions, the fastest marks on the paper — gets six minutes at the end. The paper is 52 marks in 75 minutes: that is 85 seconds a mark, and it does not care which part you enjoyed.
Answers written in lower case, or written on the question paper and never transferred. The Cambridge rubric says IN CAPITAL LETTERS for Parts 2, 3 and 4 and there is no transfer time in this paper at all. A perfect answer in the wrong place scores zero, and this is the cheapest mark in the exam to protect.
Why the lesson is built this way
Almost the whole lesson is the paper, and that is correct: the point of a mock is the seventy-five minutes, not the teaching around it. Everything the class needs is said in the seven minutes before the start — the shape of the paper, the clock, the two procedural rules — and nothing at all is said during it. The Writing half of the same exam cannot fit into any ninety-minute lesson, so it goes home tonight as the Workbook's own timed essay and comes back marked against the Cambridge criteria next lesson. Nothing is shortened and nothing is invented.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Today you sit a real paper. Not a version of one, not one I wrote — the real thing, with the real clock. Seven parts, fifty-two questions, seventy-five minutes. That is eighty-five seconds a question, so the only way to lose badly is to fall in love with Part 5. Answers go on the sheet as you decide them, in capitals where it says capitals, because there is no time at the end to copy anything. Nothing about today goes in a report. It exists so that you and I know what to do with the last three lessons.”

Why: Two minutes of ritual turn a classroom into an exam room, and the thirty-second plan question makes every student enter the paper with a strategy rather than with page one.

WHAT WE DO: phones in the box, desks separated, papers face down, one sentence of plan from each student

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Meet them at the door with the box: phones in, and the box stays on your desk until the papers are collected. No exceptions and no discussion — it takes ten seconds if you never negotiate.
  2. Desks apart, bags on the floor at the front, two pens and a pencil on the desk and nothing else. Books stay in the bags today.
  3. Kagan Timed Pair Share, thirty seconds each way, and it is the last talking of the hour: What is your plan for the seventy-five minutes — which part will you do first, and why?
  4. Two answers to the floor, no comment from you. Then papers face down on every desk.
📺 On screen: nothing on display — the room is deliberately bare, and that is part of what is being rehearsed
👦 Students do: hand in phones, set their desks, and say one sentence about their own plan for the paper

Briefing · seven parts, one clock, two rules
whole class, papers still face down
Why: Everything a candidate is allowed to know must be said before the clock starts, because afterwards the teacher goes silent. Seven minutes here buys seventy-five minutes of independent work.

WHAT WE DO: the shape of the paper, the arithmetic of the time, and the two procedural rules — said once, then never again

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the seven parts on the board in one column with their question numbers beside them: 1 multiple choice 1–8 · 2 open cloze 9–16 · 3 word formation 17–24 · 4 transformations 25–30 · 5 multiple choice reading 31–36 · 6 gapped text 37–42 · 7 multiple matching 43–52.
  2. Kagan RallyRobin, ninety seconds in pairs: taking turns, name what each part actually asks you to do. They know all seven from the Workbook — this is retrieval, not information.
  3. The arithmetic, written under the list and left there: 52 marks, 75 minutes, 85 seconds a mark. Parts 1 to 4 in about 30 minutes, Parts 5 to 7 in about 40, five minutes to check.
  4. The two rules, said once and plainly: Answers go on the sheet as you decide them — there is no time at the end to copy. Parts 2, 3 and 4 are written in CAPITAL LETTERS.
  5. Answer sheets out: they number them 1 to 52 themselves, in four columns, while you count to sixty. It takes one minute and it removes every question about where to write.
  6. Last sentence before the clock starts: I will say two things in the next seventy-five minutes: halfway, and ten minutes left. Everything else is yours.
📺 On screen: the seven parts and the time arithmetic written on the board — they stay up during the paper, because timing information is not cheating
👦 Students do: recall what each of the seven parts demands, then rule and number their own answer sheet

Why: 🎯 Reading and Use of English · the paper, 75 minutes

WHAT WE DO: the official paper, sat whole, in silence, with two time calls and nothing else

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Papers turned over together. Names on. Start.
  2. Seventy-five minutes, silent, individual. You circulate every five minutes with your hands behind your back and you say nothing at all.
  3. Two announcements only, and no others: Halfway — you should be starting Part 5 at minute 37, and Ten minutes left at minute 65.
  4. At minute fifteen, glance at three answer sheets as you pass to check that answers are actually being written there. Say nothing; if a sheet is empty, tap it once with one finger.
  5. For the student who is genuinely stuck, one sentence and then walk away: Leave it, go on, come back. Never explain an item, never confirm an answer, never say «nearly».
  6. Pens down at seventy-five minutes. Answer sheets on top of question papers, both face down.
📺 On screen: nothing is displayed during the paper except the seven parts and the timing arithmetic from the briefing. Backup: if the timer app or the wall clock fails, write the finishing time on the board in large figures before the start and call the two announcements from your own watch
👦 Students do: work independently through 52 questions in 75 minutes, choosing their own order and transferring every answer to the sheet

Why: Papers in, and the one thing said afterwards

WHAT WE DO: scripts collected, the homework said once, and no post-mortem at all

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Collect the answer sheets first, then the question papers, in that order. Count them. Nobody takes a paper home.
  2. Do NOT run a discussion of the questions now — it is the strongest instinct in the room and the least useful thing you could do. Say so: No answers today. Next lesson you mark it yourselves, with the official key, and that is worth ten times more.
  3. One question, answered by a show of hands, ten seconds: Hands up if you ran out of time. Note the number; it is the first line of next lesson.
  4. Homework said once and written up: Workbook p.99, the Writing exam summary and the Part 1 essay, timed at forty minutes. Say why: That essay is the half of this exam we could not sit today. It is the same paper, written at your kitchen table.
  5. Phones back at the door, one at a time, as they leave.
📺 On screen: the homework written on the board while they pack — nothing else, and no answers on any surface
👦 Students do: hand in both papers, answer one show-of-hands question, and copy the timed writing homework

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
In Part 2 you write ONE word in each gap. Can it be two? → no, and «don't» counts as two — that is why the answer is «not».
Part 4 says between two and five words including the word given. If your answer is six words, how many marks? → zero, however good the English is.
You cannot do question 19. What do you do? → leave it, go on, come back at the end. What you never do is stop.

Reading and Use of English is 75 minutes, the four listening tracks run about 40 with the transfer time, the Writing paper is another 80, and the Speaking test takes 14 to 20 minutes per pair. This block splits it without cutting anything: Reading and Use of English in lesson 69 at full official timing, the listening and the speaking test in lesson 71, and the Writing paper as the Workbook's own timed homework (WB p.99, the essay) after lesson 69. Reading and Use of English · Part 4key-word transformations, questions 25–30 — the second sentence is completed with between two and five words including the word given, which may not be changed
Reading and Use of English · Part 5multiple choice, questions 31–36 — an extract from a novel about a teenager called Sam who has done badly in his exams; four options per question, and two of them ask what the writer is doing rather than what he says
Reading and Use of English · Part 6gapped text, questions 37–42, «Online fun sports» — six sentences put back into the article from a list of seven; the extra one is there on purpose
Reading and Use of English · Part 7multiple matching, questions 43–52, «Young collectors» — ten questions matched to four teenagers, who may be chosen more than once

Reading and Use of English: seven parts, 52 questions, 1 hour 15 minutes. Parts 1–4 are Use of English (30 questions), Parts 5–7 are Reading (22). Answers go on a separate sheet in capital letters and there is no extra transfer time — this is the single procedural fact that costs marks.

Listening: four parts, 30 questions, approximately 40 minutes including 5 minutes' transfer time. Every recording is played twice. Four separate audio files, one per part.

Writing: two questions, 140–190 words each, 1 hour 20 minutes — Part 1 is compulsory and Part 2 is chosen from three. Model answers for all four tasks are printed in the answer key, which makes them usable as classroom models.

Where the block sits each part: Reading and Use of English is sat whole in lesson 69 (75 minutes). The Listening is sat whole in lesson 71 (40 minutes) and the Speaking test runs in the same lesson, in pairs, with a third student as the examiner. The Writing paper is set as the Workbook's own timed homework after lesson 69 and marked with the Cambridge criteria in lesson 70.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Somebody finishes in fifty minutes and sits backSay it in the briefing and hold to it: Nobody leaves the paper early. If you have finished, you go back to Part 2 and to any transformation where you wrote fewer than three words — those are the two places marks hide.
Answers written on the question paper and never transferredThis paper has no transfer time. Show them the two-column answer sheet in the briefing, make them number it themselves, and say the rule once: An answer that is not on the sheet does not exist. Then check the first two rows of three students at minute fifteen without saying anything.
A student panics in Part 5 and stops workingKneel down, point at the clock, and say the only sentence you are allowed: Leave it, go to Part 6, come back. Do not explain the item and do not comfort at length — thirty seconds and move away, or the whole room turns round.
The lesson is cut short by a fire drill or a lost twenty minutesThen stop the paper cleanly at the point you reach, write the time on every script, and finish it at the start of lesson 70 before the marking. Never let students take an unfinished paper home — the review lesson depends on the scripts being honest.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: The whole written paper. Part 1 of this paper sits on tasks 32–38 (lexical choice), Part 2 on tasks 19–25, Part 3 on tasks 26–31 (word formation), Parts 5, 6 and 7 on tasks 10–18.

This is the first time in the year that the class sits a complete examination paper to the real clock, and that is the point of it — not the score. Two things are measured that nothing else in the course measures: stamina across seventy-five unbroken minutes, and the discipline of writing answers on a separate sheet in capitals. Neither has anything to do with English and both are worth marks in every exam these students will ever sit. The full Russian mapping is given to the class in lesson 70, not today; today they simply sit the paper.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
