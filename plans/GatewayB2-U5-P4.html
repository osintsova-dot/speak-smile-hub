🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Compare two photographs for a full minute — position, similarities, differences, speculation, fillers, opinion — and answer the examiner's question; then write an informal email (or a story) that covers every point the task sets, with the range of vocabulary and grammar a B2 mark needs.
What is genuinely hard
In speaking: students describe both photos separately and never compare, then run out of language at forty seconds and stop. The cure is a set of frames plus fillers, drilled before the clock starts. In writing: the commonest lost marks are not grammatical at all — a question in the task is not answered, or the same adjective («good») is used five times.
Typical error to expect
Describing instead of comparing: In the first photo there is a woman. She is wearing a coat. In the second photo there is a man … — fluent, accurate, and it scores badly, because the task said compare.
Silence at forty seconds — a filler is not a weakness: Well … let me think … OK, so … buys the three seconds that keep the minute alive.
Why the lesson is built this way
The model comes first and is analysed for LANGUAGE CATEGORIES rather than for content — six categories, six colours in the notebook — because a category is retrievable under pressure and a phrase list is not. Then everyone speaks for a timed minute, twice, in pairs, so the whole class gets two minutes of exam speaking instead of one student getting all of it. The writing half is short and surgical: the task is analysed, the two models are judged against criteria, and the piece itself is planned in class and finished at home.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
One minute is longer than you think. Today you get the six things you need — where things are, what is the same, what is different, what you can guess, what you think, and how to buy yourself three seconds when your mind goes blank. Then everybody speaks for a full minute, twice. After the break, the same skill in writing.

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting at the door, then a fast speculation round on last lesson's grammar

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what was the last thing you bought? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — rewind, speculation. Hold up an object from a student's desk. Three guesses, one each: it must … it might … it can't … Round the room, fast.
  3. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): You've been guessing about objects. Today you guess about photographs — out loud, for a full minute, which is exactly what an examiner asks for. Page 66.
📺 On screen: the three frames on the board. Backup: the object and your voice — nothing else needed.
👦 Students do: one speculation each, standing where they are.

5–12′
Lead-in · the thirty-second memory test
desks → teams
Why: A memory game shows the class what they systematically fail to notice, which is exactly what makes their photo descriptions thin.

WHAT WE DO: the Teacher's Book warmer — students look at one photo for thirty seconds, then defend what they remember

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Look at the photo on the left of page 66 for thirty seconds. Remember everything. Time it. Then books closed.
  2. Two teams. You read eight statements about the photo, some true, some false; teams confer for five seconds and call. (There are men and women in the picture. — True. It's a very hot day. — False.)
  3. Books open, check, and ask the question that sets up the lesson: which details did you all miss? Why? (Everybody looks at people and nobody looks at background, weather, position.)
  4. Board the four things nobody notices: foreground · background · position · weather.
📺 On screen: the four «invisible» categories on the board. Backup: the photo is in the book; no screen involved.
👦 Students do: look, remember, then argue in teams over eight true/false claims.

12–30′
The model · six categories, not a phrase list
desks → pairs
Why: Six retrievable categories beat thirty phrases. The notebook page made here is the page they revise from before every speaking exam this year.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.66 exercises 1, 3 and 5 (track 26) — the model analysed by language category

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 1: two example expressions — which describes a similarity, which a difference? Then pairs write three more of each, from memory of Unit 3. Three minutes. Take the best six for the board.
  2. Exercise 3 — play track 26. Two questions: is he comparing or describing? What exactly did the examiner ask? Answers: track 26: the student is comparing the two photos rather than describing each one in detail, and the examiner's questions are «Why have the people chosen to go shopping in these places?» and «Which of the two places do you prefer to shop in?». The photos are Camden Market in London and the Bullring in Birmingham.
  3. Exercise 5 — the six categories. This is the core of the lesson. Pairs sort the Speaking bank language into: position · similarities · differences · speculating · fillers · opinions. Six minutes. Answers: the six language categories: 1 position (In the foreground; on the right/left) · 2 similarities (Another similarity is …; In both photos we can see …) · 3 differences (This photo is … not as … as the other) · 4 speculating (Perhaps/Maybe …) · 5 fillers (OK, so …) · 6 opinions (In my view …; It seems to me that …).
  4. Make them copy the six categories into the notebook as six boxes with two phrases in each. Six boxes, not thirty phrases. Under pressure you remember a box.
  5. Drill the fillers properly, because students refuse to use them until they hear a teacher do it: Well … · Let me think … · OK, so … Say them with a calm falling tone, then the class repeats.
  6. Two culture notes in ten seconds: the photos are Camden Market in London and the Bullring in Birmingham.
📺 On screen: recording — track 26 in the board app; the six category names written on the board as headings. Backup: the audio script is in the Teacher's Book (TB p.123) — read the model aloud yourself, which also lets you exaggerate the fillers.
👦 Students do: listen, sort the language into six categories, copy six boxes, then drill fillers chorally.

30–36′
Stirrer · thirty-second bursts, standing
open floor
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies. It also removes the content excuse: comparing two pens leaves nothing to hide behind except language.

WHAT WE DO: a fast standing drill of the comparison frames, with no content pressure

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. Hold up two objects (two pens, two phones, two shoes — anything).
  2. Mix-Pair-Share: thirty seconds each, compare the two objects using at least one frame from each of three categories — position, difference, speculation. Then swap partners. Three rounds.
  3. The rule that makes it work: you may not stop before the thirty seconds are up. If you dry, use a filler and keep going.
  4. Finish standing and go straight to the timed exam task while the frames are hot.
📺 On screen: the six category names still on the board. Backup: no equipment — two everyday objects are the whole material list.
👦 Students do: speak for three timed thirty-second bursts with three different partners, using a filler rather than stopping.

36–56′
🎯 The exam task · one timed minute each, twice
pairs
Why: Two timed minutes per student is the point of the lesson. Peer feedback on what was NOT compared is the most transferable correction available in a speaking task.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.66 exercises 6 and 7a–b — the full task with the partner's comment, then swapped

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the task exactly as an examiner would: Student A, look at photos A and B. They show people asking for money in the street. Compare them and say how you think the people are feeling. You have one minute.
  2. Fifteen seconds of silent thinking, then one timed minute. Partner B listens, then answers the follow-up question in twenty seconds.
  3. Swap roles and run it again with the second pair of photos.
  4. Then the most useful three minutes of the lesson: tell your partner one thing they could have compared but didn't. That is exercise 7b, and it is where the improvement happens.
  5. Second round with new partners if the timing allows — a second attempt after feedback is worth more than a first attempt with someone new.
  6. You circulate with the six categories in your head and tick which ones you hear. Feed that back as a class, not individually: I heard a lot of difference language and almost no speculation.
📺 On screen: a visible timer for the whole room, and the six categories on the board where every pair can see them. Backup: you call «start» and «stop» aloud; the photos are in the book.
👦 Students do: speak for a full timed minute twice, comment on a partner's photos, and give one specific improvement.

Developing writing · read the task before you like it
desks → pairs
Why: Students improve writing fastest by judging someone else's text against explicit criteria — the criteria then travel into their own drafting.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.67 exercises 1, 2a and 3–4 — the two options analysed, then the models judged against criteria

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Warmer, thirty seconds, books closed. Say the phrases; the class says email or story: It was great to hear from you · In the end · How are things? · Use a variety of past tenses · At first · Use contractions.
  2. Exercise 1: read both tasks and choose. Show of hands for each, then ask each half why. Two minutes.
  3. Exercise 2a in pairs: brainstorm the language each genre needs. Half the class does the email, half does the story, then one pair from each side pairs up and shares.
  4. Exercises 3 and 4: judge the two model texts against the criteria in the book — range of vocabulary, adjectives and adverbs, linkers, time expressions, comment adverbs, varied grammar. Findings: the two model texts checked against the criteria: the email repeats «good» five times (weak range) but has varied grammar («If I were you, I would …», «Not only will you …»); the story repeats «morning», «look out» and «sea» but uses strong description (sparkling sea, full of energy) and clear time sequence (just at that moment, at first, then).
  5. Land the point that costs the most marks: the email uses «good» five times. It is accurate and it is dull, and dull loses a band. What could replace each one? Take four suggestions and write them up.
📺 On screen: the six criteria written as a checklist on the board. Backup: the same criteria are printed in the Writing bank on SB p.67.
👦 Students do: sort genre language in pairs, then judge two model texts against six criteria and suggest replacements for a repeated word.

70–82′
Writing · plan and open, in class
desks
Why: The commonest B2 writing failure is a missing content point, and it is caught at the plan stage in ninety seconds — never at the marking stage.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.67 exercise 5a — the four-step plan and the first paragraph, written where help is standing

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board the four steps from the Teacher's Book and make them copy: 1 brainstorm ideas · 2 write a paragraph plan · 3 check the plan answers everything the task asks · 4 write a draft, then add at least three things from the Writing bank.
  2. Four minutes: brainstorm and plan only. Circulate and check step 3 specifically — does your plan answer every question in the task? That is the mark most often lost.
  3. Seven minutes of silent writing: the greeting and the first paragraph only.
  4. Rally Coach for two minutes: partner reads the plan (not the text) and says which task point is missing.
  5. The rest is homework — but the hard part is done, and I have seen your plan.
📺 On screen: the four planning steps on the board. Backup: the same plan is printed in the Workbook on p.47, which they take home.
👦 Students do: brainstorm, plan, write an opening, then check a partner's plan against the task.

82–86′
Micro-feedback · one upgrade each
pairs
Why: A four-minute editing habit at the end of every writing stage is worth more than a comment written on a page a week later.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute editing burst on the repeated-adjective problem the models exposed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Find one adjective in your own paragraph that you could upgrade. Cross it out. Write a better one. Two minutes.
  2. RallyRobin: read your upgrade to a partner; the partner says stronger or not really.
  3. Take five upgrades for the board and let the class vote on the best one. Twenty seconds.
  4. One caution: range means variety, not the longest word you can find. «Good» five times is worse than «good» once and four other words.
📺 On screen: the five class upgrades on the board. Backup: no equipment needed.
👦 Students do: edit their own text, then check a partner's upgrade.

Structure
Both photos show … · Another similarity is that … · This photo isn't as … as the other one. · Perhaps they're … · Well … let me think … OK, so … · It seems to me that …
Form
comparison structures from Unit 4 reused in speech · speculation with may / might / could + be + -ing · fillers as fixed chunks · informal email conventions: greeting, contracted forms, direct questions, closing formula.
Pron. watch
in a photo comparison the contrast is carried by stress: in the FIRST photo … but in the SECOND …, BOTH photos show …. Fillers need to sound natural rather than panicked: well … let me think … OK, so … with a falling, unhurried tone
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
compare vs describe: If I say «in photo A there is a man, in photo B there is a woman» — am I comparing? — No. What word makes it a comparison? — whereas, both, unlike.
fillers: Is «let me think» a mistake? — No. What does it buy you? — Three seconds.
the task question: How many things must you do? — Two: compare, and answer the question.
ICQ before the timed minute: How long do you speak? What happens if you stop early?

Assumptions: Students compared photographs in Unit 3 and have the comparison grammar from Unit 4. Fillers have never been taught explicitly and are usually regarded as errors.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students describe each photo in turn and never compare.The six categories on the board and the peer-feedback question «what could you have compared but didn't?» do the work. If it persists, ban the phrase «in the first photo there is».
Silence at forty seconds.Fillers are taught as language, drilled chorally, and the rule «you may not stop early» is enforced in the stirrer where there is no content pressure.
Only the confident half speaks in the exam-task stage.It is timed and in pairs, so all speak simultaneously — the quiet half cannot be talked over. Circulate to listen, not to correct.
The email misses one of the task's questions.Step 3 of the planning frame exists for exactly this, and the Rally Coach check reads the plan rather than the prose.
«Good» five times in the writing.The model analysis exposes it in someone else's text first, which is much easier to accept, and the four-minute editing burst forces one upgrade per student.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 37 (the personal email) and tasks 39–40 (the oral: describing and comparing pictures).

The two halves of this lesson are the two halves of the Russian written and oral papers. Comparing two photographs with speculation is task 40; an informal email with three questions answered and three asked is task 37, and the marks there are lost on structure, not on language — no greeting, no closing, questions missing.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
