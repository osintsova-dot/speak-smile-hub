🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Choose the word that fits a gap in a connected text by deciding what kind of word it must be first — and write about 60 words of predictions joined with also, too and as well.
What is genuinely hard
In a gapped text all three options usually make sense on their own. Only one fits the grammar AND the meaning of the whole sentence. Students read the options first and choose the word they know.
Typical error to expect
They pick the option that repeats a word from the text. The correct option is almost always paraphrased; the wrong ones copy the wording [exam report]
Plus the linking-word error this lesson exists to kill: Also, I think … written with no comma, and too parked in the middle of the sentence.
Why the lesson is built this way
The two capsule texts do the work twice: first as a gist read (one title each, ninety seconds), then as the raw material for the gapped task and the comparison questions. The gapped stage sits in the middle of the lesson, not at the end, because it is the hardest thinking of the day and it needs fresh heads. «Words with two meanings» is placed straight after it on purpose: it is the same idea one size down — the same letters can be a different word, and only the sentence decides. The writing is started in class and finished at home, with the IMPROVE stage done here, in pairs, where it can still change something.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“There is a bag on the moon. Inside it is a message the size of a coin, left there in 1969, and nobody knows who will read it. Today you decide what goes in ours — and you write a letter to whoever opens it.”

door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, six predictions with a chosen modal, and the bridge into the time capsules

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. Six prompts, one sentence each, and the modal must match how sure they are: cars without drivers · school on Saturday · living on Mars · robots in the kitchen · exams on a computer · snow in Norilsk in July.
  3. The challenge rule stays: any will can be met with Are you sure?
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the second spread: Books open, page forty-eight. Somebody left a message on the moon. Nobody knows who will read it.
📺 On screen: the six prompts in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: produce one prediction each with a chosen modal. Expect might to be much more frequent than last lesson. Typical problem — It might to snow: put the crossed-out to on the board and move on

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write one gapped sentence on the board: The capsule is about the size of a small ______ . and three options: money · coin · pence.
  2. Do not choose yet. Tell me what KIND of word the gap needs. Push until somebody says «a noun» and then «one thing, because of 'a'».
  3. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: in a gapped text all three words usually make sense. Only one fits the grammar. Decide what kind of word it is BEFORE you read the options, and two of the three disappear on their own.
  4. Set the bar: Word class first. Meaning second. Never the other way round.
📺 On screen: the gapped sentence and the three options in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: name the word class before choosing. Expect the class to jump straight to «money» — which is the whole demonstration. Typical problem: they cannot name word classes in English; write noun / verb / adjective up and leave them there

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.48: the three photos, and what a time capsule is

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Warmer from TB p.93, 90 seconds: I am from the year 2099. Ask me questions about your world. Do people still watch TV? Give them the categories — houses, food, school, work, towns.
  2. ex.1 in pairs: What do you think a time capsule is? Are they a good idea? Why?
  3. Pre-teach the two words that block the texts (TB p.93): solar system — the sun and everything going round it; bury — to put something in a hole in the ground and cover it.
  4. Background, 20 seconds: TB p.93: one of the earliest time capsules was the Detroit Century Box, sealed on 31 December 1900 and opened on 31 December 2000. There are capsules in space too: Voyager 1 and 2 each carry a record with sounds, images and music from Earth.
📺 On screen: the three photos on the screen so the whole class looks at the same one (Backup: the photos are printed on SB p.48 — work from the paper book and write the two pre-taught words on the board)
👦 Students do: answer the question in pairs and interview you as a person from 2099. Expect strong opinions about whether capsules are worth it. Typical problem: they mix up bury and berry — say both, let them hear it

14–24′
Reading · two capsules
solo → pairs
Why: Gist reading against the clock: one title each, nothing else. It stops the natural instinct to translate the text word by word.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.48 (track 61): read both texts fast and give each one its title

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the gist task BEFORE they read: Two texts, two titles. Two minutes. Nothing else. The titles are «Two are better than one» and «Out of this world».
  2. Key (TB p.93): text A — «Out of this world» (2) · text B — «Two are better than one» (1)
  3. Ask for the evidence in one word each: Which word in text A gave you the title? (moon) And in B? (second).
  4. Play track 61 while they read along once — the reading text is recorded, and hearing it read at speed is a small, free piece of pronunciation work.
  5. Explain the idiom in ten seconds (TB p.93): out of this world also means «extremely good» — which is why the title is a joke.
📺 On screen: track 61 in the board app, with both texts on the screen (Backup: both texts are printed on SB p.48 — set the timer on the wall clock and read text A aloud yourself if the class needs the model)
👦 Students do: read against the clock, then justify each title in one word. Expect the titles to be swapped by two or three students. Typical error: they start translating — the two-minute limit is what stops it

Reading · the gapped text
solo → pairs → class
Why: The core skill of the lesson. Decide what KIND of word the gap needs before you look at the options — that move is what transfers to every gapped text they will ever meet.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.48: six gaps in text A, three options each — the core skill of the lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The strategy in three lines, from the book's own box: A2 Key box, TB p.93: a cloze text with six gaps, three options each. The focus is vocabulary, but one or two gaps are grammar words. Strategy: read the whole text fast first; for each gap decide the WORD CLASS and the form (singular or plural, noun or verb) before you look at the options; then check the chosen word both fits the grammar and makes sense.
  2. Do question 1 together, out loud, exactly as TB p.93 does it: There is 'a' before the gap. What does that tell us? (a noun, singular, countable) Why are the others wrong? (money is uncountable; pence is plural).
  3. Then solo, 6 minutes, questions 2–6.
  4. Kagan RallyCoach to check: partner A explains 2–4, partner B explains 5–6, and the explanation must name the word class first.
  5. Key (TB p.93): 1 B · 2 C · 3 B · 4 A · 5 C · 6 C
  6. For every item, ask one student why the OTHER two are wrong. That is where the learning is.
📺 On screen: the gapped text on the screen with the options covered, uncovered gap by gap (Backup: everyone works from the paper book with a ruler or a slip of paper over the options column, and you uncover them on the board one at a time)
👦 Students do: predict the word class, then choose, then justify. Expect 4–5 out of 6. Typical error: gap 5 (understands / thinks / knows) — all three fit the meaning and only one fits the sentence pattern

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–5 p.48: six comparison questions — which capsule, A or B?

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4 in pairs, 5 minutes. The evidence rule again: Finger on the line. Key: 1 A · 2 B · 3 B · 4 A · 5 B · 6 A.
  2. The facts they are hunting in, for your own reference: Text A: a bag on the moon holds a capsule the size of a small coin, left there by the Apollo 11 astronauts in 1969, with 73 messages from 73 countries written in letters smaller than a human hair. Text B: Harold Davisson buried a capsule in Seward, Nebraska on 4th July 1975 with more than 5,000 objects and a new car inside; when he heard about a bigger one he put a second capsule on top; his grandchildren opened them both on 4th July 2025.
  3. Check with Kagan Showdown: A or B written on the mini-whiteboard, all boards up at once.
  4. ex.5 — the opinion question: Which capsule will be the most interesting for the people who open it? Why? One minute in pairs, then two answers to the class.
📺 On screen: the two texts on the screen so the evidence line can be pointed at (Backup: everyone works from the paper book and the pair that finds the line first reads it out)
👦 Students do: answer with evidence, then reveal together. Expect question 3 (which might people open first) to cause a genuine argument. Typical error: question 4 (which is smaller) answered from the photo instead of from the text

Why: A stirrer that also forces one English sentence. Teenagers who have been sitting for forty minutes need to stand before the grammar block, or the grammar block dies.

WHAT WE DO: two minutes of movement and a switch of gear

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. I say a word. If it is a NOUN, hands up. If it is a VERB, hands down. Ready: book. Watch. Ring. Picture. Kind.
  2. Let the confusion happen — every one of those words can be both, and that is the bridge into the next stage.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: put hands up or down and immediately disagree with each other. Expect exactly the argument the next stage needs. Typical error: none — being wrong here is the point

Vocabulary · words with two meanings
pairs → class
Why: A vocabulary point that is really a reading strategy: the same letters can be a different word, and context decides. Fun, fast, and it fixes a real source of misreading.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 p.49 and SB p.136: six words that are two words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: the two sentences with letters. In which sentence does letter mean 'message'? (the second) Why is that one hard to read? (the letters are tiny).
  2. ex.2 in pairs, 4 minutes: both meanings of book, kind, picture, ring, watch. Key (TB p.94): letter — a message you send · a symbol of the alphabet · book — something you read · to reserve something · kind — nice to people · another word for type or sort · picture — something you draw · another word for a photo · ring — to make a phone call · something you wear on your finger · watch — a small clock you wear · to look at something continuously, e.g. a film
  3. The concept check that makes it stick (TB p.94): What is a small clock? (watch) An apple is a … of fruit. (kind).
  4. ex.3 — go to SB p.136 and complete the ten sentences, solo, 4 minutes. Key: 1 ring · 2 picture · 3 kind · 4 watch · 5 book · 6 kind · 7 ring · 8 book · 9 picture · 10 watch.
  5. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with cards: one side the word, the other side one of its two meanings; the partner must give the OTHER meaning.
  6. Fast finishers: TB p.89: fast finishers go back to adjective order (SB p.39) and build two-adjective phrases with the new words — expensive new air conditioning, old white fridge. TB p.94: fast finishers collect more words with two meanings — light, may, right, second — and write an example sentence for each.
📺 On screen: the six words on the screen, each opening into its two meanings as the class calls them (Backup: write the six words in a column on the board and let students come up and write the second meaning next to each)
👦 Students do: give both meanings, then complete the p.136 sentences. Expect kind to be the hardest. Typical error: they treat these as translations rather than as two different words that share letters

Why: Five unconnected situations, main idea each time. The sub-skill — reset your head between recordings — is exactly what the exam lesson will need.

WHAT WE DO: Listening ex.1–2 p.49 (track 62): five separate situations, main idea each time

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 first, no audio: Read the five questions. In which ones will you hear ONE person, and in which ones two? Key: SB p.49 ex.1 first: how many people speak in each question? Key (TB p.94): one person in questions 2 and 3; two people in questions 1, 4 and 5..
  2. The strategy in one line, from the book's own box: A2 Key box, TB p.94: read every question carefully BEFORE you listen so you know who is speaking and what the situation is; underline the key words in the question and in the options.
  3. Have them underline the key words in every question and every option BEFORE you play anything. 2 minutes, in pencil.
  4. Play track 62. Then the 30-second silent check, then play it again.
  5. Key: 1 A · 2 A · 3 B · 4 A · 5 B
  6. Say the trap out loud after the check: Where marks go: all three options are mentioned in the recording. The one that is MEANT wins, not the one that is said.

pairs
Why: Three tiny words with three different positions. Taught from a model letter, so the rule arrives attached to a text they have just read for meaning.

WHAT WE DO: the GET READY stage p.49: Liliana's 1965 letter, and the three positions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set it up (TB p.94): There is a capsule from 1965 with a letter inside. Guess three things that are in it. Then read.
  2. Two questions on the letter: How many predictions does Liliana make? How many are true now? Key: Liliana's letter from 1965 with predictions for 2025. Key (TB p.94): she makes five predictions; none of them are true now..
  3. Now the three blue words. Complete the rules in pairs, 3 minutes. Key: 1 too comes at the end of a sentence · 2 as well comes at the end of a sentence · 3 at the beginning of a sentence, also HAS a comma after it.
  4. Put the three positions on the board as a picture, not as a rule: Also, ______ . · ______ too. · ______ as well. Leave it up for the writing stage.
📺 On screen: Liliana's letter on the screen with the three linking words highlighted (Backup: the letter is printed on SB p.49 — copy the three position patterns onto the board and leave them there for the rest of the lesson)
👦 Students do: read the letter, count the predictions, then derive the three positions. Expect the comma after also to be the thing nobody noticed. Typical error: Also I think with no comma — that is exactly the error the stage exists to kill

🎯 Writing out · your time capsule letter
solo → pairs
Why: Writing built on a model and a plan, started in class so nobody takes an empty page home; the peer IMPROVE stage is where the linking words actually get fixed.

WHAT WE DO: PLAN, WRITE and IMPROVE: about 60 words of predictions for the town's capsule

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. PLAN, 3 minutes, with the categories on the board: TB p.94 gives the categories to plan with: Transport (travel by bicycle / no private cars) · School (old people study / children teach them) · Free time (a lot more / fast food not popular) · Home (live under the sea / 100-floor flats). About five predictions, roughly 60 words.
  2. The two rules of the task, written up: Five predictions. Also, too or as well at least twice. Only ONE sentence may start with 'I think'.
  3. WRITE, 7 minutes on the timer, silent mode. Model on the board is Liliana's opening, which they may steal: Hello, I'm writing this in 2026.
  4. IMPROVE in pairs, 4 minutes, against a three-item checklist: (1) five predictions? (2) the linking words in the right place? (3) a comma after also? Kagan RallyRobin — each partner names one thing that works and one thing to change.
  5. The model answer, shown only AFTER they have written (TB p.94): Hello, I'm writing this in 2020. I think in 2070 there will be no cars and we will travel by bicycle. Also old people will study at school and they might go to university as well. A lot of people will live under the sea but there will be big 100-floor flats too. Life will be great! Are my predictions true? Maria
  6. Log errors silently; the feedback slot is the start of the next lesson.
📺 On screen: the plan categories and the three position patterns on the screen, and the model answer revealed only at the end (Backup: all of it in chalk on the board — the plan categories in one column, the three patterns in another, and the model answer written up while they are doing the peer check)
👦 Students do: plan, write about 60 words, then repair a partner's text. Expect the linking words to be used correctly by most and punctuated correctly by few. Typical error: Too I think there will be robots — too pulled to the front

WB Unit 8 (p.32–35)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can complete a gapped factual text by working out the word class first and write a short prediction text using also, too and as well correctly.
  • Subsidiary. Reading for gist and for comparison (SB p.48); six words with two meanings; listening for the main point of five short recordings.
  • Personal. In the gapped stage, never give the answer. Ask «what KIND of word does the gap need?» every single time, until somebody asks it themselves.
By the end, students can…
  • say what kind of word a gap needs before looking at the options
  • give both meanings of book, kind, letter, picture, ring and watch
  • catch the main point of a short recording
  • join two ideas with also, too or as well and punctuate it correctly

Structure
There will be cities under the sea and there might be cities on Mars, too. · There may not be any teachers. · Also, I think there will be cars that drive themselves. · Most doctors will be robots as well.
Form
too and as well at the END of the sentence · also at the BEGINNING, with a comma after it (SB p.49).
Pron. watch
the two-meaning words are homographs, so the pronunciation does not change — say so explicitly, it is what makes them tricky. Watch the /ʌ/ in bury — it is /ˈberi/, not /ˈbʌri/.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«There is a little bag on the moon with a time capsule inside.» What KIND of word goes after «about the size of a small ___»? → a noun, singular and countable, because of «a» before the gap (TB p.93).
«Also, I think …» — where is the comma? → after also, at the start of the sentence.
«I like pizza too.» Can I say «I too like pizza»? → not at this level: too lives at the end.
«I read a book» / «I book a table» — same word? → same letters, different word class. Only the sentence tells you which.

Assumptions: SB p.48–49, syllabus lesson 25. will / may / might is one lesson old. The gapped-text format is new to this block but not to the course (they met it in Unit 6). The two-meaning words are all known in ONE of their meanings, which is exactly what makes the second meaning stick.

They read the three options first and pick the familiar wordCover the options physically. Give them the text with the options folded under, ask for the word class of each gap in writing, and only then unfold.
“Why is my answer wrong? It makes sense.”Use the TB p.93 move: ask what comes immediately before the gap. In question 1 it is a, so the word must be singular and countable — money is uncountable, pence is plural. The grammar decides, not the meaning.
The writing turns into five sentences all starting with «I think»Put the ban up before they start: Only ONE sentence may begin with I think. Then give them the model letter's openings to steal.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
