🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use enough, too, very and not … enough in the right position (enough money but big enough) and write an article of about a hundred words that answers every question in the advert — then check it against a seven-point list before it is handed in.
What is genuinely hard
The word order is the whole difficulty and it is invisible: enough goes BEFORE a noun and AFTER an adjective, which is the opposite of the Russian pattern in half the cases. And too is not very — too means more than is wanted, and a student who writes «the film was too interesting» has said something they did not mean.
Typical error to expect
«enough big», «too much difficult» — enough goes BEFORE a noun and AFTER an adjective (enough money, big enough), and too goes in front of an adjective with nothing between them.
The article that answers two of the three questions in the advert. Exactly the same failure as last block's email, in a different costume — and the Student's Book model on the page makes the same mistake on purpose, which is the best teaching moment in the unit.
Why the lesson is built this way
The grammar half is deliberately short and physical: two rules, one drill, and a position game where students hold cards and stand in the right order. The writing half is built around a discovery — the model article on the page does NOT answer one of its three questions, and the class finds that itself before writing anything. Then the checking routine, which is the transferable skill of the lesson: seven categories, an error hunt in somebody else's text first, and only then in their own. Students who have hunted seven errors in a stranger's article check their own differently for the rest of the year.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“The article in your book is good. It is also wrong — it forgets to answer one of its own three questions, and I am not telling you which. Today: three small words that decide half a sentence, and a seven-point check that finds the mistakes you cannot see in your own writing.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a 60-second retrieval of the four future forms, and the bridge into enough / too / very

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what are you doing this evening? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — yesterday's four boxes, on the board. Four sentences, one per box, jumbled on the board: I'm seeing the dentist at four. / I'll carry that for you. / We're going to paint my room. / This time tomorrow I'll be sitting on a train.
  3. Kagan RallyRobin in pairs, 60 seconds: name the box for each. Take four answers, one per student, no discussion.
  4. Then thirty seconds of the wall: three students, three technology words each, spelled aloud.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page fifty-six, the right-hand side. Three small words. One of them changes the meaning of the whole sentence.
📺 On screen: yesterday's four boxes still on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: sort four sentences in pairs, then spell nine words aloud

Why: Two phrases and one picture carry the entire grammar of the lesson, so they go up first and everything else refers back to them.

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and put up the two model phrases

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write exactly two phrases on the board and nothing else: enough MONEY · big ENOUGH. Ask what has changed and let somebody state the rule.
  2. Then the too/very picture: a cup of coffee with steam. Very hot — I drink it slowly. Too hot — I don't drink it.
  3. Say the shape in 30 seconds: fifteen minutes of grammar, then the model article which is hiding a mistake, then you write your own and check it seven ways.
  4. Set the bar: Today nothing is handed in unchecked. Checking is not what you do if there's time — it's part of writing.
📺 On screen: the two model phrases and the coffee picture on the board — they stay up all lesson, nothing to fail

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: match the four uses, derive the position rule, then produce

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1, two minutes: the four examples matched to the four uses — 1 c, 2 a, 3 b, 4 d.
  2. ex.2: the position rule, derived not given. Two sentences (enough petrol / big enough), one question — What is in front of enough, and what is behind it? Write the rule under the two model phrases.
  3. ex.3 individually, four minutes: six items, each word used twice. Check with Kagan Showdown — everyone writes, all reveal on the signal.
  4. The two that always go wrong: item 2 (too) and item 4 (enough after an adjective). Write both on the board correctly and leave them.
  5. Fast finishers: write two more items in the same shape for the position game that follows.
📺 On screen: the position rule written under the two model phrases — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: match, derive a rule, then produce six answers and reveal them simultaneously

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 as a physical word-order task, then the pairwork on SB p.135

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Five word cards per team, held by five students: isn't · the screen · enough · big · . The team arranges itself into the correct sentence by standing in order. Ten seconds.
  2. Six rounds, six sentences, alternating between enough + noun and adjective + enough. The physical version is the point: word order is a body problem, not a rule problem.
  3. Then sit and do ex.4, the pairwork on SB p.135, four minutes, face to face.
  4. Check by asking for the position first: Before the noun or after the adjective? Then the sentence.
📺 On screen: six sets of word cards, prepared before the lesson — nothing to fail, they are pieces of paper
👦 Students do: arrange themselves into six sentences physically, then work through eight items face to face

26–30′
Move break · can I drink it?
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer on too against very

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. You say a sentence with too or very (The tea is very hot / The tea is too hot / The bag is too heavy / The film was very long); the class sits down if the thing is IMPOSSIBLE and stays standing if it is just a lot.
  2. Ten sentences at speed. Anyone who gets it wrong says the correct version aloud before standing back up.
  3. Then the pair round, thirty seconds: One thing in this school that isn't big enough.
  4. Freeze and sit: Page fifty-seven. There is an article in your book with a hole in it.
📺 On screen: no technology — the sentences are said aloud, so nothing can fail
👦 Students do: everyone judges ten sentences with their body and repairs their own mistakes aloud

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: read the advert's three questions, find the one that is not answered

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: the photograph of the smartphone and the advert with its three questions. Read the three aloud and write them on the board as a numbered list. They stay there for the whole lesson.
  2. ex.2, three minutes silent: read the model article and tick each question as you find its answer. One of the three never gets ticked.
  3. Pairs, sixty seconds: which one is missing? Answer — whether phones will become even more popular. Let them find it; do not tell them.
  4. ex.3, two minutes: everyone writes the missing sentence. Take three aloud and choose the best as a class. A possible one: Mobile phones are already really popular, so they probably won't become much more popular in the future.
  5. Then say the transferable line, once: A good article that forgets a question is a weaker answer than a plain one that answers all three. Both exams work that way.
📺 On screen: the advert's three questions numbered on the board and ticked as they are found — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: read a model with a ticking job and discover its hole themselves

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–6: the checklist named, then seven errors hunted in a real student text

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4: the Prepare to write box. Write the seven categories on a strip of card and put it on the wall: spelling · punctuation · word order · agreement · pronouns · grammar · vocabulary. It stays there until May.
  2. ex.5, three minutes: six sentences, and for each one the class names the CATEGORY of the error before correcting it — battery is (grammar), fast enough (word order), it (pronouns), wider (vocabulary), Canadian (spelling), is (agreement).
  3. ex.6 in RallyCoach pairs, five minutes: a real student's article with seven errors in it. One pen, alternate turns. The rule is on the board: Circle and label. Do not rewrite.
  4. Check the seven fast, category first: because · it is broken · It is very important · the most important thing · the amount of memory · I enjoy taking photos.
  5. Close the stage with the honest observation: You just found seven mistakes in somebody else's hundred words. You will find two in your own. That is why we swap.
📺 On screen: the seven-category strip on the wall — it is card, so it cannot fail, and it stays for the rest of the year
👦 Students do: name the category before the correction, seven times, in coached pairs
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE task 38, criteria К4 and К5 (grammar, spelling and punctuation). The seven checking categories map one to one onto the two criteria that Russian candidates lose most often on the written paper.

Why: 🎯 The article, in real time

WHAT WE DO: ex.7–8 = B1 Writing Part 2: about 100 words on «Great games for mobile phones»

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the advert aloud once and write its three questions on the board underneath the ones from the model. Three questions, three ticks, exactly as in block 4.
  2. Two minutes, in pairs, spoken not written: plan it out loud, one question each, alternating. A plan said aloud survives into the writing.
  3. Fifteen minutes, silent, clock on the board. About 100 words. You sit down and answer no language questions (Write the word you have. We fix it after.).
  4. Announce the time at minute 10 and minute 13, nothing else.
  5. Last three minutes of the stage: everyone checks their OWN text against the seven categories on the wall, in that order, with a pencil. Not reading it through — seven separate passes is the instruction, and it is the whole point.
  6. Fast finishers: rewrite the opening sentence two different ways and choose the better one.
📺 On screen: the three questions and the running clock on the board, the seven categories on the wall — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: plan aloud, write 100 words alone against a clock, then check their own text seven times

72–86′
Peer proofreading, by category
pairs → class
Why: Peer proofreading, by category

WHAT WE DO: the articles swapped and audited against the same seven categories

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap papers. Six minutes. The reader circles and LABELS with one of the seven categories — no corrections, no crossing out of another student's English.
  2. Then a second pass, two minutes, for the three questions: are all three answered? Three ticks in the margin or a cross.
  3. Ninety seconds, spoken, in pairs and in English: the reader says one thing that worked and one category that came back more than once.
  4. Papers back to their owners. Three minutes to correct their own labelled circles — the writer corrects, always.
  5. Take two articles to the floor with permission and read the good sentences aloud. Read only the good ones.
  6. Collect them. You mark them properly against the three questions plus the seven categories.
📺 On screen: the seven-category strip and the three questions, both visible during the swap — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: label a partner's errors by category, receive their own back, and do the correcting themselves

Form

Pron. watch
enough /ɪˈnʌf/ — the gh is an /f/, and half a group says «enug» for a whole year unless it is drilled · the linking in big enough is /bɪɡɪˈnʌf/, one word · and too is long: /tuː/, not /tu/
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«The coffee is too hot.» Can I drink it? → no. Too means more than I want. Very hot means I can still drink it.
«enough money» or «money enough»? → enough money. Before a noun.
«big enough» or «enough big»? → big enough. After an adjective. Say the pair aloud twice: enough money, big enough.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
enough is put in the wrong placeTwo model phrases on the board all lesson, nothing else: enough MONEY · big ENOUGH. Every wrong answer gets the same three-second treatment — point at the pair and say the correct one.
too is used to mean veryOne picture on the board: a cup with steam and a cross through it. Too = I cannot. Very = I can, but it is a lot. Check it with three quick examples before the exercise, not after.
Proofreading turns into rewriting somebody's textThe rule is written on the board: Circle and label. Do not correct. The label is one of the seven categories, and the writer does the correcting.
Fast finishers in the writingThe Teacher's Book task: write the opening sentence again in two different ways and choose the better one. Cutting and rewriting is harder than writing.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 38 (the written opinion piece) and tasks 19–25.

An article is not on the Russian paper, but everything that makes one work is: a clear opinion, a reason, an example, and a conclusion that does not repeat the opening. The checking routine taught today — spelling, punctuation, word order, agreement, pronouns, grammar, vocabulary — is worth two marks on the Russian task 38, where К4 and К5 are decided by exactly those seven categories. Teach the checklist as a habit, not as a lesson.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
