🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name twelve school subjects and say HOW somebody does something — better, more carefully, the most easily — instead of saying what they are like.
What is genuinely hard
The adjective/adverb line. In Russian one word covers both, so You speak English really good feels correct, and it is the single most common mistake at this level. On top of that, the two forms that get used most — well, better, the best — are the irregular ones.
Typical error to expect
You speak English really good instead of well — an adjective put where an adverb is needed [high priority, A2/B1]. The coursebook flags this one itself on SB p.33 ex.5.
more quicker, more easier — the comparative built twice [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The grammar is discovered from the article students have just read: all seven example sentences on p.33 are lifted straight out of the Finland text, so the adverbs arrive inside an argument about homework rather than in a table. Vocabulary comes first because the twelve subjects are what the article is made of, and because they are the words the class will need in every lesson of the unit. Pronunciation sits between the reading and the grammar as a change of gear, and it is not decoration: half the subject names are three and four syllables long and are unrecognisable with the stress in the wrong place. The practice block ends with error correction rather than production, because ex.5 on p.33 is a list of the five mistakes Russian students actually make — repairing them is worth more than ten new sentences. The productive stage puts the superlative where it belongs: on real people in this room.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Not what you are — how you do it. You are a good student and you work well are two different words in English, and today we learn the second one. By the end you can say who in this room does what best — and prove it.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, two rules of block 2 recalled and a first count of the subjects they already have

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. The block 2 rules, in one question each: Which question do you answer LAST? (the whole-text one) · What does «Not stated» mean? (the text says nothing about it). Two answers, no more.
  3. Then the switch, on mini-whiteboards: Thirty seconds — write the names of three school subjects in English. Any three. Reveal on Boards up! and count how many different ones the class has between them.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the new unit: Books open, page thirty-two. New unit — school. And the first question is: whose school is better, and how would you know?
📺 On screen: the two questions written on the board before the lesson — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: answer the two questions, then write three subjects. Expect maths, English and PE from almost everybody and very little else. Typical problem: they write the Russian names in Latin letters — that is exactly the gap the next stage fills

Vocabulary 1 · school subjects
pairs → class
Why: Twelve words matched to twelve pictures: meaning is instant, and the mingle that follows gives the repetitions. Two of them are said as letters, not as words, so they get their own drill.

WHAT WE DO: twelve subjects on twelve pictures: ex.1 p.32 (track 35) plus a Quiz-Quiz-Trade mingle

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two questions before the book, TB p.61: What is PE? (Physical Education) · What is ICT? (Information and Communications Technology). Both are said as letters, not as words.
  2. ex.1 — match the twelve subjects to pictures A–L, in pairs, 4 minutes. Key: A biology · B chemistry · C drama · D science · E geography · F foreign languages · G ICT · H design and technology · I PE · J history · K maths · L physics
  3. Play track 35 — check and repeat chorally.
  4. Sort out the science family on the board once, with a bracket: science is the umbrella; biology, chemistry and physics are under it.
  5. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with subject cards, 4 minutes: Quiz your partner. Coach if he's wrong. Trade cards. Find a new partner!
  6. Two questions from TB p.61 to finish: Which of these do you NOT study? Which subject of yours is not in the pictures? (usually music)
📺 On screen: track 35 in the whiteboard app with the twelve pictures on the screen (Backup if the board is down: read the twelve subjects aloud twice yourself and use the pictures in the paper book; the Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards are printed in advance and need no technology at all)
👦 Students do: match, repeat, then mingle with cards. Expect 8–9 of 12 unaided. Typical error: design and technology shortened to technology — accept it in speech, correct it in writing

Reading · new ways of learning
solo → pairs
Why: A text with a genuine argument in it — Finnish schools do better with less homework — so gist reading is motivated. It is also where every adverb of the grammar stage comes from.

WHAT WE DO: the article about Finnish schools on p.32: choose the title, then seven questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pre-teach two words, TB p.61: TB p.61: feature — a typical quality or an important part of something; re-enactment — making something happen again exactly as it happened, as entertainment or to help people remember. And a fact worth having ready: Pompeii was destroyed in 79 AD; the children in the project were 12.
  2. Set the gist task BEFORE they read: ex.2 — Two minutes. Three possible titles. Which one fits? Key: B — «New ways of learning»

Key: 1 They start at the age of 7. · 2 For about five hours. · 3 They have a little. · 4 Yes, they do. · 5 They think our brains understand better when knowledge is connected. · 6 a · 7 History, ICT, design and technology.
  4. Kagan RallyCoach to check: one answers and points at the line, the other checks.
  5. Then the two questions that turn a text into a conversation (TB p.61): Do you think students learn better when the school day is shorter? When there is no exam, do you think students study hard? Take three opinions and stop — the grammar stage will need them.

Pronunciation · word patterns
class → pairs
Why: Stress is what makes a long word recognisable. Three patterns instead of twelve separate words is a system they can carry into every new word of the year.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5 p.33: three stress patterns and nine long words (tracks 37–38)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.62: write university on the board and say it syllable by syllable — u-ni-ver-si-ty, five of them. Then ask the question that does the teaching: «Is it univerSIty or uniVERsity?» (uniVERsity — the stress is on the third). Mark the pattern above the word and only then open the table. Extension in teams, two minutes: add words to the first two patterns — 0o orange, window, chocolate · 0oo holiday, beautiful.
  2. ex.5 in pairs: put the nine words into the three columns, 3 minutes. Play track 38 to check and repeat. Key: 0o topic, classroom, history, science · 0oo audience, chemistry, favourite, languages · o0oo communicate, biology, geography, technology
  3. A ten-second duel to fix it: I say a word — you clap on the strong syllable. Six words, fast.
  4. Extension in teams if there is a minute (TB p.62): add two more words to the first two patterns — orange, window, chocolate (0o) · holiday, beautiful (0oo).
📺 On screen: tracks 37 and 38 in the whiteboard app with the three columns on the screen (Backup: draw the three columns in chalk and use your own voice instead of the recording — say each word twice, once with the stress exaggerated)
👦 Students do: sort the nine words, then clap the stress. Expect geography and technology to be stressed on the first syllable by half the class. Typical error: they mark the pattern by the number of syllables and forget the stress itself

Grammar · discovery · adverbs
pairs → class
Why: Guided discovery from the sentences they have just read: they sort the adverbs into simple, comparative and superlative themselves, and the one rule they have to state — the article before a superlative — is the one they forget in speech.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 p.33: sort the adverbs from the article, then build the whole table

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The lead-in from TB p.62, and it takes ten seconds: say You pronounced those words beautifully and write it up. What kind of word is «beautifully»? Does it describe WHAT you are or HOW you do it?
  2. ex.1: seven sentences from the article, sort the highlighted adverbs into simple, comparative and superlative, 5 minutes in pairs. Key: Simple adverbs: well, badly, hard · Comparative: more carefully, better, more efficiently · Superlative: the most easily
  3. ex.2 — the one rule they must state themselves: Which word do we often use before superlative adverbs? Key: the
  4. ex.3 — the sixteen-gap table, 6 minutes. Kagan Numbered Heads Together to collect: Gap seven — number twos, boards up! Key: 1 badly · 2 good · 3 well · 4 better · 5 careful · 6 carefully · 7 more carefully · 8 efficient · 9 more efficiently · 10 the most efficiently · 11 easier · 12 more easily · 13 most easily · 14 hard · 15 hard · 16 the hardest
  5. ⚠️ Reproduce the table exactly as the Teacher's Book prints it: item 11 is printed easier and item 13 most easily without the. By the pattern of the other rows one would expect easily and the most easily. Do not silently «fix» the book — tell the class the row is odd and give the regular pattern alongside it. Two minutes of honesty here saves an argument at the test.
  6. Two extra points from TB p.62, said once and written up: TB p.62: well – better – the best are the irregular adverb forms of good, and they are the ones that get used most. Two board examples worth writing before the practice: I couldn't do it more quicker (→ more quickly) and She shouted loudly something (→ shouted something loudly).
📺 On screen: the table on the screen, filled in live from students' answers (Backup: draw the same four-column table in chalk and have students come out to write their own rows — it must stay on the board until the end of the lesson either way)
👦 Students do: sort, state the rule, fill the table. Expect the irregular row (good – well – better – the best) to be the one they get wrong and then never forget. Typical error: hardly written for the adverb of hard

Grammar · practice · adverbs
solo → pairs → class
Why: Controlled practice, then correcting other people's sentences — faster and safer than producing them — and the five sentences of ex.5 are precisely the five mistakes Russian students actually make.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 (the right form) and ex.5 (five sentences with a mistake) against the clock

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4 solo, 5 minutes. Key: 1 quickly · 2 the fastest · 3 better · 4 more slowly · 5 the worst · 6 harder
  2. Kagan RallyCoach to check: Say WHY — is it a comparative or a superlative? Where is the «the»?
  3. ex.5 — the error hunt, 5 minutes. These five sentences are the five mistakes Russian students actually make, so this is the most valuable exercise on the page. Kagan Showdown: the team writes the corrected sentence and reveals on your command. Key: 1 You speak English really well. · 2 I made friends much more easily at my new school. · 3 Drama classes helped me speak more clearly and better. · 4 Catch this bus and you can get home more quickly. · 5 My brother likes the music class the best.
  4. Item 5 is the one nobody expects — My brother likes best the music class: an adverb cannot stand between the verb and its object. Say it once and write the corrected version up.
📺 On screen: the ex.5 sentences one at a time on the screen so the whole class repairs the same one (Backup: write the five sentences on the board before the lesson and uncover them one by one with a sheet of paper)
👦 Students do: solve, justify in pairs, reveal the boards. Expect sentences 1 and 3 to be caught by everybody and 4 and 5 by about half. Typical error: they correct good to well and leave more quicker untouched two lines later

74–84′
🎯 Speaking out · who does what best
pairs → class
Why: The productive outcome: real comparisons about real people in the room, which is the only context in which a superlative is worth saying. Errors are logged silently.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6 p.33 (the partner activity on SB p.136) plus real comparisons about this class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Start with the book: ex.6 sends the pairs to the activity on SB p.136 — one student has the information the other needs. Five minutes, both roles.
  2. Then the real thing, and it is where the grammar becomes worth saying. Three questions on the board: Who writes the most quickly? Who speaks English the most clearly? Who remembers new words the most easily?
  3. Rule of the stage: Every claim needs a comparison AND a reason. I think Dima writes the most quickly, because he always finishes first.
  4. Two rounds, new partner for the second. Then three claims to the whole class, and the class votes.
  5. Log errors silently on your correction sheet; do not correct aloud — the feedback slot is the start of the next lesson.
📺 On screen: the three questions and a timer on the screen (Backup: the three questions on the board and you time it on the wall clock; the pair activity is in the book on p.136 and needs nothing at all)
👦 Students do: do the book activity, then compare real people. Expect the class vote to be the loudest moment of the lesson. Typical error: He writes more quicker — note it, say nothing, it opens the next lesson

WB Unit 5 (p.20–23)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can name twelve school subjects and compare how people do things with comparative and superlative adverbs.
  • Subsidiary. Reading an article for gist and detail (SB p.32 ex.2–3); three word stress patterns (ex.5); correcting five typical adverb mistakes (ex.5 p.33).
  • Personal. Do not give the adjective/adverb rule — elicit it from «You pronounced those words beautifully». And during the speaking stage log errors silently; the clinic is the start of the next lesson.
By the end, students can…
  • name twelve school subjects and say which they are best at
  • put the stress in the right place in a long subject name
  • form and use comparative and superlative adverbs, including well–better–the best
  • correct an adjective used where an adverb is needed

Structure

Form
adverb = adjective + ‑ly (careful → carefully); long adverbs take more / the most; irregular: good → well → better → the best; hard → harder → the hardest.
Pron. watch
three stress patterns: 0o topic, history, science · 0oo chemistry, favourite, languages · o0oo biology, geography, technology. And two subjects are said as letters: ICT, PE.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«You speak English really good» — what's wrong? → good describes a person, well describes how you do it: you speak English really well.
«I did it more quicker» — why not? → the comparative is built once, not twice: more quickly.
«He runs fastest in our class» — one word is missing. → the fastest. Superlative adverbs take the.

Assumptions: SB p.32–33, syllabus lesson 16. Comparative adjectives are known from earlier years; comparative ADVERBS are new, and the difference between the two is the lesson. Of the twelve subjects they usually know seven or eight; design and technology, ICT, drama and the science/physics/chemistry split are the ones that need work.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

Comparatives are one of the few grammar topics where the exam item can be solved by rule rather than by feel, which makes them cheap marks — but only if the irregular pair well – better – the best is automatic.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the OGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
