🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Turn yesterday's paper into three named, fixable lines per student and into four repaired sentences for the whole group — and see, on the desk, the distance between September and May.
What is genuinely hard
Writing a line that can actually be acted on. «Grammar» is not a line. «Everything» is not a line. Getting a thirteen-year-old from «I'm bad at reading» to «Not stated when the text says nothing» is the entire difficulty of this lesson.
Typical error to expect
I have seen this film yesterday — the present perfect with a finished time marker. yesterday, ago, last year, in 2019 always take the past simple [high priority, A2/B1]
An email that answers two of the three questions. In Writing Part 6 and in OGE task 35 alike, an unanswered point is a lost mark whatever else is on the page [exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
This is the most valuable lesson of the year and it is built so that the student does all the work. They mark their own papers, because the person who marks the paper is the person who learns from it. They write their own three lines, and you write the page number next to each one as you walk the rows, so the homework is finished before the bell. The clinic is deliberately capped at four errors and ninety seconds each: anonymity is what makes the class willing to repair a sentence out loud, and a small fixed number is what makes the list feel finishable rather than damning. The September paper comes out at the end and not at the beginning, because a comparison only lands after the honest part is over — and it is the one stage of the year in which they see the distance travelled rather than the distance remaining. The papers are collected only after the three lines are in the exercise book: the log stays with the student, the paper comes to the teacher.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Yesterday you found out what a whole paper feels like. Today is the part that was actually worth it. You mark your own paper — every slip, including the ones you nearly had. Then you write three lines: not «grammar», not «everything», but three things I could fix with you in ten minutes. Then we repair four sentences together, with nobody's name on them. And at the end I am putting your September paper next to your May one, because you have not seen the difference and I have.”

0–5′
Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls back the one thing that is needed today rather than everything that was ever taught.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, papers handed back face down, and the rule that no score is said out loud today

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and leave the desk empty. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — papers back, face down, and the rule of the day. Hand every paper back turned over, in silence, and say nothing about any of them.
  3. Nobody turns the paper yet. First: one word each — how do you think you did? Kagan Timed Pair Share, twenty seconds each way.
  4. Then the rule, and it is the whole lesson: Today there are no scores out loud. Not yours, not anybody's. Marks come to you privately on Monday.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Yesterday you found out what a whole paper feels like. Today is the part that was worth it.
📺 On screen: nothing on display and nothing playing — the papers are face down on the desks and that is deliberately the only thing in the room, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: take their paper back without turning it and say one word to a partner. Expect the guesses to be lower than the reality for about half the group. Typical problem: someone turns the paper over immediately — one quiet hand on it is enough

5–8′
Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: the shape of the lesson and the standard a line in the log has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three lines on the board, and they stay up: 1 You mark your own paper. 2 You write three lines. 3 We repair four sentences together.
  2. Show the standard immediately, by contrast. Good lines: for and since · the ‑s in he/she/it · Not stated when the text says nothing · the third question of the email · numbers as figures, not words · was/were before the past participle
  3. Not lines: grammar · reading · everything · be more careful · listen better
  4. Three. Not thirty. If your list is long, it is useless, because you will not do any of it.
  5. And say where it ends up, now rather than later: The log stays with the student, in the exercise book. The paper comes to the teacher. Collect nothing until the three lines are copied.
📺 On screen: the three-line shape of the lesson and the two contrasting lists in chalk, and they stay up for the whole lesson — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: read the two lists and hear the standard. Expect visible relief at «three, not thirty». Typical problem: someone says all three of theirs are the same thing — that is a fine answer, and it means one line and two spare
board
8–26′
Self-marking against the key
solo, silent
Why: The student who marks the paper learns from it; the teacher who marks it learns instead. Circling every slip, including the ones that nearly worked, is what turns a score into information.

WHAT WE DO: students mark their own papers against the printed key and circle every slip

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand out one printed key per desk. Turn the paper over. Mark your own work. Circle EVERY slip — including the ones you nearly had right.
  2. Nobody swaps papers. Say why once: The person who marks the paper is the person who learns from it.
  3. They add up their own totals. There is no scale on the board today and there does not need to be one: the number is for them.
  4. Walk the rows quickly and quietly, and make your own map as you go — who will need prompts in Prepare 4 and who can be pushed from September.
  5. One instruction for anyone who finishes early: Go back to the listening. Write the correct answer next to every circle, not just the circle.
📺 On screen: one printed answer key per desk, run off before the lesson (Backup: if the photocopier died, read the key aloud item by item and students tick as you go — it is slower, but what matters is that the student does the marking, not the speed)
👦 Students do: mark their own work in their own hand and circle every slip. Expect the self-marked total to land within two marks of yours for most of the group, with the gap in the writing. Typical error: «I nearly had it» left uncircled — the rule of circling every slip is what removes it

26–36′
The error log · three lines
solo → teacher at each desk
Why: This is the stage the whole block exists for. Three named, fixable lines are worth more than any mark, because they are the only form of feedback a thirteen-year-old can act on alone.

WHAT WE DO: exactly three named, fixable lines per student, with the page written beside each

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three lines at the top of a clean page in your exercise book. What you fix before Prepare 4. Exactly three lines, and every one of them concrete enough to be fixed on a named page. «Grammar» is not a line. «Everything» is not a line. «I need to revise» is not a line.
  2. Model one on the board with your own handwriting, out loud, from a paper that is not in the room: for and since — I found four of these. That is one line.
  3. While they write, walk every desk and write the page number next to each line in their book, in your own pen. That is what turns a list into homework. The page list is: Write the page next to each line as you walk the rows, so the homework is already written by the time the bell goes: for and since → SB p.87 and p.161, WB p.61 · present perfect against past simple → SB p.91 and p.162, WB p.64–65 · reflexive pronouns → SB p.99 and p.163, WB p.68–69 · first conditional → SB p.103 and p.164, WB p.72 · the present passive → SB p.109 and p.165, WB p.76 · the past passive → SB p.113 and p.166, WB p.80–81 · the three points of an email → SB p.61 and p.83 · Not stated and reading for detail → SB p.88 and p.104.
  4. Anyone stuck gets one question and no more: Open your paper at the first circle. What is that one called?
  5. The log stays with the student, in the exercise book. The paper comes to the teacher. Collect nothing until the three lines are copied.
📺 On screen: the model line written on the board in your own hand as they watch, and left up — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write three lines and get a page number written beside each. Expect «Not stated» and «the third question of the email» to be the two most common lines in this group. Typical error: a line that names a section rather than a thing («reading») — the one-question fix at the desk handles it

36–44′
Picture stories back
pairs → class
Why: The homework from the mock comes back while it still matters, and it comes back as reading rather than as correction: they see six other ways of telling the same three pictures.

WHAT WE DO: the Writing Part 7 homework read, not corrected: six ways of telling three pictures

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand the stories back. Nothing on them is corrected in red — you have underlined, and only underlined, the past-tense verbs.
  2. Kagan Timed Pair Share, ninety seconds each way: read your partner's story out loud and count two things — how many linking words, and whether there is something about all three pictures.
  3. Two or three read to the class. The only question asked afterwards is Which picture did you hear the least about?
  4. Put the one repair that matters on the board and leave it: a story stays in the past from the first word to the last. Underline every verb in your own. Are they all past?
  5. Anyone whose story slipped into the present adds it to their log as a fourth line — the only line anybody is allowed to add today.
📺 On screen: two or three student stories read out loud, and the past-tense repair written in chalk — the board and the students' own paper are the media here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: read a partner's story and count linkers and pictures. Expect thirty-five to sixty words from most, and the middle picture to be the one that disappears. Typical error: the tense sliding into the present halfway — which is why the underlining is the only correction made

Move break
standing
Why: A stirrer between two heavy stages. Teenagers who have been marking their own mistakes for half an hour need to stand up before they are asked to look at four more.

WHAT WE DO: two minutes of movement between the honest half and the repair half

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. Books shut. Shake it out.
  2. One round, no English required and none demanded: stretch, water, window.
  3. Back down on a clap. The next twelve minutes are the useful ones.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: stand up and reset. Expect a noticeable change of mood — the marking half is over and they know it. Typical problem: nobody wants to sit back down; the clap and the one-sentence promise are enough

46–58′
Error clinic · four errors
class → mini-whiteboards
Why: Four, not twenty, and ninety seconds each. Anonymity is what makes the class willing to repair the sentence out loud, and a fixed small number is what makes the list feel finishable rather than damning.

WHAT WE DO: exactly four errors, ninety seconds each, anonymous, repaired out loud by the class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Say the rule before the first one goes up: All four of these are rewritten. Nobody in this room wrote exactly these words.
  2. One. I have seen this film yesterday. → the class repairs it chorally → I saw this film yesterday.. The self-check goes up next to it: A finished time word — yesterday, last week, in 2019, three days ago — takes the past simple, always. Self-check: find the time word first, then choose the tense. (Unit 16, SB p.91.)
  3. Two. I have played the guitar since three years. → I have played the guitar for three years.. for = how long. since = from when. Self-check: can you put «ago» after it? Then it is an amount of time and it takes for. (Unit 15, SB p.87.)
  4. Three. The school open five years ago. → The school was opened five years ago.. A passive needs be in front of the participle, and be carries the tense. Self-check: point at the word that shows the time. If there isn't one, the sentence is not finished. (Unit 20, SB p.113.)
  5. Four. An email that answers the first two questions and stops. → Three questions asked, three questions answered — and the third one answered first if you are running out of time.. Self-check: before you write, number the questions in the margin 1, 2, 3; before you hand it in, tick them off. This one costs more marks than all the grammar on this list. (SB p.61 and p.83.)
  6. After each one, Kagan Showdown: a fresh sentence with the same trap, everybody writes, boards up together.
  7. Collect the reasons with Kagan Numbered Heads Together so it is not always the same three students explaining.
  8. Close it with the number, out loud: Four. Not twenty. These four cost this group more than everything else together.
  9. Everyone marks in their own three lines which of the four are about them.
📺 On screen: the four wrong sentences written up one at a time and repaired in a second colour, and the mini-whiteboards for the Showdown rounds — the board and the boards in their hands are the media here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair four sentences chorally and write a fresh one each time. Expect the passive one to be the hardest and the email one to produce the most groans of recognition. Typical problem: the class starts hunting for the author — the «all four are rewritten» line, said first, prevents it

September beside May
solo → class
Why: The only stage of the year in which students see the distance travelled rather than the distance remaining. It does what no mark can: it makes next year's effort look like it will work, because last year's did.

WHAT WE DO: the September paper next to yesterday's, on the same desk, and one written sentence

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The September paper next to the May one, on the same desk, at the same time. Hand the September papers back by name, without comment.
  2. Put them side by side. Do not compare the marks. Compare the writing.
  3. One question, answered in writing, for themselves: One written question and one written answer, and it is not about marks: What can you write now that you could not write in September? One sentence.
  4. Kagan Think-Write-Pair-Share: they write first, then read it to one partner, then three volunteers read to the class.
  5. Do not comment on what is read out. Repeat it back, louder, and move to the next one. That is the whole technique and it is enough.
  6. For your own reference on why this stage is here at all: This is the only stage of the year in which a student sees the distance travelled rather than the distance remaining, and thirteen-year-olds need that more than they need a score. It also does something a mark cannot: it makes the next year's effort look like it will work, because last year's did.
📺 On screen: the September diagnostic papers, sorted by name before the lesson, plus the May papers already on the desk (Backup: if the September papers were not kept, use the block 1 test, or the first page of the exercise book where they wrote about themselves in September — the comparison is just as visible and takes just as long)
👦 Students do: put two papers side by side and write one sentence about the difference. Expect genuine surprise: in September most of them wrote four or five short sentences and now it is a connected text with three tenses. Typical error: they compare scores instead of texts — the question «what can you WRITE now?» steers them back

70–84′
Repair workshop
pairs
Why: Feedback is only feedback if there is time to use it. Fourteen minutes of fixing your own three lines, in pairs, is what converts the log from a list into a change.

WHAT WE DO: fourteen minutes fixing your own first line, in pairs, with the page open

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Line one. Open the page that is written next to it. Fix it now. Not read about it — fix it, in writing, on the page.
  2. Kagan RallyCoach in pairs: one works, the partner asks for the reason rather than the answer — Why that form?
  3. Pairs are made by line, not by friendship: two students with for and since sit together, two with the passive sit together. Anyone whose line is unique works with you.
  4. The minimum output is fixed and it is written on the board: Five correct sentences of your own, with the mistake you used to make written next to the first one.
  5. Last two minutes: Read your five to your partner. If any of them still has the old mistake in it, you have not finished — that is tonight.
📺 On screen: the minimum output written in chalk, and the Student's Book and Workbook pages open on the desks — paper is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: work on their own first line with a partner on the same line. Expect the for/since pair to finish first and the passive pair to need you. Typical error: they copy the rule from the grammar reference instead of producing sentences — the «five of your own» minimum is what stops it

84–90′
Wrap-up + homework
circle
Why: Self-assessment against the aim they heard at the start, so the lesson closes the loop it opened. Homework comes out of each student's own paper, not from a common list.

WHAT WE DO: what changed today, homework strictly from the log, and the bridge to the last week

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three statements on the board; each student says out loud which one is truest for them: I know what to fix. · I fixed one of them today. · I know more than I thought I did.
  2. Homework, and say the rule as well as the task: Twenty-five minutes, strictly on your three lines. The page is written next to each one. There is no «revise everything» task and there never will be.
  3. Collect the papers now — and only now, because the log is in the exercise book: The three lines stay with you. The paper comes to me.
  4. The bridge, and it is two sentences: Next lesson is the speaking paper — in pairs, at my table, eight minutes each, while everyone else does the quiz of the year. The lesson after that is the last test, and then the year is done.
📺 On screen: the three statements in chalk plus the homework line — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: choose a statement, write the homework and hand in the paper. Expect «I know more than I thought I did» to be chosen most, which is the point of putting September on the desk. Typical problem: someone still wants their score — Monday, privately, and the answer does not change

Homework

Strictly your own three lines, and nothing else. Twenty-five minutes on the three things you wrote in your error log today. the student's own error log · SB and WB pages written next to each line
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, every student has a personal error log of exactly three concrete lines, each with the page that fixes it written beside it, and has used the first of them once in class.
  • Subsidiary. Four whole-class repairs — the present perfect with a finished time marker, for against since, the passive without be, and the unanswered third content point.
  • Personal. Say no scores out loud, at any point, for anybody. The marks go in the register privately next lesson. Today the only public numbers are the four in the clinic.
By the end, students can…
  • mark their own paper against a key and circle every slip
  • name three concrete, fixable things instead of «I need to revise»
  • repair the four errors that cost this group the most marks
  • say one thing they can write now that they could not write in September

I nearly had it right — do I circle it? → yes. Every slip. «Nearly» is not a mark.
Whose paper do I mark? → my own. Nobody swaps papers today.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
A student writes «be more careful» as all three linesDo not accept it and do not argue about it. Stand at the desk, open their paper at the first circled item and ask one question: What is that one called? Name it with them, write it down, and the other two follow.
Somebody asks for their mark in front of everybodyOne sentence, the same every time: Everyone gets their mark from me on Monday, privately. Today we are doing the part that changes it. Then keep moving.
The September papers were not keptThe block 1 test does the same job. If that is gone too, use the first page of the exercise book, where they wrote about themselves in September — the comparison is just as visible and takes just as long.
The clinic turns into a hunt for whose sentence it wasChange the names and the details before the lesson, and say so at the start: All four of these are rewritten. Nobody in this room wrote exactly these words. Then it stops being interesting.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the OGE — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 35 and its four criteria (K1 content · K2 organisation · K3 grammar and vocabulary · K4 spelling and punctuation), and tasks 20–34.

This lesson is what exam preparation actually looks like when it works. Not another paper — three named lines and the page that fixes each of them. Children who learn at thirteen to write «for and since» instead of «grammar» arrive in Year 9 able to prepare themselves, which is worth more than any number of practice papers.
