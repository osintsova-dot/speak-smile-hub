🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Make comparisons accurately across the whole system — -er / more, the -est / the most, irregulars (better, worse, further), as … as, not as / so … as, less … than, the + comparative + the + comparative — and modify them (far, much, slightly, a bit); then build abstract and personal nouns with suffixes (-er, -ist, -ian, -ment, -ness, -ion, -ity, -dom).
What is genuinely hard
Two different kinds of difficulty in one lesson, which is why the halves are separated by a stirrer. Comparatives are a spelling and choice problem: -er or more, one -r or two, the or no the. Suffixes are a stem problem: the base word changes shape (creative → creativity, lazy → laziness, inactive → inactivity) and no rule saves you — this is learned by writing.
Typical error to expect
«more happier», «more easier», «the most biggest» — a double comparative. English marks the degree ONCE: either -er / -est or more / the most, never both. It is the single most common comparative mistake in Russian classrooms because Russian marks it twice quite happily (более лучший).
«She is best student in class» — the superlative needs the, and Russian has no articles to remind anyone. Same root cause as «I am student» and «I play guitar».
«Try to do it more quick», «she did it good» — comparative adverbs take more + adverb (more quickly), and well is the adverb of good. The Student's Book plants both traps in the same exercise.
Why the lesson is built this way
The grammar is taken out of the personality field the class already owns — comparing the people in this room, not abstract sentences — so every controlled item has a real referent. Guided discovery first (the class states the rule, the teacher only confirms), then a heavy controlled block, then the class stands up and compares itself. The suffix half is deliberately at the end, when accuracy energy is gone and pattern-spotting energy is still there — and it closes with the word-formation text, which is exactly the shape of the exam task nobody prepares for.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
Yesterday you learned to describe one person. Today you compare two — and this is where Russian speakers give away marks for free: «more happier», «she is best student». Half a lesson of comparisons, then something more useful than it looks: how to build a noun out of any word you already know. Bored → boredom. Concentrate → concentration. One suffix, one new word, every time.

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting at the door, then a standing vocabulary round on yesterday's adjectives

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what is the best thing that happened to you this week? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — rewind, yesterday's adjectives. Quiz-Quiz-Trade with the fifteen adjective cards from last lesson: read the definition, your partner says the word, swap cards, find a new partner. Two rounds.
  3. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): You can describe one person. Today: two. Books at page 46.
📺 On screen: the adjective cards handed out at the door as they come in. Backup: no cards? write eight of the adjectives on the board and run it as a definitions round in pairs.
👦 Students do: stand, quiz a partner, trade the card, move on — everyone speaks continuously for three minutes.

Lead-in · guess the country
desks
Why: The rule is discovered from a set the class produced by ear, which takes ninety seconds and beats any presentation of the same nine forms.

WHAT WE DO: the teacher's spoken description is stuffed with comparatives; students listen, guess, then recover the forms

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. Read this at normal speed, once: It is smaller than the US but bigger than India. It has one of the largest deserts in the world and one of the longest coasts. Probably some of the best beaches too. And some of the most dangerous animals. Only two countries reach further south than it. It is the biggest island in the world.
  2. Take guesses. (Australia.)
  3. Read it a second time: this time write down every comparative and superlative you hear.
  4. Board the harvest: smaller, bigger, largest, longest, best, most dangerous, further, biggest, most famous. Leave it up — this is the data set for the discovery stage.
📺 On screen: the harvested forms written on the board in two columns as students call them out. Backup: this stage is spoken and board-only by design.
👦 Students do: listen, guess, listen again and write — nine forms, in silence.

Grammar in context 1 · the class states the rules
desks → pairs
Why: Guided discovery costs four minutes more than presenting and buys a rule the class can state in its own words — which is the rule they will still have in June.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.46 exercise 1 — guided discovery of the whole comparison system

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books at p.46. Pairs read sentences 1–4 and sort them: which are comparatives, which are superlatives? One minute.
  2. Exercise 1a–f in pairs, six minutes. This is where they write the rules, not you. Answers: a 1 short adjective + -er · 2 consonant-vowel-consonant → double the consonant + -er · 3 -y → -ier · 4 long adjective → more + adjective · b worse, farther/further · c the + adjective + -est / the most + adjective · d more + adverb, the most + adverb · e hard–harder–hardest, early–earlier–earliest, good/well–better–best, bad/badly–worse–worst, far–further–furthest · f big difference: far, a lot, much, significantly, way; small difference: a bit, a little, slightly.
  3. Check by asking for the RULE, not the answer: why bigger and not more big? Why the happiest and not the most happy?
  4. Board the two modifiers rows and drill them, because this is what makes B2 sound B2: far better · much sooner · slightly bigger · a bit more laid-back.
  5. Anticipate here, not later: write «more happier» on the board and cross it out in front of them. Say why once: English marks the comparison once. Russian marks it twice. That is the whole error.
📺 On screen: the crossed-out «more happier» stays on the board for the rest of the lesson. Backup: none needed; the board is the only display.
👦 Students do: sort, complete the rule boxes in pairs, then answer why-questions in open class.

Controlled practice · ten errors and seventeen gaps
desks
Why: Twenty-seven controlled items in twelve minutes is the accuracy load this grammar needs. Coaching in pairs turns the check from a teacher monologue into thirty more speaking turns.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.46 exercises 2 and 3 — the accuracy block, done alone then coached

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 2 (ten sentences with mistakes) individually, five minutes. Then pairs compare before the check. Answers: 1 harder · 2 one of the most famous · 3 slightly bigger · 4 the friendliest · 5 more laid-back · 6 the moodiest · 7 more quickly · 8 much sooner · 9 far better · 10 further.
  2. Exercise 3 (the gapped text, a–q) individually, six minutes. Answers: a the most unusual · b funnier · c better · d more quickly · e longer · f more closely · g happier · h the most/more cheerful · i harder · j further · k the happiest · l more dynamically · m faster · n more colourful · o firmer · p more positive · q easier.
  3. Check exercise 3 in Rally Coach: partner A reads a–d and says which rule each one uses; partner B checks against the rule box in exercise 1; swap at e–h.
  4. Fast finishers get the three questions on the board: Who is the happiest person you know? How do they show it? What could you copy from them?
  5. Do not check every item aloud. Read out the answers to c, h, l and o only, and let pairs settle the rest — those four are the adjective/adverb traps.
📺 On screen: the three fast-finisher questions on the board from the start of the stage. Backup: no equipment involved.
👦 Students do: write alone against the clock, then coach a partner through the reasons — everybody has to name a rule, not just a form.

36–44′
Stirrer · the class compares itself
open floor
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.46 exercises 4–5 on the feet: superlatives about real people in the room

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands with the book. In pairs, exercise 4: choose ONE person in the class for each description, and both partners must agree. Four minutes.
  2. Now Pairs Compare: each pair joins another pair and defends its choices. The rule: you may not say a name until you have said the sentence. The most resourceful person in this class is …
  3. Two minutes of whole-class feedback: you say the description, the class says the name in chorus, the named student says whether they agree — and use a comparative to disagree: I'm less resourceful than Sasha.
  4. Keep it fast and kind. Any description that could sting, you skip.
📺 On screen: the sentence frames on the board: The most … in this class is … · I'm less … than … · She's far more … than I am. Backup: dictate the three frames if the board is full.
👦 Students do: stand, agree in pairs, defend in fours, then answer in chorus — the whole class is speaking or listening to a name they care about.

44–52′
Fixed comparisons · six sayings that native speakers actually use
desks
Why: Fixed comparative expressions are the cheapest fluency in the unit and they double as listening anchors — a student who knows «the sooner the better» hears it in a recording.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.46 exercises 6–8 — as … as, less … than, and six proverbs built on comparatives

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 6, books closed first: you know comparatives with -er and more. What other structures compare? Think of two. Then open and check (a as … as = the same; b not as … as / not so … as / less … than = the second is less).
  2. Exercise 7 in pairs, three minutes. Answers: 1 the further/farther you run, the healthier · 2 the older you are/get, the easier · 3 further and further away · 4 so well as · 5 are not/aren't as/so good as.
  3. Exercise 8 as a whole class, fast — and the person who answers must also say when you would use it. Answers: 1 never (Better late than never) · 2 done (Easier said than done) · 3 better (The sooner the better) · 4 safe (Better safe than sorry) · 5 merrier (The more the merrier) · 6 worse (going from bad to worse).
  4. Tell them straight: learn these six by heart. They are free marks in speaking, and Russian has an equivalent for every one of them.
📺 On screen: the six sayings written up as they are answered — they stay up until the end of the lesson. Backup: no display needed.
👦 Students do: predict with books closed, complete in pairs, then answer aloud with the situation attached.

Developing vocabulary · nouns out of everything
desks → pairs
Why: Word formation is the highest-value low-effort block in the course: the same six suffixes recur all year, in the unit test, in Cambridge Part 3 and in EGE tasks 26–31.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.47 exercises 1–3 — noun suffixes, ending on the word-formation text

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. On the board: teacher · musician · biologist. What is the root? What is the suffix? Elicit more: driver, librarian, chemist. Then ask for other noun suffixes and take whatever comes (-ment, -ion, -ness, -ship, -dom).
  2. Exercise 1 (people) — two minutes. Answers: leader · psychologist · assistant · historian (stress: inVENTor, LEADer, psyCHOLogist, asSIStant, hisTORian). Then have them underline the stress in each and say the words back to you.
  3. Define the term before exercise 2: an abstract noun is a feeling or an idea — hunger. A concrete noun is a thing you can touch — food. Exercise 2, three minutes. Answers: shyness · freedom · preference · appearance · involvement · connection (or connector) · relative / relation / relationship.
  4. Exercise 3 — the word-formation text, seven minutes, individually and in silence. Teach the procedure aloud first: read the whole text once. Then for each gap look at the words either side — do you need a noun, an adjective or an adverb? Then change the word. Then read the sentence again and check it makes sense. And check singular or plural.
  5. Answers: a boredom · b distractions · c disappearance · d relaxation · e researchers · f concentration · g writer · h inactive · i creativity · j scientists · k musicians · l freedom · m laziness · n depression. Take the four that change the stem (disappearance, creativity, laziness, inactive) and write them big.
  6. Rally Coach for the check: partner A reads a–g with the reason for the word class, partner B does h–n.
📺 On screen: the procedure for word formation written as four numbered steps and left up. Backup: it is also the box on Workbook p.95, which they have at home.
👦 Students do: predict, complete, then talk each other through the reason for each form — word class first, spelling second.

🎯 Freer practice · is being bored good for you?
groups of four
Why: The lesson's two halves meet here: today's nouns inside today's comparative structures, on a question teenagers actually have an opinion about.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.47 exercise 4 — the new nouns used in an argument, not in a gap

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board three questions: Is being bored good for us? Does boredom help us relax or be more creative? Or does it just make us lazy?
  2. Two minutes of silent note-making — the rule is that every student must use at least three of today's nouns (boredom, creativity, concentration, distraction, laziness, relaxation, freedom).
  3. Round Robin in fours: one sentence each, round the group, twice — nobody may repeat a group-mate's argument.
  4. Then open it up for three minutes and push for comparatives: is it MORE creative than scrolling? Is it the worst thing about school?
  5. Correct nothing during the talk. Note three errors and put them on the board at the end for the class to fix.
📺 On screen: the three discussion questions on the board. Backup: nothing to fail — spoken stage.
👦 Students do: make notes, then speak in turn round the group, then argue openly.

whole class
Why: Delayed correction lands when the class does the fixing. Slipping one correct sentence into the three stops them from «correcting» at random.

WHAT WE DO: the errors you collected are fixed by the class, not by you

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write three real sentences from the last stage on the board, anonymised, one of which is actually correct.
  2. Thirty seconds in pairs: which one is right? Fix the other two.
  3. Take the corrections from the class and write the fixed versions. Name the rule each time in three words: once only · the + superlative · more + adverb.
  4. Last thirty seconds: everyone writes the corrected sentence they personally needed into the back of the notebook.
📺 On screen: the three sentences on the board, one already correct. Backup: this is chalk-and-talk by design.
👦 Students do: spot and fix in pairs, then copy the one that was their own error.

Workbook p.32–33
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will be better able to compare people and things accurately, using the full comparison system and its modifiers, in the context of personality.
  • Subsidiary. Forming nouns with suffixes and completing a word-formation text; noticing stress shift (creative → creativity); fixed comparative expressions.
  • Personal. Do not check answers item by item. Read out four answers, let the pairs settle the rest, and use the time you save on the freer stage.
By the end, students can…
  • compare two people with -er / more and the modifiers far, much, slightly
  • use the superlative with the, and the irregular forms better, worse, further
  • use as … as, not so … as and the + comparative, the + comparative
  • build a noun from a verb or an adjective with the right suffix
  • complete a word-formation text and check it for word class and number

Structure
She's far more laid-back than her brother. · He's the friendliest person in this class. · I'm not as resourceful as she is. · The harder you work, the luckier you get.
Form
short adjective + -er / -est (bigger, the biggest; happy → happier) · long adjective: more / the most + adjective · irregular: good–better–best, bad–worse–worst, far–further– furthest · as + adjective + as · not as / so + adjective + as · less + adjective + than · the + comparative …, the + comparative … · modifiers: far, much, a lot, significantly, slightly, a bit + comparative.
Pron. watch
the suffix is almost never stressed and almost always reduces to a schwa: LEADer /ˈliːdə/ · psyCHOLogist /saɪˈkɒlədʒɪst/ · asSIStant · hisTORian · SHYness · FREEdom · apPEARance. Two words shift the stress when the suffix arrives — CREative → creaTIVity, INactive → inacTIVity — and that shift is a listening mark as well as a speaking one
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«more happier»: How many times do we mark the comparison in English? — Once.
superlative: Is there more than one «the friendliest person»? — No. So which word do we need before it? — the.
as … as: «Dogs are as clever as cats» — is one cleverer? — No, the same.
ICQ before the word-formation text: Do you change the word or copy it? Do you check singular or plural? How many minutes?

Assumptions: Students formed simple comparatives and superlatives at A2/B1 and know the four irregular forms. They have not systematically met the modifiers, the + comparative + the + comparative, or noun suffixation as a productive system.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«More happier» and «the most biggest» survive the explanation.Cross the phrase out on the board at the discovery stage and leave it crossed out for the whole lesson. Every time it reappears, point at the board and say nothing.
Spelling: bigger with one -g, happyer, the funniest.The three spelling rules go on the board as three examples, not as prose: big → bigger · happy → happier · large → larger. Test them in the exit slip, not in a lecture.
The suffix half is squeezed because comparatives overran.The stirrer at 36–44 is the pressure valve: cut it to four minutes if you are behind. Do not cut exercise 3 — the word-formation text is the highest-value item in the lesson.
Students change the meaning instead of the word class (creative → creatively, when a noun is needed).The four-step procedure on the board: read the whole text, look at the words either side, decide noun/adjective/adverb, then change. Enforce step three aloud in the Rally Coach check.
Comparing real classmates goes personal.Only positive descriptions are allowed on the floor, and you pre-read exercise 4 for anything that could sting. If a description lands badly, take it yourself: Actually, the moodiest person here is me before coffee.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 19–25 (grammar in a text — degrees of comparison are a named line of the codifier) and tasks 26–31 (word formation with suffixes).

Comparatives and superlatives look easy and are quietly expensive: the paper tests the spelling of the form (the happiest, further, worse) and the choice between more and -er. The noun suffixes in the second half of the lesson are literally the shape of tasks 26–31 — given BORED, write boredom; given CONCENTRATE, write concentration. Both halves of today are exam grammar dressed as unit language.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
