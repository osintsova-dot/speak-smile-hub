🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use about forty words for technology — the objects (satnav, pen-drive, external hard drive), the adjectives that describe them (cutting-edge, lightweight, unbreakable, eco-friendly), the everyday nouns (cloud, stream, firewall, earbud) and ten compound nouns — and read a science article by following its reference words to put six missing sentences back into it.
What is genuinely hard
Two different difficulties in one lesson. The vocabulary is mostly known passively from Russian tech-speak (стрим, фаервол, облако) but the class cannot SAY it: the stress is wrong, the compound nouns split into two words, and the English adjective set is genuinely new. The reading is harder than it looks — students choose the missing sentence by topic («it's about batteries, so B») instead of by reference (it, they, this), which is the only thing that actually decides the answer.
Typical error to expect
«USB-port», «touchscreen», «drop down menu» — the compound nouns of this unit are spelled in a fixed way and the Test yourself page counts letters. A right word written wrong scores nothing.
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary is not a list: it arrives through a describe-and-guess game (ex.1–2), then through a real text about words technology stole from nature — stream, net, cloud, tweet, virus (ex.3) — so every item is met in a sentence before it is met on the wall. The reading is a genuinely astonishing article about graphene, a material one atom thick that two Manchester professors isolated in a Friday-night experiment with sticky tape. The exam task on it is done TWICE: once quickly for the answer, once slowly with the reference word underlined in colour, because that second pass is the whole skill.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two professors, a Friday night and a piece of sticky tape. That is how the strongest material on the planet was discovered — it is one atom thick and it is bulletproof. Today: the English words for the technology you use every day, and how to put six sentences back into a text you have never seen.”

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 with tracks 47 and 48: eleven objects and fourteen adjectives, met through description

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Play track 47 once for the eleven objects, books open, and drill the four that will be mispronounced: SATnav · PEN-drive · exTERnal HARD drive · WIREless CHARger.
  2. ex.1 in pairs, four minutes: take turns to describe one object and say what it does; the partner names it. Stems on the board — It's used for -ing … · It's the thing you … with · It's a kind of …
  3. Take two of the best descriptions to the floor and let the class guess. Ninety seconds, no more.
  4. Play track 48 for the fourteen adjectives. Pre-teach only what is genuinely opaque — brittle (breaks easily), chunky (big and thick), heavy-duty (stronger than usual), rustproof.
  5. ex.2 in pairs, three minutes: for each adjective name a good example object; the partner guesses which adjective you were given. Then invite three pairs to say only their object and let the class produce the adjective.
  6. Everything confirmed goes onto the wall, written by the student who said it.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 with track 49 (nine gaps) and ex.4 (ten compound nouns) under a clock

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Frame the text in one line before they read it: Every word in this box used to mean something in nature. A stream was water. A net caught fish. A cloud was in the sky.
  2. ex.3, four minutes, individually: nine gaps in the University of Leeds text. Check by nomination — a stream, b net, c cloud, d storage, e earbud, f tweet, g frozen, h firewall, i virus.
  3. As each answer is confirmed, its owner writes it in column three of the wall and says the definition in their own words. The Teacher's Book asks for exactly this and it is the difference between recognising and knowing.
  4. ex.4, three minutes in pairs: match box A to box B to build ten compound nouns. Answers: battery charger, bookmark, clickbait, download/upload, drop-down menu, landline, operating system, touch screen, update, USB port.
  5. Point out the double life of four of them: bookmark, download, upload and update are verbs as well as nouns. Then the sixty-second spelling round with the book shut — one point per word, minus one for a wrong space.

Move break · could you live without it?
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Seventeen-year-olds who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies quietly.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer built on ex.5 — the room votes with its feet

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two ends of the room: COULD LIVE WITHOUT IT and COULD NOT. The middle exists but is discouraged.
  2. You call out an item from the wall — landline · smart speaker · wireless charger · earbuds · satnav · clickbait — and everybody walks.
  3. After each walk one person from each end gives a one-sentence reason using a wall word. Six items, six walks, twelve sentences, nobody sitting.
  4. Finish with the ex.5 questions as a quick standing Quiz-Quiz-Trade: Do you ever bookmark websites? How often are you a victim of clickbait?
  5. Freeze and sit: Page one hundred and eleven. Three photographs, and all three are the same material.
📺 On screen: the six items are called out, not shown — nothing to fail and nothing to prepare
👦 Students do: commit physically to six statements and produce twelve reasoned sentences with the new lexis

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: the three photographs, then one gist question over the whole article

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1, ninety seconds in pairs: what do the three photographs show? Take ideas, then tell them — a phone made with graphene, a scientist holding a flexible graphene sheet, and the atomic structure itself.
  2. The story, in two sentences, and it is worth telling properly: two professors in Manchester isolated graphene in one of their «Friday night experiments» — the fun ones that had nothing to do with their research — using sticky tape. The university still holds those Friday meetings.
  3. Say the rule for the first read: You will not understand every word and you do not need to. One question only.
  4. ex.2, four minutes, silent, books flat: which of the three summaries fits the whole text? Answer 2.
  5. Take the reasoning, not just the letter: 1 is wrong because the discovery is only in paragraph one; 3 is wrong because no other material is discussed. Two students say why, aloud.
  6. Pairs, one minute: what is the single most surprising fact so far? Answers usually land on bulletproof or on one atom thick.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: six sentences put back into the article, every answer proved by a reference word

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Name the format before anything else: Six gaps, seven sentences, one is extra. The answer is never decided by the topic — it is decided by a word that points backwards.
  2. Demonstrate on the board with option a: «It's brittle.» Ask What is IT? and make the class find the noun in the previous sentence. That is the whole method, shown in ninety seconds.
  3. ex.3 individually, six minutes, clock visible. The rule: underline the reference word in the option and the noun it points to in the text, in the same colour.
  4. Pairs compare — underlinings first, letters second. Two minutes.
  5. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: 1 d, 2 c, 3 a, 4 b, 5 g, 6 f. For each one, the nominated student reads the reference word and the noun, not the sentence.
  6. Close the stage by naming the transferable habit out loud: it, they, this and so do the work in every gapped text they will ever meet.
📺 On screen: the six answers with their reference words listed on the board as they are confirmed — chalk only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: work alone with a two-colour underlining for every answer, then read the proof aloud instead of the answer
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE task 11 (six sentence parts put back into a text). Same machine, different clothes: the answer is decided by a reference word, and candidates who match by topic lose four marks in three minutes.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: after the class check, take two of the six options and ask what would change if the reference word were different (it → they). Three minutes, taken from the words-from-context stage, which survives with six words instead of nine.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4: the highlighted words worked out from the text, not from a dictionary

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4 in pairs, five minutes: isolate, flake, vast, stem from, thermally, circuit, lifespan, bulletproof, layer. Rule: the definition must come from the sentence around the word.
  2. Check quickly and give the compact versions: isolate — separate one substance from others · flake — a small flat piece · vast — extremely large · stem from — come from · thermally — to do with heat · circuit — a closed system electricity flows around · lifespan — how long something lasts · bulletproof — bullets cannot go through · layer — a sheet covering a surface.

  4. This is the moment the two halves of the lesson meet: the adjectives learnt at minute fifteen are used to describe a real material at minute seventy.

🎯 Critical thinkers · which application actually matters
individual → pairs → class
Why: 🎯 Critical thinkers · which application actually matters

WHAT WE DO: ex.5: the uses of graphene ranked, defended and argued in the unit's own vocabulary

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three minutes, silent, individually: re-read the article and list every application of graphene it mentions — faster computer chips, unbreakable screens, batteries that charge in minutes, protection against mosquitoes, bulletproof material, longer lifespan for devices.
  2. Each student picks the MOST useful and the LEAST useful, and writes one sentence for each with a reason. Rule: every sentence contains one adjective from the wall.
  3. 🎯 Kagan Timed Pair Share, two minutes each way: read your two sentences; the listener asks one follow-up question — Why that one? Who would it help?
  4. Then take the disagreement to the floor for five minutes: Of all the uses of graphene in the text, which one would change YOUR life, and which one is just a headline? What makes you say that? Insist on the discussion rules the book keeps repeating — give a reason, let others finish, respond to what was actually said.
  5. Correct only two things today: adjective placement and compound nouns. Everything else waits.
📺 On screen: the list of applications built on the board by the class as they call them out — chalk only
👦 Students do: rank six applications, write two justified sentences each, then defend a choice against a partner and against the room

Pron. watch

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«A landline» — is it a phone you can carry? → no, it is connected by wires. And is it one word or two? → one.
«The text says It's brittle. What is it?» → the material the screen is made of. That question, asked six times, IS the exam task.
«Cutting-edge» — is it about a knife? → no, it means very modern. Which of your devices is cutting-edge?

Likely problemWhat you do
The class translates instead of describingThe rule for ex.1 is said before it starts and enforced twice: No Russian, no translation — tell me what it DOES. Give the two stems on the board (It's used for -ing, It's the thing you … with) and the game runs itself.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 10–18 (the reading section — science and technology is a standing topic of the paper) and tasks 32–38 (lexical choice).

The missing-sentence task the class does today on the graphene text is the closest Cambridge relative of EGE task 11: six gaps, seven options, and the answer is decided by reference words (it, they, this) rather than by topic. That is exactly the reading skill the Russian paper rewards and the one weak candidates never learn — they match by subject matter and lose four marks in three minutes. The technology lexis behind it is exam currency too: it prints in the lexical section and it is the vocabulary of the speaking card about gadgets.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
