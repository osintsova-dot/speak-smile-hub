🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Meet the three exam formats of Exam success 9–10 with a named strategy for each: word formation inside a text (SB p.134 ex.1), multiple matching (ex.2, ten questions to four people) and a three-option listening (p.148, track ES4) — and then write a story of 140–190 words that begins with a sentence somebody else wrote.
What is genuinely hard
Three different kinds of difficulty in one lesson. Word formation is not vocabulary, it is grammar: the gap needs a plural, a negative prefix or a past participle, and the student who knows the word still writes the bare stem. Multiple matching punishes the reader who hunts for the same word — the paper says it openly at the top of the page, the words in matching texts may not be the same. The listening is one long interview with seven questions, so a student who loses question 3 loses questions 4 and 5 as well unless they have been taught to let go. And the story is the one genre this class has never written: it needs narrative tenses, sequence linkers and a first line that is not theirs.
Typical error to expect
Writing the stem. «so that nothing is expect» instead of unexpected, «a lot of description» instead of descriptions. In the Russian paper this is exactly tasks 26–31, and it is the same loss: the student knew the word and still scored zero because the form was not changed. The fix is mechanical and it works — write the part of speech next to every gap BEFORE writing any word, and read the sentence back with your answer in it.
Why the lesson is built this way
Each format gets a strategy, then the task, then a check that names the trap — never the task alone. The two reading tasks come first while heads are fresh, then a physical break that is itself a matching drill, then the listening, then the speaking that feeds the story, then sixteen protected minutes of writing. The Collaborative project of the block is launched in the last four minutes so that the two Reach higher lessons have something to grow: groups, roles and research areas are fixed today and the projects are presented in lesson 68.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Today you meet the three tasks that decide most of an exam paper, and each of them has one habit that pays for itself. Word formation: name the part of speech before you write. Matching: never look for the same word, look for the same idea. Listening: answer the question in your head before you read the three options. Then you write a story — and the first sentence is already written for you.”

Why: Word formation · the part of speech comes first

WHAT WE DO: SB p.134 ex.1: the exam tip, a gist read, the part-of-speech step in pencil, then the eight gaps

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the Reading exam tip at the top of p.134 aloud, then ask for it back in their words: Read the text first. Think about the type of word that is missing.
  2. Gist before gaps (2 minutes). Books open, gaps ignored: What is virtual travel for, according to this text? Two answers, no more. Nobody fills a gap in a text they have not understood.
  3. The step that saves the marks (2 minutes). In pencil, next to each of the eight gaps, they write only one thing: noun, adjective, adverb or verb. No words yet. Walk the room and check this before you let them go on.
  4. The gaps (4 minutes), individually. Silence. Then the reading-back rule: every answer is read back inside its own sentence before it is kept.
  5. Kagan Rally Coach (3 minutes). A explains gaps a–d, B explains e–h. The coach may say only two things: Why? What part of speech? Nothing else. It stops the stronger partner dictating.
  6. Class check, and name the three traps as you go: b needs a negative prefix, d needs a plural, e needs a past participle. Those three are the whole task.
📺 On screen: books open at p.134 — nothing needs displaying; the three strategy lines are already on the board
👦 Students do: read for gist, label eight gaps by part of speech, fill them, then coach a partner through four of them
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE tasks 26–31 (word formation). The Cambridge task and the Russian task are the same machine: a text, a stem in capitals, and marks lost to a missing plural or a missing prefix rather than to unknown vocabulary. The part-of-speech step works identically in both.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: after the class check, put three Russian-format items on the board (PROBABLE, EXCITE, READ inside one sentence each) and give four minutes in pairs. Take the time from the speaking stage, which works with one rotation instead of two.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.134 ex.2: ten questions matched to four people, every answer written with its proof

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the second exam tip aloud — the words in matching texts may not be the same; look for synonyms — and give one example on the board before they start: question 6 says immediacy, and Sanjeev says it's instant. Same idea, no shared word.
  2. Questions first (2 minutes). They read the ten questions and underline the key idea in each — one or two words only. Not the whole line.
  3. The task (5 minutes), individually. The rule that makes it work: every letter is written with two or three words from the text beside it. A letter with no proof does not count.
  4. Kagan Numbered Heads Together (3 minutes). Teams of four compare and must agree on all ten. Then you call a number and that student answers for the team — with the proof, not just the letter.
  5. Class check with the proof lines read aloud. Stop on two: question 5 (they gather together all the news from other sites, so what you read must be true) and question 9 (when BuzzFeed broke a story about a sports hero of mine I realised how good they were = changed their mind).
  6. Point at the rubric one more time: the people may be chosen more than once — C is the answer three times and that is not a mistake.
📺 On screen: the two proof examples written on the board as they come up — chalk only, no display needed
👦 Students do: underline ten key ideas, match with written proof, reach team consensus, then answer for the team when their number is called

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that is the matching task done with feet instead of pens

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Four corners: A Pierre · B Sarah · C Maria · D Sanjeev. Books closed and left on the desks.
  2. You read a paraphrase — not the original line — and everybody walks: This person reads the news last thing at night. · This person is annoyed by the advertising. · This person likes the way the site looks. · This person trusts a site because it collects stories from everywhere.
  3. After each one, a student in the correct corner says the words from the text that prove it. If nobody can, everybody goes back and opens the book — that is a fair outcome and it teaches the same lesson.
  4. Six paraphrases, six walks, then straight to their seats for the listening.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.148: the exam tip, the lead-in question, then track ES4 played twice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Lead-in (2 minutes), p.148 ex.3. In pairs: How important is it for teenagers to know about world news? Two answers to the floor, then stop — this is a warm-up, not the lesson.
  2. Read the Listening exam tip aloud: Read the questions quickly. As you listen, try to answer them yourself before you look at the options. Give them forty seconds to read the seven stems, options covered with a hand.

Speaking that feeds the story
pairs, standing
Why: A story task collapses when students have no idea to write about. Eight minutes of talking produces the ideas, and it puts the class on its feet between two long sitting stages.

WHAT WE DO: the two speaking questions of p.148, run as a moving pair task, harvested for story ideas

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Stand Up–Hand Up–Pair Up: two rotations, two minutes each way, standing.
  2. Rotation 1, p.148 ex.5: What would you like to learn that is new for you? How would you learn it?
  3. Rotation 2, p.148 ex.6, and this one matters: What can go wrong with technology? What might the effect be? Tell them why they are answering it — Everything you say now is material for the story you are about to write.
  4. Harvest six answers onto the board in note form: a computer that switches itself on, a message from nobody, a phone that records without being asked, a lost file, a hacked account, a smart speaker that answers a question nobody asked.
  5. Leave that list up. It is the idea bank for the next forty minutes and it means nobody sits in front of a blank page.

WHAT WE DO: the task taken apart and a four-box plan on paper before a single sentence is written

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the advert on p.148 ex.7 with them: the story must BEGIN with the given sentence, must include a prize and a happy ending, and must be 140–190 words.
  2. The three questions the Teacher's Book asks, put to the class and answered by them: Which tenses will you use? Which linkers? How do you make a story interesting? Elicit and write: past simple + past continuous + past perfect · at first, then, later, in the end · adjectives and adverbs.

  5. Plans checked at a glance as you walk — four boxes with words in them, then they may start.

Why: 🎯 Writing the story · sixteen protected minutes

WHAT WE DO: 140–190 words, in silence, with the plan and the board list in front of them

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Sixteen minutes, silent, individual. Say it once and then protect it: no questions, no dictionaries, no talking. If they do not know a word they write around it, exactly as they will have to in an exam.
  2. Announce the clock twice only — at eight minutes and at thirteen — and say nothing else.
  3. Circulate with a pencil and mark nothing. Note two or three things you see for the delayed correction at the end: a tense that slipped, a linker used well, a first line that was changed.
  4. For the student who freezes, one question only, quietly: What is in box three of your plan? Do not give content.
  5. At sixteen minutes, pens down and a word count written at the bottom. Anybody still writing finishes the last two sentences at home and hands it in with the Workbook pages.
📺 On screen: nothing new on the board — the four-box plan and the six ideas stay, and the room is otherwise bare
👦 Students do: write a 140–190-word narrative from a given opening, count the words, and stop on time

Structure
the language of exam rubrics and of narrative: complete the text with the correct form of the words given · the people may be chosen more than once · your story must begin with this sentence · at first … then … in the end
Form
no new grammar is presented. What is presented is the grammar of a gap: nouns take plurals, adjectives take prefixes (un-, in-, im-), verbs take participles, adverbs take -ly. In the story: past simple for the events, past continuous for the background, past perfect for what happened before.
Pron. watch
the exam words in today's text carry stress shifts the class will get wrong: ATtract → atTRACtion → attractions · deSCRIBE → deSCRIPtion · VARy → VArious · acTUALly said in three syllables, not four. Drill each one as a pair, base word then answer, immediately after the check.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Everybody writes the stem in at least two gapsThat is why the part-of-speech step at 8–20 is done in writing, in pencil, before a single word is filled in. Check that step before you let them fill the gaps — it takes ninety seconds and it is the whole lesson.
They match by the same word and get 3, 5 and 10 wrongMake the proof compulsory: every letter is written with two or three words from the text next to it. A letter with no proof does not count in the check, and after two rounds nobody guesses.
A student loses question 3 of the listening and then loses 4 and 5Say it before you play: If you miss one, leave it and listen forward. The recording will not wait and the next answer is coming. Then play it twice, as the exam does, and mark only after the second play.
Sixteen minutes is not enough for a storyIt is enough for a planned one, and that is the point of the six-minute planning stage. Say the rule in advance: Anybody who is still writing at the bell finishes the last two sentences at home and hands it in with the Workbook pages.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 26–31 (word formation), tasks 10–18 (the multiple-matching reading) and tasks 1–9 (listening) — today the class meets all three in their Cambridge form.

Exam success 9–10 is the closest the course gets to a Russian paper without being one.
