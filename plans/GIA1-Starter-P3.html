WHAT WE DO: 6 numbers by ear with -teen/-ty traps → digits onto a slip; self-check; score privately

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. I dictate at normal speed: thirteen · forty · fifteen · fifty · eighteen · eighty.
  2. Write the DIGITS. You've known numbers for five years. By EAR, at speed — that's the real game.
  3. The key is on the slide: 13 · 40 · 15 · 50 · 18 · 80. Score in the margin.
  4. The reveal: The difference is STRESS: thirTEEN — THIRty. Fix the stress, win the listening. Rematch in Pair 4.
📺 On screen: key slide · timer (Backup: everything on the slide goes onto the board before the lesson (or onto printed cards, one per crew).)
👦 Students do: they catch the numbers solo, count honestly. Expected: 3–4/6, the -teen/-ty pairs fall apart. Typical error: 13→30 — exactly that one.

WHAT WE DO: p.10 ex.1–4: order → gaps → tens in words → audio 5 dictation; a gesture stress drill

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ex.1–2 as a race, 3 min: numbers in order + the missing 1–20 in words (keys in TB; -teen on 13/15/18, -ty on 20).
  2. Gesture drill: TEEN — fist forward on the 2nd syllable, TY — a clap on the 1st. Chorus: thirteen-thirty · fifteen-fifty · eighteen-eighty.

  4. Audio 5 (ex.4): 6 numbers in words by ear (key: twenty · eighty · twelve · eighty-five · thirty-eight · fifty-four). (Backup: no track — I dictate it myself.)

👦 Students do: they order, write in words, drill the stress with gestures, catch the dictation. Expected: the gesture solves 13/30. Typical error: fourty — anchor: forty with no u!

WHAT WE DO: Kagan Mix-Pair-Share: a name-spelling race — we mill about, pair up on the signal, ask-and-spell; the author's signature = the check

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Model: What's your name? — Dasha. — How do you spell it? — D-A-S-H-A.
  2. Launch Mix-Pair-Share: Mix — walk around! … PAIR UP! Ask, spell, write it down! The LISTENER writes it down; the author's signature = the check. 4 rounds — a new partner each time, everyone at once.
  3. I name the traps in advance: the letter that looks the same in both alphabets (C = /siː/ in English, not “es” like in Russian!).
  4. +1 for every cleanly written name (the Checker is the name's own author).

👦 Students do: they mill about, spell out, write down — all at once. Expected: they write each other's names correctly — practical! Typical error: E/I by ear — a re-ask: /iː/ or /aɪ/?

🏁 PRODUCT · The Grand Code Relay
stage · crews

WHAT WE DO: final relay: the numbers station → the spelling station → the book station; crews go through all three

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 3 stations in a circle, 3 min per station: 1) Numbers — the Timer dictates 4 numbers with traps, the crew writes; 2) Spelling — decode a word + code a reply word; 3) Book — 2 Challenge-format questions with pages.
  2. Station judge — one from the “resting” crew each time (roles at work!).
  3. Scoring: cleanliness × speed; gold run = +5.
  4. Final line: Numbers, letters, book — the codebreaker kit is complete.
📺 On screen: station cards · timer · leaderboard
👦 Students do: they go through the stations as a crew — everyone is busy, the judges are in the game too. Expected: a dense flow; the stations even out the pace. Typical error: haste loses the hyphen in numbers — the judge catches it.
🏆 The bar for the product (the judges' rubric): Fluency — pauses no longer than 3 sec (1–3) · Accuracy — forms are clean (1–3) · Richness — details, colours, numbers, connectors (1–3). 9/9 = a “gold run”, +5 to the leaderboard.		station cards · timer

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, learners can catch 2-digit numbers by ear and spell words at speed (alphabet code decoded, own name spelt clean) and navigate the coursebook like seniors (Classroom Challenge won).
  • Subsidiary. -teen/-ty stress as the key to telling them apart (13/30!); a Fun Facts quiz with numbers; international words → the confidence that “we already understand a lot”; onboarding into the Get Involved format (WDYT?, units, the project planner).
  • Personal. The gap diagnosis continues: numbers BY EAR and the alphabet AT SPEED — things they've “known since year 1” but lose under the timer. Speed is an honest examiner, no hard feelings.
By the end, students can…
  • distinguish -teen/-ty numbers by ear (13 vs 30)
  • decode and build alphabet-code words at speed
  • quiz each other on Fun Facts numbers
  • find anything in the coursebook in under 30 sec

Pron. watch
-teen stress on the SECOND syllable (thirTEEN) vs -ty on the first (THIRty) · hotel — stress on the 2nd syllable · Wi-Fi /ˈwaɪfaɪ/
Concept checks (CCQs) · Assumptions
“ThirTEEN or THIRty — did I say /θɜːˈtiːn/?” → 13, stress at the end.
“G and J — which one's /dʒiː/?” → G; J = /dʒeɪ/. A classic mix-up.
“Do we write 67 as sixty seven?” → sixty-seven, with a hyphen.

Assumptions: Numbers and the alphabet are “known” from primary school — but under the timer and by ear, 13/30, G/J, E/I fall apart. International words are an easy win for pace. Gate is diagnosis, not humiliation: the ceiling must be REAL (spelling! numbers by ear! alphabet at speed!), results are private tallies, the reveal is “so THIS is our growth zone”.

Classroom management — signals
«Eyes up in 3-2-1» — count down calmly → all eyes on you at «1». No shouting over noise, ever.
Visible timer — timer on screen = we work; timer stops = pens/hands down. The timer is the boss, not you.
Volume dial — arm up/down = louder/quieter; «radio whisper» for rehearsals.
«Boards up!» / «Showdown!» — mini-whiteboard rounds: everyone commits, all boards up together — nobody hides, nobody shouts first.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, class answers with two and freezes — instant reset mid-game.
English-first — ideas in Russian are welcome, POINTS only for the English version — recast and repeat.
