🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name the twelve TV-and-film genres (action thriller, animation, chat show… the news) and glue sentences with relative clauses: who for people, which/that for things, where for places.
What is genuinely hard
Half the genres are two-word compounds with their own stress (SOAP opera, SCIENCE fiction, PERiod drama), genre starts with /ʒ/, and series refuses to change in the plural. In the grammar, Russian «что» covers people AND things — so the man what works here writes itself, and where gets replaced by which just when it mattered.
Typical error to expect
the jacket WHAT I bought · a man WHAT works here — Russian «что» pulled straight into English; the glue words are who for people, which/that for things, where for places — never what [high priority, A2/B1]
embarrassed — TWO r, TWO s · disappointed — one s, two p · exhausted — the h hides in the middle · soap opera is two words · science fiction — sci before ence; a misspelt word scores nothing, in a class test and later in the exam
Why the lesson is built this way
⚠️ The КТП's page note says «p.80–81», but the relative clauses it names sit on p.82 (top) — the plan follows the book, same move as blocks 5–6. A dense spread, honestly triaged: the twelve genres land on a class-drawn map (TB warmer), get confirmed by the recording and sorted film/TV/both; the extras text is the hook of the week (a real teenager who got into films through a website) and doubles as the grammar quarry — all five discovery examples come from it. Controlled practice runs Frankenstein (with its glorious monster-has-no-name fact) and the Corpus set with the that-tick. The productive stage grows the TB warmer into a riddle factory — nobody can build a riddle without building a relative clause — and Quiz-Quiz-Trade closes the loop with genre definitions. Detail questions 3 and 5 of the reading go to fast finishers; the lesson keeps its pace.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“A kid from Texas googled a casting website — and ended up in a real film. Today: the twelve kinds of film and show English talks about, the true story of how extras get hired, and the three glue words that let you define ANYTHING — a director is the person WHO is in charge; a studio is the place WHERE films are made. By the end you'll write riddles the class has to crack.”

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 definitions + ex.5 examples + ex.6 fan lines

  1. ex.4 — five definitions to complete, then pairs write one-liners for the other seven. WHAT WE DO: Kameron's story: gist → the word harvest → triaged detail → the warning box

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hook, books closed: A fourteen-year-old from Texas is IN a real film. He isn't famous. He isn't rich. How did he do it? Ten seconds of theories, then books open.
  2. Gist race, 3 minutes — ex.2: find the three ways in. Key: gist — Kameron's three ways in: join a casting website · contact your local film board · take acting classes. Pre-teach on the way: an extra (= a background actor) · volunteer (works free) · film board (a local organisation supporting film-making) · hire.
  3. ex.3, pairs, 3 minutes — the six highlighted words. Key: highlighted words: 1 warning (tells you something bad may happen) · 2 appear (perform in a film) · 3 hire (begin to employ) · 4 in charge (having control) · 5 part (role) · 6 support (help).
  4. ex.4 triaged: items 1, 2, 4, 6, 7 — solo first, then Kagan RallyCoach; the partner must HEAR the line that decides. Key: detail (seven questions; run 1, 2, 4, 6, 7 in class, 3 and 5 to fast finishers): 1 he was an extra · 2 extras act in films but don't have a main part · 3 volunteers meet interesting people and help a local director · 4 a director is in charge of the filming; a producer is responsible for the team, including hiring actors · 5 film boards can tell you if directors need extras near you · 6 because producers look for students in drama schools · 7 because you often wait around for long periods.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 Frankenstein + ex.3 Corpus with that-ticks + ex.4 join

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.2 — the Frankenstein text, seven choices: solo, 4 minutes, then the TB format — sentences read aloud in turn, the class judges and says why. Key and the facts: the Frankenstein text: 1 that · 2 who · 3 where · 4 that · 5 which · 6 that · 7 that.   2. ex.3 — complete + tick where that also works. Kagan RallyCoach, one pen. Key: Corpus: complete + tick where that also works: 1 where · 2 who ✓that · 3 which ✓that · 4 who ✓that · 5 where.

🎯 Speaking out · the riddle factory
teams of 3–4
Why: The productive outcome: teams turn the TB warmer into their own riddles — a person who…, a place where…, a thing that… — and the class guesses. Nobody can build a riddle without building a relative clause.

WHAT WE DO: teams build riddles with the glue words; the class cracks them

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Model with the TB warmer riddles: TB grammar warmer — riddles with the glue words underlined: a man WHO was president of the USA 2009–2017 · a game THAT two teams of eleven play · a place WHERE you go to watch films; the class guesses, then groups write three riddles of their own.
  2. Teams write THREE riddles about the film world: one person (who), one thing (which/that), one place (where) — a person who shouts Action!, a place where you buy popcorn… Kagan shape: the team agrees before anything is written; what is banned as glue and it is said out loud.
  3. Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Team two, number threes — your who-riddle! The class guesses; the guess must be a full noun answer.
  4. Class vote: the cleverest riddle of the day — its authors take a bow.
  5. You walk with the log: what-slips and clause-order slips go down SILENTLY — they open lesson 5.
📺 On screen: the three model riddles in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: build riddles, perform them by number, crack the others'. Expect at least one team to define the teacher (a person who gives homework) — take the hit gracefully. Typical error: a place that we watch films — logged, not corrected; the clinic opens lesson 5

WB Unit 14 (p.56–59)

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«A director is the person who is in charge» — can I say WHICH? → no: a person needs who (or that).
«The place where producers look» — can I swap where for that? → no: where keeps places, that only covers who/which.
«The jacket WHAT I bought» — does English glue with what? → never: Russian «что» maps onto which or that, not what.

what appears as the glue word (the man what works…)Named early, hunted all lesson: the mixed-ability board sentences include it, the CCQs include it, and the riddle stage bans it out loud. Russian «что» is the source — say so; naming the enemy helps.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
