🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use seven fixed phrases with on (on board, on display, on foot, on my own, on purpose, on sale, on time), complete a gapped-notes listening by writing one or two words exactly as heard, and answer personal questions with an extra sentence instead of a bare fact.
What is genuinely hard
Two very different difficulties. The listening is a discipline problem: the answer is one or two words, written unchanged, and candidates lose marks by improving what they heard or by writing three words where two are wanted. The speaking is a habit problem: a teenager answers «Do you like travelling?» with «Yes» and stops, and in both exams that is worth almost nothing.
Typical error to expect
«in time» for «on time», «by foot» for «on foot» — fixed phrases with no logic to reason from. They are learned as chunks or they are lost.
The one-word answer. «Yes, I do.» is grammatically perfect and scores badly. The fix is mechanical and it is the whole speaking half of this lesson: answer, then add one more sentence, every single time.
Why the lesson is built this way
The phrases are drilled as chunks and then hidden inside personal stories, so every one of them is used in a sentence somebody wants to hear. The listening is run exactly as the exam runs it — predict the TYPE of information for each gap first, then hear it twice — and the prediction stage is where the marks come from. The speaking half is built on a single observable behaviour: answer plus one extra sentence. It is modelled by a recording of two real candidates, counted in a fishbowl, and then practised in a rotating line so that everyone answers eight questions to four different partners.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“«Do you like travelling?» «Yes.» That answer is perfect English and it scores almost nothing. Today: seven phrases with «on», six gaps you fill with exactly the words you hear — and the one habit that turns a two-mark answer into a four-mark one.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a 90-second drill across all four modal squares, and the bridge into the on-phrases

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — how did you get to school today? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — yesterday's grid, out loud. Four prompts on the board, one per square: a rule at this school · something important but not a rule · something you don't have to do · advice for a new student.
  3. Kagan RallyRobin in pairs, 90 seconds: one sentence each, alternating, all four squares covered.
  4. Take four answers, one per square, and check the pattern rather than the content — have TO, need TO, needn't, should.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page seventy, the right-hand side. Seven more chunks — and this time the preposition is «on».
📺 On screen: yesterday's grid still on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: produce four modal sentences each, one per square

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and put up the one speaking rule that runs the lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ask the class one question in English and take the first answer, whatever it is: Do you like travelling by train? If it is one sentence, say nothing — write it on the board.
  2. Then write the same answer with one extra sentence added, and put the rule underneath: ANSWER + ONE MORE SENTENCE. It stays up all lesson.
  3. Say the shape in 30 seconds: seven phrases, an announcement on a plane where you write down what you hear, then eight exam questions with four partners.
  4. Set the bar: By the end, nobody in this room answers a question with one sentence.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: matched, drilled as chunks, then produced

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: the pairwork on SB p.136 — a real story for each phrase

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer on the seven chunks

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. You describe a situation and the class calls the phrase: He arrived at 9.00 for a 9.00 lesson (on time) · She broke it deliberately (on purpose) · They walked (on foot) · Half price this week (on sale).
  2. Eight situations at speed. Anyone who is wrong says the correct chunk twice before standing back up.
  3. Last thirty seconds, in pairs: Something you did on your own for the first time.
  4. Freeze and sit: Page seventy-one. You are on a plane, the captain is talking, and you have to write down six things.
📺 On screen: no technology — the situations are said aloud, so nothing can fail
👦 Students do: everybody produces eight chunks aloud and repairs their own errors

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 = B1 Listening Part 3: six gaps, one or two words, heard twice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Name the format difference first, because it is the point: Nothing to choose from. You write one or two words, or a number, exactly as you hear them.
  2. ex.2 BEFORE any audio, three minutes, in pairs: for each of the six gaps, what KIND of information is needed? A language, a time, a type of programme, a vegetarian dish, something you can buy on board, the name of a magazine.
  3. Write the six types on the board. This stage is where the marks come from and it deserves its three minutes.
  4. ex.3, track 46: play once, whole. Ninety seconds of pair comparison — types first, then answers.
  5. Play a second time, exactly as the exam does. Then check: 1 Spanish · 2 10.40 p.m. (or twenty to eleven) · 3 comedy · 4 tomato pasta · 5 sunglasses · 6 Explore.
  6. Two minutes on HOW answers are written: numbers may be figures or words; a plural stays plural; nothing is rephrased. Ask what happens if somebody wrote «sun glasses» — and answer it honestly.
📺 On screen: the six information types on the board before the recording plays. Backup: if the audio fails, read the announcement from the audioscript (TB p.296–297) twice, at announcement speed — slightly faster than you want to

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 = B1 Speaking Part 1: two real candidates, and what they add

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1, track 47: Luca and Kasia answer the examiner's questions. One job — WHICH of the eight questions do they answer? Answer: 2, 3, 5, 6, 7 and 8.

  3. ex.3: the Prepare to speak box. Write the four connectors on the board — For example… · Actually… · because… · but above all… — under the ANSWER + ONE MORE SENTENCE rule.
  4. Then the tense observation, which is what lifts a Part 1 answer: in two minutes of speech Luca and Kasia use the past simple, the past perfect, the present perfect, going to and a conditional. Say that number out loud: Five tenses in two minutes.
  5. Fishbowl, three minutes: two volunteers answer four questions in the middle while everybody else counts added sentences on their fingers. Debrief with the number only.

🎯 Speaking out · eight questions, four partners
two circles → class
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · eight questions, four partners

WHAT WE DO: ex.4: the full Part 1 routine, rotated, with a counting partner

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set up an Inside-Outside Circle: half the class in an inner circle facing out, half in an outer circle facing in. If the room will not allow circles, two facing lines work identically.
  2. The eight questions from ex.4 are on the board, numbered. Inner circle asks questions 1–4, outer circle answers; then swap; then rotate three places and repeat with questions 5–8.
  3. The rule, enforced by the listener and not by you: Answer plus one more sentence. If your partner stops after one, raise one finger and wait. No talking about it, just the finger.
  4. Four rotations, about four minutes each: everybody answers eight questions and asks eight, to four different partners.
  5. Sit down for the last four minutes and run a Timed Pair Share reflection: Which question was hardest to add a sentence to? Why?
  6. Take three answers to the floor and give the one piece of feedback that matters: name two students who added a REASON rather than just more words, and quote them.
  7. Close with the count: Sixteen exam answers each, in twenty-eight minutes. In the exam you get eight, and they take two.
📺 On screen: the eight questions numbered on the board so both circles can see them — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: answer eight exam questions and ask eight, to four different partners, with a partner counting the added sentence every time

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«on time» or «in time»? → on time means not late. In time means with enough time to spare. The exam wants on time.
The gap says one or two words. Can I write three? → no. And can I change the words I heard? → no. Write exactly what was said.
«Do you like flying?» — is «Yes» an answer? → it is an answer and it is not enough. Answer, then add one sentence.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The on-phrases are half-invented (by foot, in time)They go on the card strip in fixed form and are drilled as whole chunks. Any invented version is answered with the correct chunk said twice, not with an explanation.
In the listening, answers are rewritten or expandedThe rule goes on the board before the recording: One or two words. Exactly as you hear them. Do not improve them. Then in the check, ask what was actually said, not what was meant.
Speaking answers stay at one sentenceThe rule is countable and therefore enforceable: answer + one added sentence. In the final stage the partner literally counts, and anybody who stops at one gets a raised finger, not a comment.
Fast finishers in the vocabulary stageThe Teacher's Book task: write two gapped sentences on the on-phrases for the class to solve. Theirs get used.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
