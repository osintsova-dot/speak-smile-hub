🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Form and use the second conditional — If + past simple, would + infinitive — for unlikely or imaginary situations, including the fixed frame If I were you,…, and correct the two errors that cost marks: would in the if-half and a real-world half glued to an imaginary one.
What is genuinely hard
Two things fight the learner at once. Russian builds this with one particle (бы) in BOTH halves, so would migrates into the if-clause — and the English past simple here is not past at all, which feels wrong every single time.
Typical error to expect
«If I would have more time, I would travel» — would in the if-half is THE second-conditional error of Russian learners; the if-half stays past simple, always.
«It will be fun if you came with me» — half real, half imaginary. Both halves must live in the same world, and the book's Corpus item plants this trap on purpose.
Why the lesson is built this way
The grammar is not imported — it is harvested. Three second conditionals were read in yesterday's Takeover article and at least one was SAID by a student in the pitch stage; the lesson opens by quoting them, by name. Then the TB warmer (if you could do any job…), guided discovery from the book's four examples, matched halves where the form is chosen rather than produced, then free production, then the Corpus clinic on real learner errors. The productive half is personal: eight prompts run as a rotating interview and reported in the third person, then a short structured debate about their own school where the second conditional is the only way to say anything at all.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Yesterday one of you said: I would do it if I could. That sentence is the most useful shape in English for everything that isn't true — dream jobs, advice, arguments. Today you own it — and you use it to redesign this school.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, three real sentences from their own week, and the bridge into the grammar

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — their own sentence, quoted back. Write on the board, with the student's name beside it, what somebody actually said in yesterday's pitch stage: I would do it if I could.
  3. Beside it, two lines lifted from yesterday's article: I'd go mad if I were a headteacher permanently. and If everyone did a politician's job for a day, they'd be amazed.
  4. One question, Kagan RallyRobin: What do these three sentences have in common? Thirty seconds. Take two guesses, confirm nothing yet.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): name the destination: You've been using it since yesterday. Today it gets a shape, a name and a trap warning. Page one hundred and four.
📺 On screen: the three quoted sentences in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: recognise their own sentence on the board and hunt for the pattern. Expect «they're all about the future» — a good wrong answer to keep for minute 20

Why: The TB's own warmer: the target structure walks in as a conversation about dream jobs before anybody calls it grammar. Produce first, analyse second.

WHAT WE DO: the TB warmer: dream jobs answered before the form is taught

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. One question on the board and nothing else: If you could do any job in the world for one day, what would you choose?
  2. Pairs, 90 seconds each way. You listen, and you write four of their answers on the board EXACTLY as they said them — errors included, no names.
  3. Read the four aloud. Two of these are perfect. Two have the same small problem. By minute thirty you'll all see it. Leave them up.
  4. This is the lesson's own before-and-after evidence — the class will fix these four sentences themselves at minute 56.
📺 On screen: the four student sentences in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: answer the question fluently and see their own English on the board. Expect at least one would in an if-half — that is the whole point of collecting them

Grammar · second conditional discovery
pairs → class
Why: The rules are extracted from four sentences the class read yesterday in the Takeover text — familiar sentences make honest rules, and the CCQs settle the real question: is this true or imaginary?

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: four sentences from yesterday's text, three rules extracted

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 in pairs, four minutes: the four examples (two of them already on the board from the opening) and the rules box. Key: a If + past simple, would + infinitive · b 'd · c were (I were, he were — the old form survives in the if-half) · d either half can come first.
  2. CCQ round with the wall question, one example at a time (see the CCQ list): am I a politician? is this about the past? will this happen?
  3. The were rule gets its own thirty seconds: If I were you. Not was. Old English survived here because advice matters. Drill it chorally three times — it is the most useful chunk in the lesson.
  4. Grammar reference p.155 open for one minute: students tick the table they have just built themselves.
  5. Contractions drilled by ear: I'd, he'd, they'd, we'd — three fast rounds, because the full form is what makes students sound like a book.
📺 On screen: SB p.104 and Grammar reference p.155 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: extract, check, drill, tick the reference. Expect rule c (were) to be doubted — the If I were you drill settles it faster than any explanation

Grammar · matched halves
pairs
Why: Controlled practice with two jobs at once: match the halves AND choose the verb forms, so the shape of the sentence is felt before the forms are produced from scratch.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2: match six halves AND choose the right verb forms

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Frame the double task honestly: Two jobs at once. Find the other half, then choose the form. The meaning tells you which half; the line on the board tells you which form.
  2. Pairs, six minutes, Kagan RallyCoach, one pen. Key: 1 c wouldn't earn, became · 2 e 'd get, became · 3 a Would you be worried, was · 4 f were, would start · 5 d wanted, would I have to study · 6 b worked, 'd get.
  3. Whole-class check where every answer is read as a COMPLETE sentence, not as a letter — the point is to hear the shape six times.
  4. Item 5 gets a comment: If I wanted to be a vet, what would I have to study? A question in the second half — the shape survives.
📺 On screen: SB p.104 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: match, choose, read whole sentences aloud. Expect items 1 and 2 (wouldn't earn / 'd get, both with became) to be swapped — the meaning of the money decides

Why: Free-standing production of both halves, contractions included — the stage where 'd sleep and didn't waste stop being decoration and start being the form.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: both halves produced from scratch, contractions included

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Solo first, five minutes, no talking. Key: 1 'd sleep, did · 2 failed, would…say · 3 didn't take, wouldn't have · 4 'd finish, didn't waste · 5 could, 'd choose · 6 Would…be, didn't give — contractions wherever they fit.
  2. Then Kagan Showdown for the check: you read the item number, everyone writes the two verb forms on a mini-whiteboard, three-two-one — show! All boards up at once.
  3. One rule enforced on every answer: contractions where they fit. Not «I would sleep». «I'd sleep». Say it again.
  4. Item 5 (could) gets its own note: If I could live anywhere — could replaces the past simple, and the shape is untouched.
📺 On screen: answers on mini-whiteboards — the boards are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: produce both halves, then show simultaneously. Nobody hides, and you see the whole class's forms at the same moment

48–58′
Corpus clinic
pairs → class
Why: Real learner errors — the exact ones Russian students make — get fixed by the class, not by the teacher. Finding someone else's would in the if-half is the fastest way to stop writing your own.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4: six real learner errors, plus the four sentences from the warmer

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Frame it honestly: Six sentences written by real students in real exams. Every one has exactly one problem. Find it, fix it, name it.
  2. Pairs, five minutes. Key: 1 I'D get a job · 2 if you HAD the chance · 3 you WOULD really like her · 4 It WOULD be fun · 5 if I FOUND my job stressful · 6 If I SAW them again.
  3. Then the moment the lesson was built for: point at the four student sentences from minute 8. Now fix these. They're yours. The class corrects its own English from thirty minutes ago.
  4. Only NOW the first-conditional contrast, one pair of sentences on the board: If it rains, I'll stay in — it might rain. If it snowed in July, I'd be amazed — it won't. Probability, not tense.
  5. The two red rules go up under the chalk line: no WOULD on the left · both halves in the same world.
📺 On screen: SB p.104 plus the warmer sentences still in chalk — paper and board are the media, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: hunt, fix, then fix their own. Expect a small cheer when they recognise their own sentence — that recognition is worth more than the correction

🎯 Speaking out · the dream-job carousel
pairs, rotating
Why: The productive outcome: eight personalised second-conditional prompts run as a rotating interview, then reported in the third person. Nobody can take a turn without producing the form.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5–6: eight personal prompts, interviewed and then reported

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two minutes of silent thinking on the eight prompts of ex.5 (any job for a day · €100,000 for the school · never bored again · in charge of my school · my parents wouldn't be pleased if… · coach of any team · prime minister). Notes only, no full sentences.
  2. Kagan Timed Pair Share in three rotations, two minutes each way: A asks any two prompts, B answers in FULL second conditionals, then swap and rotate to a new partner.
  3. ex.6 — the report, in the third person: If Luca could do any job for a day, he'd be a photographer. Six or seven reports, and the class listens for the form, not the content.
  4. You circulate with the log sheet. Errors are written, not spoken — they open lesson 5.
📺 On screen: the eight prompts stay in the book, the reporting frame goes up in chalk — paper and board are the media, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: think, interview, rotate, report. Expect the €100,000 prompt to produce the longest answers — money always does

72–84′
If I were in charge
teams of 3–4
Why: A short structured debate on the class's own world (school rules, timetable, money) where the second conditional is the only way to say anything at all — grammar used for an argument, not for an exercise.

WHAT WE DO: a short structured debate about their own school

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Teams of three or four. One task: If you were in charge of this school, what three things would you change? And what would happen if you did?
  2. Five minutes to build, with a rule that forces the structure: Every proposal needs TWO sentences — what you'd change, and what would happen.
  3. Kagan Numbered Heads Together: you call a number, that team member presents. Each team gets one challenge question from the room, which must also be a second conditional: But what would happen if nobody…?
  4. Class vote on the best proposal. One line of honesty at the end: Three of those would actually work. I'll take them to the staff room.
📺 On screen: the two-sentence rule in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: propose, defend, challenge, vote. Expect lessons starting at ten o'clock — the book's own example, and it always arrives independently

WB Unit 18 (p.72–75)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can build and use second conditional sentences in both orders, with contractions, and can give advice with If I were you,…
  • Subsidiary. Reactivating the jobs set inside the new structure; justifying real-or-imaginary choices aloud; reporting a partner's answers in the third person.
  • Personal. Open with 2 minutes of delayed feedback from lesson 3's log. Keep ONE question on the wall all lesson: IS THIS TRUE, OR IMAGINARY? Do not compare with the first conditional for the first thirty minutes — the contrast helps only after the new form is stable.
By the end, students can…
  • build both halves: If I had…, I would…
  • keep would OUT of the if-half
  • use If I were you to give advice
  • say whether a sentence is about a real future or an imaginary one

Target language
Lexis

Structure
If everyone did a politician's job for a day, they'd be amazed. · I would do this job every day if I could. · I'd go mad if I were a headteacher permanently. · If you could do any job for a day, what would it be?
Form
If + past simple, would ('d) + infinitive; either half can come first (comma only when If comes first); were is possible for all persons in the if-half; could and might can replace would in the result half.
Pron. watch
the contraction is the sound of the tense: I'd /aɪd/, they'd /ðeɪd/, we'd /wiːd/ — students who say I WOULD every time sound like a textbook · wouldn't /ˈwʊdnt/ keeps a silent l
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«If I were a politician…» — am I a politician? → no, and I probably never will be. That is exactly why it is not will.
«If I had more time, I'd read more» — is this about the past? → no, it is about NOW. The past simple here means imaginary, not yesterday.
«If I were you, I'd talk to your parents» — whose problem is it? → theirs. This frame is how English gives advice without giving orders.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«If I would have a car…» — would in the if-halfThe physical rule beats the explanation: would lives on the RIGHT of the board, and there is a chalk line down the middle. Point at the line, do not speak. Students self-correct within three attempts.
Students hear the past simple as a real pastThe wall question answers it every time: IS THIS TRUE, OR IMAGINARY? Ask it, wait, let them answer, then move on. Ten seconds each time, twenty times in the lesson.
Interference from the first conditional (Unit 16)Deliberately not raised until minute 48, then settled in one sentence pair on the board: If it rains, I'll stay in (it might rain). If it snowed in July, I'd be amazed (it won't). Probability decides, not tense.
Fast finishers in the controlled stagesThey rewrite two of their answers with could or might instead of would and explain the difference in meaning to a partner.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
