🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Build the opposites of twelve adjectives with un-, in- and im- and write a real online profile (~80 words) using the Prepare to write phrases and the weak/strong adverbs (quite, fairly, a bit / very, really).
What is genuinely hard
Not the writing itself — the choice of prefix: Russian has one negative prefix where English has three, so unpossible and uncorrect are produced by honest analogy. And in writing, the blank page: without a plan and a phrase bank, weak writers freeze.
Typical error to expect
unpossible · impatient→unpatient · incorrect→uncorrect — the wrong negative prefix picked by analogy [A2/B1]
becouse · tomorow · alot · wich · beatiful · intresting — the most frequent misspellings; a misspelt word scores nothing, in a class test and later in the exam
Why the lesson is built this way
The lesson is built backwards from the writing at 56′: the profile needs the prefixed adjectives (I can be a bit impatient), the model texts and the phrase bank, so those three arrive in exactly that order and each is used the minute it is met. The two model profiles are read twice on purpose — once as readers (who is most like you?), once as writers (which verbs? which adverbs?): students steal better from a text they have enjoyed. Planning gets its own stage because four bullet points are what stop the blank-page freeze. Peer review comes before polishing so the second draft has a reason to exist.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“One prefix changes your whole character. Patient — impatient. Lucky — unlucky. Today you learn which of the three prefixes each word takes, and then you write the text every social network starts with: your own profile. Real words, real you, eighty words.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a clinic on lesson 1's three errors, and the bridge into the lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. A clinic on the correction sheet: three sentences on the board with no names attached — I'm owning two phones. · She don't talk much. · I'm not understanding this exercise. Thirty seconds — fix all three on your board.
  3. Check together; the student who put a hand up first explains WHY.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Yesterday you described people. Today you flip every adjective — and then the internet meets YOU: you write your profile.
📺 On screen: the three sentences written on the board before the lesson — the board is the medium, no backup needed
👦 Students do: correct them on mini-whiteboards. Expect everybody to fix the third (yesterday's rule is fresh), two thirds to fix the second. Typical error: they fix the tense but forget the -s

8–20′
Vocabulary · prefixes
pairs → class
Why: Twelve opposites for free: the base adjectives are known, so the whole cost is the choice of prefix — and that choice is exactly what the error data says goes wrong.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.12: twelve opposites sorted into the three columns

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 in pairs: write the opposite of each adjective in the right column, 5 minutes. Key: 1 unkind · 2 unfriendly · 3 impatient · 4 inexpensive · 5 unknown · 6 impolite · 7 invisible · 8 unhealthy · 9 unwell · 10 unfair · 11 unlucky · 12 incorrect.
  2. Point at the pattern only AFTER the columns are full: un- is the default; im- before p (impatient, impolite, impossible); in- for correct, expensive, visible, dependent — meet them as words, not as a rule to memorise.
  3. ex.2 — respond with the right word. Key: 1 impatient · 2 unwell · 3 visible · 4 unfair · 5 lucky · 6 incorrect. Watch item 3 (visible) and 5 (lucky): the answer is the POSITIVE adjective — reading the context beats pattern-matching.
  4. Drill the stress: unˈfriendly, imˈpatient, inˈvisible — the prefix never takes the stress.
📺 On screen: the twelve base adjectives on the screen while pairs sort (Backup: the words are in the book box — sort straight from the page into the chalk columns)
👦 Students do: sort the twelve words, spot the im-before-p pattern themselves, respond to contexts. Expect unwell and inexpensive to be the two surprises. Typical error: unpolite / unpossible — the columns fix it visually
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare4_Unit1_Vocabulary2_Negative_Prefixes_A5
A5 glue-in sheet — PREFIX POWER: un- · in- · im-: eight adjectives assembled from prefix + root on the sheet (im- + patient/polite/possible · in- + correct/dependent · un- + fair/healthy/tidy), each with its own model sentence (I'm impatient when I have to wait. · It's impolite to interrupt. · That answer is incorrect. · Too much sugar is unhealthy.), under the STRATEGY NOTE «Learn the whole adjective as a chunk»
  • Glue the sheet in — the prefix is not a rule, it is part of the word.
  • Colour the three prefix families and say each word out loud.
  • Cover the adjectives and rebuild them from the model sentences.
  • Write three sentences of your own with three different prefixes.

20–26′
Prefix rally
teams
Why: Retrieval under time pressure straight after input: the fastest way to find out which prefixes stuck and which are still being guessed by analogy (unpossible, uncorrect).

WHAT WE DO: a two-minute retrieval race on the fresh prefixes, then personalisation

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan RallyRobin in pairs: A says a base adjective, B flips it — kind → unkind! possible → impossible! — then swap. 90 seconds, count your pairs.
  2. Then Kagan Showdown, spelling round: you say the opposite meaning («not correct»), teams WRITE the word on the mini-whiteboard. Five words; the doubled letters and the prefix both count.
  3. Personal bridge to the writing: Which ONE of these words is a little bit true about you? Keep it — you'll need it in your profile today.
📺 On screen: nothing to display — voice and mini-whiteboards are the media, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: flip adjectives at speed, then write five under spelling pressure. Expect the oral round to be loud and near-perfect and the written round to drop — that gap is the lesson's point made visible

Reading · two profiles
solo → pairs
Why: The model text read as a reader first (who is most like you?), then mined as a writer: verbs, adverbs, phrases. Students steal from a text they have already enjoyed.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.13 ex.1: Snowy and Vogue — first as readers, then as writers

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Gist first: Read the two profiles. Which person is most like you — Tom or Flic? One reason. 3 minutes, then a quick hands vote.
  2. Profiles in short: Two online profiles: Snowy (Tom, 15 — a British boy living in the USA, Carson High School, Boston; music, guitar, starting to write his own songs) and Vogue (Felicity/Flic, 16, Australia — 1970s–80s fashion, learning to play the drums, fairly confident, a bit careless, «I can sometimes be impatient. I'm trying to change!»).
  3. Second pass — as writers. ex.3: the underlined verbs — which form for likes and regular things, which for right now? Key: 1 present simple (likes, dislikes, regular things) · 2 present continuous (happening at the moment).
  4. ex.4: sort the highlighted adverbs into weaker / stronger. Key: weaker: quite, fairly, a bit · stronger: very, really.
  5. Kagan Think-Pair-Share on the closing line: Flic writes: I can sometimes be impatient. Why does she ADMIT it? Does it make the profile better or worse? (better — honesty is what makes a profile sound human — and that sentence pattern is free to steal).
📺 On screen: the two profiles on the screen for the second, language pass (Backup: everyone reads from the book; write the two adverb columns on the board by hand)
👦 Students do: read for themselves first, then mine the text for verbs and adverbs. Expect the vote to split. Typical error: quite filed as «strong» — anchor the scale on the board: a bit → quite/fairly → very/really

Move break
standing
Why: A stirrer that also forces one English sentence. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: 90 seconds of movement that recycles the scale

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. I say a sentence — you show the strength: hands low = a bit, hands middle = quite, hands high = really. 'I'm A BIT tired.' 'I'm REALLY hungry.' 'I'm QUITE good at English.' Last one: show YOUR true answer to 'I'm … confident in English' — and say the sentence.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: show the adverb strength with their hands and say one true sentence. Expect honest variety on the last one — glance around: it tells you who needs support in the writing stage

Prepare to write · the toolbox
class → pairs
Why: The phrase bank and the weak/strong adverbs are collected BEFORE planning, so when students write, the language is on the desk and the working memory is free for ideas.

WHAT WE DO: the phrase bank (ex.2) + ex.5: five true sentences about yourself

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Walk the box: Prepare to write: introduce yourself (I'm…, My name's…, I'm from…) · what kind of person you are (I think I'm very/quite…, My friends say I'm…, I can sometimes be…) · hobbies (I'm interested in…, I'm really into…, My hobbies are…) · what you're learning at the moment (At the moment I'm…, Right now I'm…).
  2. Tick which phrases Tom and Flic actually used — the box is stolen FROM the profiles, not invented.
  3. ex.5 in pairs, out loud first, then on paper: I'm very… · I'm fairly… · Sometimes I'm quite… · My friends say I'm… · I think I can be a bit… — five TRUE sentences about yourself.
  4. Kagan RallyRobin: partners alternate sentences; the listener challenges anything that doesn't sound true — Really? Prove it!
📺 On screen: the Prepare to write box enlarged on the screen (Backup: the box is on p.13 of every book — students tick the phrases right on the page)
👦 Students do: collect phrases, then say and write five true graded sentences. Expect the «My friends say» frame to produce the best sentences. Typical error: a bit friendly — a bit wants a negative quality, catch it now, not in the drafts

48–56′
Planning · notes
solo
Why: Four bullet points stop the blank-page freeze: nobody starts writing without knowing what goes in each paragraph. Planning is where weak writers are made average.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6: four bullets that stop the blank page

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Four bullets on the board, students copy and fill for THEMSELVES, notes not sentences: name + facts · what I'm like · hobbies · learning at the moment. 5 minutes, silent.
  2. One rule: every bullet gets at least two words of notes — no empty bullets into the writing stage.
  3. Circulate; where a bullet is empty, ask the question out loud (What ARE you learning right now? Anything — a game, a trick, a language.) — the answer is the note.
  4. Silent mode, palm down: this is thinking time, questions afterwards.
📺 On screen: the four bullets in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: plan in notes, alone and in silence. Expect «learning at the moment» to be the empty bullet — the circulating question fills it. Typical problem: full sentences instead of notes — point at the model: two words per line

56–72′
🎯 Writing out · my online profile
solo, silent mode
Why: The productive outcome in writing: a real profile, built on a model, a plan and a phrase bank. Started AND finished in class, so nobody takes an empty page home.

WHAT WE DO: the profile itself: ~80 words, plan + toolbox + the bar from 5′

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Sixteen minutes. About eighty words. Your plan is your map; the box on p.13 is your phrase bank. Start with 'I'm … and I'm from …' if you need a push.
  2. Visible timer on. One lap of the room per draft: comments in pencil, only on checklist points, never on ideas.
  3. Model answer for YOUR reference (not shown yet): TB model answer: «I'm Victor and I'm from Manchester in England. I love sport and I'm really into football. I play every day with my friends and I'm in the school team, too. I'm quite confident and I like learning new things. At the moment I'm learning to play ice hockey. It's fairly difficult, but I know I can do it!»
  4. Fast finishers: the TB task — four extra sentences with prefix adjectives, read to a neighbour without the adjective, neighbour guesses.
  5. Two minutes before the end: Full stop. Now read your own text once, only for spelling.
📺 On screen: a visible timer on the screen (Backup: the wall clock and two chalk marks — start and stop time — on the corner of the board)
👦 Students do: write alone against the clock. Expect 60–90 words from most; the checklist extension absorbs the fast ones. Typical error: one giant paragraph — the pencil comment is one word: «paragraphs?»

72–84′
Peer review + polish
pairs
Why: A checklist swap turns every student into an editor: they catch each other's dropped -s and misspelt adverbs faster than they catch their own — and both sides learn.

WHAT WE DO: checklist swap, then five minutes to make the second draft better than the first

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap notebooks. The bar from 5′ becomes the checklist: about 80 words? two paragraphs? both tenses? two graded adverbs? one un-/in-/im- word? spelling? Tick or mark — in pencil, kindly.
  2. Kagan RallyCoach in role: the reviewer reads ONE favourite sentence aloud to the writer, then names ONE thing to fix.
  3. Five minutes back with your own text: fix what your editor marked.
  4. Now show the TB model on the screen: Compare — not to copy, to check: does Victor do anything you forgot?
📺 On screen: the model profile on the screen only AFTER the swap (Backup: read the model aloud twice — it is six sentences, students catch what they need by ear)
👦 Students do: edit a partner's text against the checklist, then improve their own. Expect the checklist to catch missing paragraphs and adverbs; spelling still slips — that's what the trainer link and the workbook are for

WB Unit 1 (p.4–7)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can form opposites with un-/in-/im- and have written and polished their own online profile (~80 words) with paragraphs, both present tenses and graded adverbs.
  • Subsidiary. Reading two model profiles for gist and for language; grading adjectives with quite / fairly / a bit / very / really; peer editing against a checklist.
  • Personal. Open with the correction sheet from lesson 1 (three anonymous sentences). During the writing stage resist the urge to hover — one lap of the room per draft, comments in pencil, only on checklist points.
By the end, students can…
  • form the opposite of twelve adjectives with the right prefix
  • grade an adjective up and down (a bit careless → really careless)
  • plan a profile in four bullet points
  • write an 80-word online profile and improve it after peer review

Form

Pron. watch

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«He's a bit friendly» — why does it sound odd? → a bit goes with NEGATIVE qualities: a bit careless, a bit impatient.
«Which is stronger — I'm quite confident or I'm really confident?» → really; quite, fairly, a bit make it weaker.
«impossible or unpossible?» → im- before p: impossible, impatient, impolite.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
unpossible / uncorrect produced by analogyDon't lecture — group the words on the board in three columns as they come up, and let the im-before-p pattern be noticed by the class. Then drill the three in- words as a chant: incorrect, inexpensive, invisible.
The blank page: “I don't know what to write”Point at the plan: four bullets are already on their sheet, and every bullet has a ready phrase in the Prepare to write box. The first sentence is free: I'm … and I'm from …
Fast writers finish in five minutes with 40 wordsThe checklist is the extension: paragraphs? both tenses? two graded adverbs? one prefixed adjective? Then the TB fast-finisher: four extra sentences, read out without the adjective — the class guesses it.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
