🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use the present perfect with just, yet and already — and put each of them in the right place in the sentence.
What is genuinely hard
Not the meaning: just, yet and already translate cleanly. It is the POSITION. Just and already go inside the verb — after have, before the participle — and yet goes at the very end, and Russian word order gives no help with either. Add to that yet being impossible in a positive statement.
Typical error to expect
I have already not finished · I have finished already it — the word order: just and already go after have and before the main verb, yet goes at the end [A2/B1]
I have yet done it — yet used in a positive statement, where English needs already [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The second lesson of a three-lesson unit is the grammar lesson, and this one opens with three cans of soft drink on the teacher's table because just, already and not yet are three physical states of the same can. Ninety seconds of demonstration replaces ten minutes of explanation, and the rules in ex.2 then complete themselves. After that the practice is deliberately in three separate blocks with different shapes: a counting task (seven bought, five not bought yet), a personal task about the last ten minutes, and error correction — because word order is invisible until it is wrong, and each shape catches a different half of the class. Pronunciation comes late and it is not decoration: ex.7 is a question-asking task and it dies without the rising tone, so the intonation block is placed immediately before it. The productive stage is the teacher's own day on the board, which is the one topic about which the class will genuinely want to ask questions.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Three small words, and the only hard thing is where they stand. Just and already go inside the verb. Yet goes at the very end and only in questions and negatives. I have three cans of drink here, and by the end of ninety seconds you will see why.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, the apostrophe spelled aloud and one recent action from everybody

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. Two questions on the board: Where do you buy medicine? (a chemist's) · Spell «butcher's». One student spells it aloud, letter by letter, apostrophe included.

  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): put the three cans on the table without a word, then: Page eighty-one. Three words today, and three cans.

Grammar · discovery · just, yet, already
table → solo
Why: Three cans of drink on the teacher's table make the meaning visible in ninety seconds. The rules in ex.2 then complete themselves, which is the whole point of the demonstration.

WHAT WE DO: the three cans, then ex.1–2 p.81

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.158, and it is the best two minutes of the block for weaker students: bring three cans of soft drink — two unopened and one finished. Put them on your table. Open one and say I've just opened the can. They repeat, you write it up. Show the empty one: I've already finished this. Repeat, write it up. Open the last one, drink some: I've just opened the can but I haven't finished it yet. Then the half-empty one in your hand: «Have I finished it yet?» (no) → You haven't finished it yet. · «Have I already opened it?» (yes) · «Have I just drunk it?» (no). The rules in ex.2 then complete themselves.
  2. Only now open the book. ex.1 — which of the two shops is open now and which will open soon? Match a and b. Key: Shop and Try – a · Brown's Chemist's – b
  3. ex.2 — match the sentence halves to complete the rules. Key: 1 d · 2 b · 3 c · 4 b
  4. Point out the word order explicitly, once, and then only point at the board: already and just come after have and before the main verb; yet goes at the end of the sentence.
  5. Now go back to the mini-whiteboards from the opening: Your sentence from four minutes ago. Say it again with just. Three students, out loud.
  6. Say where the rule lives for homework: Grammar reference SB p.160, answer key TB p.266.
📺 On screen: the three cans on the teacher's table and the sentences going up on the board as they are said (Backup with no cans: any three objects will do — a full bottle, an empty one and a half-drunk one, or three pens with the lids on, off and half off. The demonstration is about states, not about drink)
👦 Students do: watch the cans, repeat three sentences, then complete the rules. Expect the cans to get a laugh and the rules to be filled in correctly by almost everybody — which is the demonstration working. Typical problem: somebody asks for a can

Grammar · practice 1 · the camping list
pairs → class
Why: A picture and a shopping list turn word order into a counting task: seven already bought, five not bought yet. The grammar arrives as a consequence of the counting.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.81: eight things for a camping trip, then what Carla has and hasn't bought

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. SB p.81 ex.3, and it works better with a time limit: in pairs, write eight things you need for a camping trip (they can look back at the kit list on SB p.44 from block 4), then open the book and compare with Carla's picture and shopping list. «How many things has Carla already bought?» (seven) · «How many hasn't she bought yet?» (five). Then they make the sentences.
  2. Do the counting out loud before any sentence is made — seven already, five not yet — because the counting is what makes the grammar inevitable. Key: She's already bought socks, biscuits, a scarf, boots, a water bottle, gloves and a hat. She hasn't bought a blanket, a toothbrush, fruit or a pillow yet. (Seven already bought, five not bought yet.)
  3. Then the sentences, in pairs, 5 minutes. Every sentence must use already or yet, and the word must be in the right place.
  4. Kagan RallyCoach for the check: A says a sentence, B coaches on the word order only, then swap.
  5. One extra question that recycles yesterday: Which shops could she buy the other five things in? Fast, round the room.
📺 On screen: the picture and the shopping list on screen side by side (Backup: both are printed on SB p.81 — the screen only saves them looking down, and the counting works identically from the paper book)
👦 Students do: list eight things, count, then build sentences. Expect the counting to be argued about, which is fine. Typical error: She hasn't yet bought a blanket — the board line catches it without a word

34–44′
Grammar · practice 2 · six things you've just done
solo → pairs
Why: Personal, immediate and impossible to fake. Just means a few seconds ago, so the sentences have to be about the last ten minutes.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 p.81: six things you have just done, compared and reported

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Give your own two examples first, as TB p.158 does: I've just asked Sandra a question. I've just remembered something important.
  2. Six things you have just done. Just means a few seconds or a few minutes ago — so these are all from this lesson or this morning. 4 minutes alone.
  3. In pairs, compare the six. 3 minutes, and the rule is that you have to react to your partner's, not just read yours.
  4. Feedback in the third person, which is where has has to appear: Pedro has just learned a new word. Six students, one sentence each.
  5. Kagan Think-Pair-Share on one question to close: What is the most boring thing you have just done?
📺 On screen: your two model sentences on the board (Backup: none needed — this stage is notebooks, pairs and chalk)
👦 Students do: write six, compare, report in the third person. Expect the reporting to produce have where has is needed. Typical error: sentences about last week — just means minutes, and it is worth one reminder

WHAT WE DO: a two-minute stirrer built out of the grammar itself

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The Teacher's Book cooler, used here as the move break because it is a standing activity: TB p.158, «sit down if…», and it is the grammar of the lesson standing up: they all stand, and you say sentences in the present perfect with yet and already. Sit down if … you've sent a text today · you've drunk some coffee · your mum has phoned you · you've already watched a video · your best friend has said «Hello» to you · you haven't been to a shop yet. The last person standing wins.
  2. Six sentences, fast, and the last person standing wins. It takes exactly two minutes if you do not let them argue.

👦 Students do: stand, sit when the sentence is true of them. Expect the last two standing to be the ones who have not been to a shop today, which is a good sentence to have them say out loud

Grammar · practice 3 · correcting
pairs → class
Why: Word order is invisible until it is wrong. The five sentences of ex.5 are exactly the five word-order mistakes this structure produces.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5 p.81: repair five sentences — this is where word order becomes visible

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Put the book's own board example up first: Max just has been to the shop. Cross out just has, write has just above it, and say nothing else.
  2. ex.5 — correct the five sentences, in pairs, 5 minutes. Key: 1 You've already borrowed my book for a week. · 2 I've just bought a new smartphone but I haven't put music on it yet. · 3 I've just seen a football match with my father. · 4 I've already bought something to eat. · 5 If you haven't found them yet, I think they are in your bag.
  3. Kagan Showdown on the five: everybody writes, everybody reveals at Boards up!
  4. Then four more of your own on the board, and these are the ones the block test will use: I have yet done my homework · She has bought already the tickets · Have you already finish? · We haven't yet arrived. Thirty seconds each.
  5. Kagan Numbered Heads Together for the last one: call a number, that student repairs it and says which rule was broken.
📺 On screen: the five extra sentences on screen one at a time (Backup: dictate them — four short sentences takes thirty seconds and the mini-whiteboards do the rest)
👦 Students do: correct five, reveal, then repair four more. Expect item 2 (the two-clause one) to be the hardest, because it needs both just and yet in one sentence. Typical error: they fix the tense and leave the word order

58–68′
Pronunciation · intonation
class → pairs
Why: A question said with a falling tone stops being a question. Eight minutes here is worth more than any amount of politeness advice — and it is a marked skill in the speaking exam.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6 p.81 (track 107): questions rise, statements fall

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. SB p.81 ex.6 (track 107): yes/no questions RISE at the end, statements FALL. It is put here on purpose — ex.7 is a question-asking task and it dies without this.
  2. Play track 107, then let them repeat. Elicit the rule rather than giving it: What happens at the end of a question? And at the end of a statement?
  3. TB p.158: if they cannot hear the difference, HUM the sentences instead of saying them. Without words the tune is obvious, and they can hum it back before they try to say it.
  4. In pairs, 3 minutes: one student says a sentence either as a question or as a statement, WITHOUT changing the words, and the partner says which it was. You've already eaten. ↗ or ↘
  5. One sentence of why it matters, said once and not laboured: This is not decoration. Speaking task 1 of the Russian exam is reading a text aloud, and a question read with a falling tone costs a mark there.
📺 On screen: track 107 in the whiteboard app (Backup with no sound: say and hum the sentences yourself — for intonation a live voice is genuinely better than a recording, because you can exaggerate and repeat on demand. The sentences are printed on SB p.81)
👦 Students do: listen, hum, repeat, then test each other. Expect the humming to be the moment it clicks for the three who could not hear it. Typical error: the rise put in the middle of the sentence instead of at the end

68–84′
🎯 Speaking out · have you … yet?
class → pairs
Why: The productive outcome: real questions about a real day, asked with a rising tone and answered with a falling one. The teacher's own list goes on the board first, so the model is heard before it is used.

WHAT WE DO: ex.7 p.81: the teacher's own day on the board, then their own

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. SB p.81 ex.7, books closed: write a short list of your own day on the board — what you have done and what you have not done yet — and let them ask you questions with yet and already: Have you prepared your lessons for tomorrow yet? Then they make their own lists and ask each other. Monitor for two things only: the rising tone on the question and the falling one on the answer.
  2. Write your list first — six lines, three done and three not — and answer honestly. 5 minutes of the class asking you questions. This is the stage they enjoy most in the whole unit and it is worth the time.
  3. Then they write their own lists, 3 minutes, and ask each other in pairs. 5 minutes.
  4. Monitor two things and nothing else: the rising tone on the question and the word order in the answer. Log everything else silently.
  5. Feedback in the third person, 2 minutes: Anya has already finished her maths but she hasn't packed her bag yet.
  6. Close the loop with three logged errors on the board, no names, thirty seconds to repair.
📺 On screen: your own six-line list written on the board (Backup: none needed — the list is handwritten by you and that is the whole material)
👦 Students do: interrogate the teacher, then each other. Expect the questions to get sharper once they realise you are answering truthfully. Typical error: Have you already prepare? — the participle disappears under the excitement of asking

WB Unit 14 (p.56–59)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can use just, yet and already with the present perfect and place each of them correctly in the sentence.
  • Subsidiary. Counting what has and has not been done from a picture and a list (ex.3); the intonation of questions and statements (ex.6, track 107); correcting five word-order mistakes (ex.5).

  • say what you have just done, in the last few minutes
  • say what you have already done and what you haven't done yet
  • put just, already and yet in the right place in a sentence
  • ask a yes/no question with a rising tone

Structure
The shop has just opened. · I've already bought something to eat. · I haven't put music on it yet. · Have you finished it yet?
Form
have / has + just / already + past participle · … + past participle + yet. Yet only in questions and negatives.
Pron. watch
yes/no questions RISE at the end, statements FALL. Have you finished yet? ↗ · I've already finished. ↘
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I've just opened the can.» How long ago? → a few seconds. Just means a very short time ago.
«Have I finished it yet?» → no — you haven't finished it yet. Yet means it is still not done, and it goes at the end.
«I have yet done my homework» — what's wrong? → yet is for questions and negatives. In a positive sentence: I have already done my homework.
«Max just has been to the shop» — correct it. → Max has just been. Just goes after have, not before it.

Already and yet are half known from Give Me Five and have never been placed correctly. The twelve shops of yesterday get recycled in ex.3.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Max just has been to the shopThis is the classic, and the Teacher's Book puts it on the board as its own example. Write it up, cross out just has, write has just, and leave it there for the whole lesson.
I have yet done my homeworkOne line on the board: yet = ? and not. Point at it. It is the fastest possible statement of the rule and it does not need a sentence.
The questions in the last stage come out flatDo not talk about intonation again. Hum the question, then hum the statement, and let them hum it back. Thirty seconds and it comes right.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
