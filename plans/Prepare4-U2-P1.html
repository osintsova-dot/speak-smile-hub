🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name thirteen life events in order (be born → start school → … → retire) and compare lives correctly: -er/-est, more/most, the irregulars, and not as… as — spelled right.
What is genuinely hard
The events are collocations, not words: GET a degree but GO to university, LEAVE home but MOVE home — Russian shuffles the verbs. And in the grammar the trap is not the concept (Prepare 3 met -er) but the system: when -er, when more, why never both, and what not as… as actually claims.
Typical error to expect
more better · more easier · the most happiest — two comparative markers glued onto one adjective [high priority, A2/B1]
not so tall like me · not as taller as — the as… as frame rebuilt with like or with a comparative inside [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
Vocabulary comes first because the reading and the grammar are both made of it. The life events arrive as a LINE (ordering task) rather than a list, then get used immediately in the Around-the-world quiz — meaning before form. The forum text does double duty: careful reading for detail AND the quarry the grammar table is mined from — all six missing forms hide in Simon's and Emily's paragraphs. not as… as is anchored on Simon's own line about teenagers' health. The productive stage at 66′ is a best-age discussion because ages are what comparatives are FOR: real opinions, real numbers, logged errors opening lesson 2.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“At what age does life actually start? Today you get thirteen phrases that cover a whole life — being born to retiring — and the grammar to argue about it: is eighteen the best age to leave home, or is thirty better? By the end you'll compare lives like you compare phones — properly.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts. The rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a hot round of block-1 opposites, and the bridge into the unit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — opposites hot round. Eight block-1 adjectives on the board: cheerful, confident, shy, polite, rude, careless, friendly, miserable. Teams fire the opposites back: Cheerful? — Miserable! Rude? — Polite! A point per correct opposite.

Why: Thirteen phrases arrive as a LINE, not a list: ordering them builds the concept (a life has a shape) while the phrases are still warm — and shows you which ones the class already owns.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: six events ↔ photos A–F; ex.2: order all thirteen on the life line

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Think-Pair-Share on the About you questions: When did you learn to walk and talk? How old were you when you learned to swim? What are the most important events in a person's life? Twenty seconds to think, tell your partner, two pairs report.
  2. ex.1 in pairs: match SIX of the thirteen phrases to photos A–F — 2 minutes. Key: A start school · B get a degree · C get a driving licence · D get married · E move home · F vote.
  3. ex.2: pairs put ALL thirteen events into a sensible order — warn them more than one order is possible. Build the life line on the board as pairs call events out. Key: a natural order (others possible): be born → start school → leave school → get a driving licence → vote → go to university → get a degree → leave home → get a job → get married → have children → retire; move home fits anywhere.
  4. Drill the sound traps by ear, three choral repetitions: degree /dɪˈɡriː/ · licence /ˈlaɪsəns/ · retire /rɪˈtaɪə/ · university /juːnɪˈvɜːsəti/ · than is weak: /ðən/ — better THAN /ˈbetəðən/ in one breath.
📺 On screen: the thirteen phrases are boxed in every book on p.20 — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed; the life line grows in chalk as pairs call events
👦 Students do: discuss, match, then argue the order (does university come before or after leaving home?). Expect get a degree and move home to be the new ones. Typical error: go to home — the verbs are the lesson

Reading · Simon vs Emily
solo → pairs
Why: Gist first, then detail, then the six extreme adjectives — the CELTA order. The forum text is also the quarry the grammar is mined from: every form in the p.22 table hides in it.

WHAT WE DO: the forum text: gist → six choices → the six extreme adjectives

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hook, books closed: Who has the easier life — you, or your parents when they were sixteen? Hands: you? them? Count, keep the score on the board corner.
  2. Gist race, 3 minutes, books open: Simon is 47, Emily is his daughter, 16. Who says teenage life is better NOW, who says it was better THEN? Key: better now — Simon (1) · better in the past — Emily (2). Pre-teach on the board: bullying · device · relaxed · exhausted.
  3. ex.2 in pairs — Kagan RallyCoach: partner A answers 1–3 pointing at the line in the text, B coaches; swap for 4–6. Key: 1 B · 2 B · 3 C · 4 A · 5 A · 6 B.
  4. ex.3: the six highlighted words to meanings, fast. Key: 1 unbelievable · 2 relaxed · 3 exhausted · 4 awful · 5 tiny · 6 huge. Drill them as a volume ladder: tiny (whisper) → huge (roar).
  5. Close with the hook vote again — did the text move anyone? Talking points if the pace allows: How was your parents' teenage life different from yours? · What problems can modern life bring for teenagers?
📺 On screen: track 14 is the recording of the forum text — optional (Backup: skip the audio entirely, the stage is built on silent reading)
👦 Students do: read for the verdict, then argue the choices from the text. Expect item 4 (why Simon lost friends) to need the line pointed at. Typical problem: exhausted read as excited — the volume ladder fixes both meaning and stress

40–52′
Grammar · discovery
pairs → class
Why: Guided discovery, not presentation: the forms are hunted down in a text the class has already read and argued about. Rules extracted from familiar sentences are rules they keep.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: the table mined from the article + ex.4: what not as… as claims

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Warmer, books closed: TB warmer: good – better – the best on the board, then life-event questions: Is it better to stay at home or leave home when you finish school? What's the best age to get married?
  2. ex.1 — the table with six holes, and the book says it: the answers are IN the article. Pairs hunt them down, 4 minutes. Key: 1 the greatest · 2 safer · 3 easier · 4 the most important · 5 better · 6 the worst — all six checkable in the p.21 article.
  3. ex.2 rules from the examples: than follows a COMPARATIVE adjective (a) · the goes before a SUPERLATIVE (b). Add the spelling row under the compare corner: hot → hotter (double), brave → braver (+r), happy → happier (y→i), new → newER only — w never doubles.
  4. Box the one law: ONE marker per adjective — -er OR more, never both.
  5. ex.4 — not as… as on Simon's own line. Key: not as… as = NOT the same (the paraphrase of Simon's line about teenagers' health). Draw two unequal bars: teens now (short) vs teens then (tall). Who wins? The PAST teenagers.
  6. Concept checks from the plan header — all three, cold-calling by name.
📺 On screen: the compare corner fills in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: hunt the forms in a text they already know, extract the rules, read the bars. Expect the greatest and the worst to be found fast, safer to hide (it is in Simon's roads line). Typical error: more easier — point at the box
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare4_Unit3_Grammar_Comparatives_Superlatives_A5
A5 grammar card — COMPARE IT!: three tools at once — SHORT ADJECTIVES (small → smaller than → the smallest · big → bigger → the biggest) · LONG ADJECTIVES (expensive → more expensive than → the most expensive · interesting) · IRREGULAR (good → better → the best · bad → worse → the worst) — plus NOT AS + ADJECTIVE + AS (A town isn't as busy as a city.) and a SPELLING CLUE (y → ier/iest · one vowel + one consonant doubles)
  • Glue the card in and colour the three routes in three colours.
  • Learn the two irregular chains by heart — they are the ones that fail.
  • Compare your town and another one: one comparative, one superlative.
  • Say the same idea twice: X is smaller than Y / Y isn't as small as X.
When: ~5 min at the start of the “Grammar · discovery” stagePrep: 1 × A5 per pupil. Scissors, glue sticks, pencils.

Why: Controlled practice with immediate peer correction — the Corpus error hunt makes them justify the choice, which is what transfers to speaking.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 IT'S A FACT + ex.6 Corpus error hunt + ex.5 not as… as about us

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 IT'S A FACT solo against the clock (3 minutes), then Kagan Showdown on mini-whiteboards: you call the item number, teams write JUST the form and show on three. Key: IT'S A FACT: 1 The oldest · 2 the largest · 3 heavier than · 4 the happiest, the best · 5 more expensive than · 6 The youngest · 7 cheaper than.
  2. ex.6 — Corpus error hunt in pairs: six real learner sentences, find and fix. Say WHY, not just the fix. Key: Corpus, correct the mistake: 1 a bigger house than · 2 more relaxed THAN · 3 the MOST popular drink · 4 quieter than · 5 My happiest (no most) · 6 not as TALL as. Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Sentence five — number twos!
  3. ex.5 — not as… as turned personal, pairs, out loud: ex.5 models (box: comfortable, hard, old, serious, untidy): 0 English isn't as hard as maths · then children/adults, you/your best friend, dad/mum, you/a relative — students' own true sentences
  4. Fast finishers (TB): take Simon's other adjectives from the reading (simple, exciting, safe, healthy…) and build their comparative and superlative forms.
📺 On screen: item numbers called aloud, answers on mini-whiteboards — voice and boards are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: commit in writing at speed, then justify the fixes. Expect ex.6 item 5 (My happiest, no most) to split the class — it is the double-marker law in disguise. Typical error: not as taller as

🎯 Speaking out · the best age
solo → pairs, then swap
Why: The productive outcome: comparatives, superlatives and life events in personal opinions about real ages, under time pressure. Errors are logged silently — correcting now would kill fluency.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6–7: I want to… sentences, then the best-age discussion

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two minutes solo — ex.6: six sentences with I want to… + a life event + an age: I want to leave home before I'm 25. I want to retire before I'm 40!
  2. Kagan Timed Pair Share, 2 minutes each way — ex.7 questions on the board: What's the best age to get married? Is university more important than a job? Do people leave school too early? Opinions need a because; comparatives are the currency.
  3. Swap partners once (left seats move one place) and repeat faster — steal the best argument you heard in round 1.
  4. You walk with a paper: log double markers and backwards not-as…-as SILENTLY — they open lesson 2.
  5. Close the loop: two students report the boldest plan they heard — She wants to get a degree before she's twenty. That's earlier than Charlie!
📺 On screen: the three discussion questions in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write six true wishes, then argue ages with comparatives. Expect retire-at-40 jokes — allow them, the grammar rides on them. Typical error: more better under speed — logged, not corrected

WB Unit 3 (p.12–15)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can name the thirteen life events with the right verbs and build comparatives, superlatives and not as… as — spelled correctly — in sentences about real lives.
  • Subsidiary. Reading the two-voice forum text for gist and detail (SB p.21); the six extreme adjectives (huge, tiny, awful…); guided discovery of the comparison system from the text (SB p.22).
  • Personal. Do not pre-teach the table — let them hunt the six forms in the article (ex.1 says to). During the best-age stage log comparison errors silently; delayed feedback opens lesson 2.
By the end, students can…
  • say the thirteen life events with the right verbs, in a sensible order
  • build -er/-est and more/most forms — and never both at once
  • use not as… as and say who «wins» the comparison
  • give an opinion about the best age for something, with a because

Structure
You can vote at eighteen. · Technology is the greatest change. · Roads were safer than they are today. · Teenagers aren't as healthy as they were. · I want to leave home before I'm 25.
Form
short adj → -er / the -est (hot→hotter, happy→happier); long adj → more / the most; irregular good–better–best, bad–worse–worst, far–further; not as… as = not the same, the first one is less.
Pron. watch
degree /dɪˈɡriː/ · licence /ˈlaɪsəns/ · retire /rɪˈtaɪə/ · university /juːnɪˈvɜːsəti/ · than is weak: /ðən/ — better THAN /ˈbetəðən/ in one breath
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Norway is happier than Spain» — how many countries am I comparing? → two: -er + than. «The happiest country in the world» — two or all? → all of them: the + -est.
«Teenagers aren't as healthy as they were» — the same or not the same? → not the same; and who is healthier? → the teenagers of the past.
«This exercise is more easier» — what's wrong? → one comparative marker per adjective: easier, full stop.

Assumptions: SB p.20–22 (top), syllabus lesson 9. Present tenses and past simple are solid after block 1 and its test. Comparatives existed in Prepare 3 but were never systematised with superlatives and not as… as. Of the thirteen life events the class usually owns three or four (be born, start school, get married as a chunk); the verb+noun pairings are effectively new.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Verbs shuffled inside the collocations («make a degree», «go to home»)Keep the life line on the board all lesson with the verbs written LARGE and the nouns small — point at it silently every time a verb slips.
«more better», «the most happiest» — double markingOne boxed rule on the board: ONE marker per adjective. Every slip, point at the box and make the student choose which marker survives.
not as… as decoded backwards (who is healthier?)Concept check with Simon's line every time: Teenagers aren't as healthy as they were — so WHO wins? The past teenagers. Draw the two bars unequal on the board.
One-word answers in the best-age discussionThe rule of the lesson: one word is not an opinion — an opinion needs a because. Hold up two fingers: the reason is the second finger.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the OGE — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 20–26 (put the word into the right grammatical form inside a text) — comparative and superlative forms are one of their most regular moves — and task 12 (reading: match questions to texts).

Choosing between -er/-est and more/most, and spelling the form right (happier, the biggest), is exactly the muscle tasks 20–26 test. And today's two-voice forum text about teenage life is careful reading for detail — the task-12 skill. Nothing extra to drill: today's lesson does it.
