🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Tell a life in order using twelve fixed phrases, and write a biography in four paragraphs from a fact file — with the full name once, the pronoun everywhere else, and the passive where it belongs.
What is genuinely hard
Not the sentences. The shape. Left alone, a Year 7 biography is eight facts in a row, each one starting with the person's full name, and no paragraph breaks at all. That is not a grammar mistake and it is exactly what criterion K2 of the exam email takes marks off for.
Typical error to expect
A biography in which the full name is repeated in every sentence: Kevin Pearce was a snowboarder. Kevin Pearce won competitions. Kevin Pearce had an accident. The full name goes in the first sentence of the text, the first name opens the other paragraphs, and everything else is he or she [exam report, criterion K2]
He was born in 15th June / We met in Monday instead of on — on with dates and days, in with months and years [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary stage is deliberately built as an argument: twelve life events put into an order that has no single right answer, because move house can come anywhere or never. Arguing about the order is what fixes the phrases, and the invented life that follows is the spoken rehearsal for the written task. The listening sits in the middle rather than at the end because it is the hardest format in the course and needs fresh ears. Then the writing takes the last forty minutes in four separate stages, exactly as the book stages it, and GET READY is the one that matters: the class takes the Kevin Pearce text apart and discovers the four-paragraph shape, where the full name goes, and where it turns into he. Nothing is explained; it is counted. The first draft is written in class so that nobody takes an empty page home, and IMPROVE is a peer check against three named criteria rather than a general read-through.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Kevin Pearce's name appears four times in that whole text. Not twenty. Four — once at the top of each paragraph, and after that he is just «he». Today you find out how that text is built, and then you build one about Serena Williams. It is the last thing you write this year and it is the one I will read properly.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, four rounds of past-passive retrieval, and the bridge into the last lesson of the unit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — passive rewind. Kagan Showdown, four rounds, past passive only: This school / build / 1974 · The letter / write / my grandmother · My phone / not make / in this country · The park / open / last year.
  3. Then the question that closes Thursday: Which of those four needs «by»? (the second one — it is the news).
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page one hundred and fifteen. Today: a whole life in twelve phrases — and then you write one.
📺 On screen: the four prompts read out one at a time and the corrections written in chalk — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: build four past passives against the clock. Expect the form to be solid and the by decision to be right in three out of four. Typical problem: The park was opened last year by somebody — the overcorrection, and one reminder is enough now

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard the writing has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write three sentences on the board, one under the other: Kevin Pearce was a great snowboarder. Kevin Pearce won many competitions. People gave him many prizes.
  2. All three are correct English. All three are badly written. Why? Take answers for 45 seconds — the name three times, two sentences that should be one, and a third that wants the passive.
  3. Do not rewrite them yet. Say: Keep that. We fix it at the sixtieth minute.
  4. Explain the aim in 30 seconds: twelve phrases, one listening, and then the last piece of writing of the year — a biography.
  5. Set the bar: Four paragraphs. The full name once. Everything else is «she».
📺 On screen: the three bad sentences in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up until the writing stage, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: find three things wrong with three correct sentences. Expect the repeated name to be spotted first and the passive last. Typical problem: they say «it's boring» — accept it and ask what exactly makes it boring, which produces the real answer

8–20′
Vocabulary · a life in order
pairs → fours
Why: Twelve phrases put into an order that has no single right answer — which is what makes the class argue, and arguing is what fixes the phrases.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.115: twelve life events put into an order, and the argument that comes with it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed, 90 seconds in pairs: Write down the big things that happen in a life. As many as you can. Then open the book and compare with the twelve.
  2. Pre-teach the three that are usually missing: find part-time work, start working or training, take exams.
  3. ex.1 in pairs, 4 minutes: put all twelve in order. Then pairs join into fours and have to agree on one order — the disagreement is the task. A possible order (TB p.226): be born · learn to walk and talk · start school · change schools · become a teenager · go to high school · find part-time work · take exams · travel · start working or training · get married. move house could come at any point, or several times, or not at all — and that is a good argument to let run for a minute.
  4. Take the argument about move house deliberately: it can come anywhere, several times, or never, and there is no right answer.
  5. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with the twelve phrases on cards, 3 minutes, five trades each.
📺 On screen: the twelve phrases on the screen so the class can move them into order together (Backup: they are printed on SB p.115 — pairs number them in pencil in the paper book and you write the agreed order on the board)
👦 Students do: order twelve phrases, then argue about the order in fours. Expect a genuine disagreement about get married and travel. Typical error: born used without be — the one phrase in the list that is a passive

20–30′
Speaking · invent a life
fours
Why: A whole life invented in four minutes using all twelve phrases. It is the rehearsal for the biography, and it is spoken, so nobody is stuck on spelling yet.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.115: a whole invented life, using all twelve phrases, told to another group

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the task: Invent one person. Tell their life from birth to now, using ALL twelve phrases. Four minutes. Use the Kevin Pearce text as the shape if a group stalls.
  2. The rule that makes it language and not a list: Every phrase has to have a date or an age with it. She was born in 2005. She started school in 2012.
  3. Groups tell their life to another group, 3 minutes. The listeners' job: count the twelve phrases and say which one was missing.
  4. Two groups tell the class. Take one question each from the floor.
  5. Variation from TB p.226 if the class is quick: research a real person on a phone and tell their life for the others to guess who it is.
📺 On screen: the twelve phrases left on the board as a checklist — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: invent and narrate a whole life in fours. Expect the invented lives to become improbable fast, which is fine — the phrases are what is being practised. Typical error: the present simple creeping in for the middle of the life

30–42′
Listening · Tanya's new school
solo → pairs
Why: Five three-option questions on one long dialogue — the hardest listening format in the course, and the one where all three options are always mentioned.

WHAT WE DO: the Listening on p.115 (track 146): five questions, three options, one long conversation

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The format in two lines: Tanya talks to her friend Jon about her new school. Five three-option questions: what time she arrived for her exam, how she gets to school at the moment, what she can take home from school, what Jon has just started doing, and why Jon wants to stay at his school next year.
  2. Before anything plays, 2 minutes on the questions: TB p.226: underline the key words in each question before the recording. In question 1 underline time and arrive, because several times will be said; in question 2 underline at the moment, because the answer depends on which period is being talked about. Ask the class how they say the times out loud first — eight thirty, half past eight.
  3. Ask the two orientation questions from TB p.226: How do you say these times out loud? (eight thirty, half past eight) Does Jon want to go to a new school too? (no).
  4. Play track 146. First time you answer. Thirty seconds of silence, pens down. Play it again: Second time you check. Change an answer only if you can say why.
  5. Check with Kagan Showdown — A, B or C on the mini-whiteboard, all boards up at once. Key: 1 A · 2 C · 3 A · 4 A · 5 B.
  6. Name the trap after the check: Where marks go: questions 1 and 2. Both have all three options mentioned in the recording, and both turn on a single phrase — I got there at and at the moment. Hearing a number is not an answer.
📺 On screen: track 146 in the board app with the five questions on the screen (Backup: the questions are printed on SB p.115 and the audioscript is on TB p.304 — read it out yourself, twice, at natural speed and with no extra help)
👦 Students do: listen twice under exam conditions, then reveal together. Expect 3 out of 5. Typical error: question 1 — three times are said and the one that counts is the one after I got there at

Prepare to write · GET READY
pairs → class
Why: The model is not a text to copy but a text to take apart. Counting the paragraphs and tracking where the name turns into a pronoun is the entire craft of the genre.

WHAT WE DO: the GET READY box p.115: the Kevin Pearce text taken apart, not re-read

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books at p.114. The task is counting, not reading: GET READY — five questions about the Kevin Pearce text, and the answers are the whole lesson (TB p.226): 1 there are four paragraphs · 2 their topics are early life · the accident · life after the accident · what he does now · 3 Kevin's name is used in the first sentence of each paragraph · 4 his full name is used only in the first sentence of the whole text · 5 the pronoun he is used in the rest of each paragraph.
  2. Give them the five questions in pairs, 4 minutes, and make them mark the text: circle every occurrence of the name, underline the first sentence of each paragraph.
  3. Take the answers and write the shape on the board as four boxes: early life · the accident · life after it · what he does now.
  4. Now go back to the three bad sentences from the lesson frame and fix them together: TB p.226, the board demonstration that teaches the whole thing in ninety seconds. Write: Kevin Pearce was a great snowboarder. Kevin Pearce won many competitions. People gave him many prizes. Ask what is wrong (the name three times; two sentences that should be one; a third that would be better in the passive). Then rewrite it together: Kevin Pearce was a great snowboarder who won many competitions. He was given many prizes.
  5. Kagan Numbered Heads Together on one last question: Find two passives in the Kevin Pearce text. (was born, was started by Kevin and his brother).
📺 On screen: the Kevin Pearce text on the screen so the class can mark the same copy together (Backup: it is printed on SB p.114 — everyone marks their own paper book, and you draw the four-paragraph shape on the board as the class reports)
👦 Students do: count paragraphs and names, then repair the board sentences. Expect the «name four times, not twenty» point to genuinely land here. Typical error: they count the name in the fact file as well as in the text — worth clarifying, because the fact file is not prose

54–62′
Prepare to write · PLAN
solo → pairs
Why: Nobody writes a good biography from a fact file without deciding first what goes in which paragraph. Ten minutes of planning saves the twenty minutes of writing.

WHAT WE DO: the PLAN box p.115: the Serena Williams fact file turned into four paragraph headings

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the fact file together, 3 minutes: SB p.115, the Serena Williams fact file: born 26th September 1981 in Michigan; the family moved to Los Angeles in the early 80s; began to play tennis in 1984; her father became her coach in 1995; won her first tournament, the US Open, in 1999; won Wimbledon and became the number one player in the world in 2002; started her own designer label, Aneres — Serena spelt backwards — in 2004; won the Australian Open in 2005 and 2007; opened the Serena Williams Secondary School in Kenya in 2008; was made an International Goodwill Ambassador for UNICEF in 2011; won the singles gold medal at the Olympic Games in 2012. She speaks English and some French, Spanish and Italian; her husband is Alexis Ohanian and their daughter was born on 1st September 2017.
  2. Ask what they already know about her and what they think of the life — 60 seconds, no more.
  3. Then the planning itself: PLAN — how many paragraphs, what each one is about, and when you will use the full name, the first name and the pronoun. TB p.226 adds one rule worth writing on the board: a paragraph has a minimum of two sentences.
  4. The decision to make on paper before writing: which facts go in which paragraph. Not all of them will fit, and choosing is part of the task.
  5. Kagan RallyCoach: partners read each other's four headings and ask one question — Why is that fact in that paragraph?
  6. Two minutes on the past endings while the plan is still on the page: moved /d/ · opened /d/ · started /ɪd/ · worked /t/.
📺 On screen: the Serena Williams fact file on the screen (Backup: it is printed on SB p.115 — read it aloud from the paper book, line by line, and write the four empty paragraph headings on the board for everyone to copy)
👦 Students do: turn thirteen facts into four paragraph headings. Expect the tennis facts to crowd into one paragraph and the school in Kenya to be left out — both are good decisions to discuss. Typical error: four headings that are all «tennis»

🎯 Writing out · the biography
solo, silent
Why: The last piece of writing of the year, built on a model and a plan, started in class so that nobody takes an empty page home.

WHAT WE DO: the WRITE stage: the first draft, in silence, against the clock

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The instructions, and all three of them go on the board: Four paragraphs. The full name once, at the top. The passive at least twice.
  2. WRITE: 14 minutes, silent mode, alone. The realistic instruction: Two paragraphs finished is better than four unfinished. Start at the beginning of her life.
  3. Give one alternative opening on the board so that nobody writes thirteen sentences beginning with a date: In 1999 she won her first tournament against She won her first tournament, the US Open, in 1999.
  4. Walk the rows in silence. Answer nothing about content; point at the three instructions on the board instead.
  5. Two minutes before the end: Stop. Count your paragraphs. Count how many times you wrote her name.
📺 On screen: the three instructions and the alternative opening written on the board, and a 14-minute timer (Backup: if there is no timer, write the minutes remaining on the board at 10, 5 and 2 — in writing, never out loud)
👦 Students do: write a first draft in silence. Expect two to three paragraphs from most and four from a few. Typical error: the second paragraph opening with Serena Williams again — which the two-minute count at the end catches before you do

78–86′
Prepare to write · IMPROVE
pairs
Why: Peer repair against three named criteria. The missing paragraph break and the fifth repetition of the full name are found by a partner far faster than by the writer.

WHAT WE DO: the IMPROVE box p.115: peer repair against three named criteria

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap papers. Kagan RallyCoach with a fixed three-item checklist, and nothing else: (1) how many paragraphs? (2) how many times is the full name written? (3) find one passive.
  2. The rule of the stage: You may not rewrite your partner's biography. You may only write a number in the margin and ask one question.
  3. Then the criteria said out loud, because they are also the exam's: IMPROVE — pairs read each other's biography and check three things: is the information all there, are the paragraphs real paragraphs, and is the full name used once rather than five times.
  4. Name what actually costs marks: Where marks go, and it is the same in the exam email: the name repeated instead of a pronoun, and four facts stapled together instead of four paragraphs. Neither is a grammar mistake, and both cost marks under criterion K2.
  5. Everyone makes ONE change to their own text in a different colour before the papers go in. One, not five.
  6. Two students read a paragraph to the class — the first paragraph only, because that is where the shape is.
📺 On screen: the three checklist items in chalk — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: check a partner's draft against three items, then make one change. Expect the paragraph count to be the thing most often missing and the passive the thing most often already there. Typical error: they correct spelling instead of shape — the three-item rule is what prevents it

Structure
She was born in Michigan in 1981. · The family moved to Los Angeles in the early 80s. · Her father became her coach in 1995. · She was made an International Goodwill Ambassador for UNICEF. · Since 2014 he has been visiting schools and hospitals.
Form
past simple for the events, past passive for what was done to the person, since + present perfect for what is still true. Dates: on 26th September, in 1981, in the early 80s.
Pron. watch
the three past endings inside a biography: moved /d/ · opened /d/ · started /ɪd/ · worked /t/. Two minutes inside the writing stage, not a separate block.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«be born» — can you say I born in 2012? → no. It is always I was born, and it is a passive.
In the Kevin Pearce text, how many paragraphs are there? → four. And how many times does his full name appear? → once, in the first sentence.
«Write four paragraphs.» How many sentences does a paragraph need at minimum? → two. One sentence is not a paragraph.
In the listening, question 2 asks how Tanya gets to school at the moment. Does it matter what she did last month? → no, and last month will be mentioned.
«Serena Williams won Wimbledon in 2002. Serena Williams became number one in 2002.» What is wrong? → the name twice. The second one is She — or the two sentences become one.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
