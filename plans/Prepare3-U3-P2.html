🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Talk about school for a minute without stopping — the seven collocations with take, and a group presentation of a school they invent themselves.
What is genuinely hard
Not the vocabulary. The difficulty is that a question about school gets a one-word answer — «Maths.», «Two.» — because nobody has ever asked them to keep going. And take looks like one word and behaves like seven.
Typical error to expect
A one-word reply («Maths.», «Two.») does not count as an answer at all: an answer needs a verb in it [national exam report]
I make sport / I make homework instead of do sport / do homework [high priority, A2/B1] — and in this unit the same trap with take: you TAKE an exam, you do not make one.
Why the lesson is built this way
The lesson is built backwards from the presentation at 66′: to describe a school you need a model of somebody else's, the words, and notes — so those three arrive in exactly that order. The blog goes first because it is the richest input of the unit and because a text about a girl who left school at eight starts the argument by itself; the five questions on it are the same format as the exam lesson three weeks ago, run here as ordinary classwork with no mention of that. Talking points comes straight after, while the disagreement is hot. Take sits in the middle as a change of gear and is learnt as whole phrases, never as a verb with a list of translations. The listening is placed before the productive stage on purpose: hearing a real family talk about learning without school is what makes the invented school ambitious rather than lazy. Notes get their own ten minutes because a presentation without notes is a mumble.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Today you build the school you would actually go to. But first — meet Clarissa, who did not go to school at all for seven years, and a family who learnt in a camper van. One rule for the presentation: everybody in the group speaks. Nobody hides behind the poster.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a clinic on three adverb sentences and the bridge into the spread

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. A clinic on yesterday's correction sheet: three sentences on the board with no names attached — He writes more quicker than me. · You speak English really good. · She remembers words easiest in our class. Thirty seconds — fix all three on your board.
  3. Check together; the student whose hand went up first explains why. Ninety seconds, no lecture.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Yesterday you compared people in this room. Today — a girl who was not in any room. Page thirty-four.
📺 On screen: the three sentences written on the board before the lesson — the board is the medium, no backup needed
👦 Students do: correct them on mini-whiteboards. Expect the first two to be fixed by almost everybody and the third by half. Typical error: they add the and forget to change easiest to the most easily

Lesson frame

Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim: talk about school without stopping after one word

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. One question on the board and nothing else: What's your favourite subject? Ask a student. Take the one-word answer. Then write it up and put a big cross next to it.
  2. That is not an answer. An answer has a verb and a reason. Write the good version underneath.
  3. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: seven meanings of one verb, and by the end of the lesson a school of your own, described out loud.

Reading · Clarissa's blog
solo → pairs
Why: One long text and five questions — the format met in the last exam lesson, now inside an ordinary lesson and on a topic they have opinions about. The skill is the same; the framing is deliberately not.

WHAT WE DO: the blog on p.34: where she is studying now, then five multiple-choice questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open, one minute on the photo: Look at the girl. What is she doing? How is she feeling? Elicit or pre-teach blog — a record of your activities or opinions that you put online and keep updating (TB p.65).
  2. ex.1 against the clock: Ninety seconds. Is she studying at home or at school NOW? Key: At school.
  3. Comprehension before the questions, TB p.65: TB p.65, before the five questions: «How old was Clarissa when she started homeschooling?» (eight) · «What was her hobby?» (computer games) · «When did she see her friends?» (at the weekends). And one note that unlocks question 1: wasn't doing well in tests means the same as having problems at school.
  4. ex.2 — five questions, 7 minutes, silent. Then Kagan RallyCoach: the partner asks for the LINE, not the letter. Key: 1 C · 2 B · 3 B · 4 A · 5 C
  5. Fast finishers (TB p.65): draw Clarissa's timetable for one homeschooling day and be ready to present it.
📺 On screen: the blog on the screen so a line can be shown to everybody at once (Backup: everybody reads from the book on p.34 and you keep the five letters in a column on the board as the answers come in)
👦 Students do: read for gist, then answer five questions with the line. Expect 3–4 of 5. Typical error: question 1 — they miss that wasn't doing well in tests and having problems at school are the same thing

24–30′
Talking points · school or home
pairs → class
Why: A short opinion exchange with a real disagreement in it: they use the unit's language to argue about something that could actually happen to them.

WHAT WE DO: the four questions on p.34: what is good about school, and what about learning at home

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The four questions off the page: What do you like about going to school? · What are the good things about homeschooling? · What are the bad things about it? · Would you like to study at home? Why? / Why not?
  2. Kagan Timed Pair Share: 40 seconds each, then swap.
  3. One minute of background if the class is hungry for it (TB p.65): TB p.65: before state schools, home education was the normal thing and only the rich could afford tutors. It came back in the middle of the twentieth century with parents who were dissatisfied with schools, and it is still small — under five percent in the USA. Two usual reasons: a child who needs a personal approach, and parents who want to be involved. It is regulated differently everywhere and is not legal in every country — it is illegal in Germany. The two standard criticisms: teaching is a profession, and a child at home meets fewer other children.
  4. Take a vote at the end, hands up: who would actually choose home? Then one sentence from the biggest minority.
📺 On screen: the four questions on the screen (Backup: the same four questions are printed in the book on p.34 — read them out and keep the vote count on the board)
👦 Students do: give an opinion with a reason. Expect the class to split roughly in half and to argue about friends rather than about learning. Typical error: an opinion with no because — ask for it every time

Vocabulary 2 · take
pairs → class
Why: One verb with seven meanings. Learnt as whole collocations, because that is how the exam asks for them and how they are stored in the head anyway.

WHAT WE DO: one verb, seven meanings: ex.1–4 p.35 (track 40) plus a Quiz-Quiz-Trade mingle

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 first, ten seconds: what does take mean in that sentence? Key: to do
  2. ex.2 — the mind map: match the seven meanings to the seven phrases, in pairs, 4 minutes. Play track 40 to check and repeat. Key: a take an umbrella (carry) · b take photos or pictures (make) · c take exams (do) · d take a subject (study) · e take the second turning (go along) · f take medicine (use) · g take a train (catch)
  3. Say the trap out loud once: in English you take an exam. You never make one and you never do one.
  4. ex.4 — seven gapped questions, then answer them in pairs, 4 minutes. Key: 1 umbrella · 2 turning · 3 subject · 4 photos · 5 train · 6 medicine · 7 exam
  5. ⚠️ Two typos in the Student's Book on p.35 ex.4, worth knowing before a student points them out: item 2 reads «do I need take» (the to is missing) and item 3 has a space before the question mark. Read them out correctly and move on.
  6. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with phrase cards, 3 minutes: the partner has to give the whole phrase, not the translation.
📺 On screen: track 40 in the whiteboard app with the mind map on the screen (Backup if the board is down: read the seven phrases aloud twice yourself and use the mind map in the paper book; the Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards are printed in advance)
👦 Students do: match, complete and trade. Expect take the second turning to be new to everybody. Typical error: make an exam — take it out on the spot, it is the one that costs marks later

Listening · world schooling
solo → pairs
Why: Eight right/wrong statements on a long interview, with the recording paused as they go — listening for detail without drowning.

WHAT WE DO: the interview with Ethan on p.35 (track 41): the phrase he uses, then eight statements

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pre-listening, TB p.66: TB p.66: pre-teach camper van — a large vehicle you can live in while travelling, with cooking equipment, beds and sometimes a toilet. Then «Where is Bali?» (Indonesia) · «What do you know about Bali?» (it is popular with tourists).
  2. Play track 41 once for ex.1: What phrase does Ethan use for the education he had on the trip? Key: World Schooling
  3. Then ex.2, eight statements, right or wrong. Play it again — and pause. TB p.66: pause the recording after each answer, or after two if they come close together — a long recording breaks into pieces this way and nobody drowns. For stronger students one extra question: «How old was Ruth when they went travelling?» (nine).
  4. A wrong statement is not ticked, it is REPAIRED: say what the recording actually said. Key: 1 ✓ · 2 ✗ (Ethan's mum lost her job.) · 3 ✗ (Ruth and Ethan went to an ordinary school before they went travelling.) · 4 ✗ (Their family and neighbours thought Ethan's mum and dad were mad, so they must have told them.) · 5 ✓ · 6 ✓ · 7 ✗ (Ethan wanted to go back to school.) · 8 ✗ (Ethan says he worked much harder when he was travelling.)
  5. Kagan Numbered Heads Together to collect: Statement seven — number threes, boards up!
  6. ex.3 in pairs, two minutes: would you like to be world schooled, and what would you miss?
📺 On screen: track 41 in the whiteboard app, played twice with pauses (Backup if the board is down: read the interview aloud from the audioscript on TB p.289, twice, taking both voices and pausing where the recording pauses)
👦 Students do: listen twice with pauses and repair the wrong statements. Expect 5–6 of 8. Typical error: statement 2 — they hear «job» and tick it, missing that she LOST it

Notes · my perfect school
groups of 3–4
Why: Planning time is not dead time: eleven questions turn into notes, and notes are what make the difference between a presentation and a mumble.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.35: eleven questions turned into notes, and four speaking roles agreed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of three or four. The eleven questions go on the screen: Where is the school? · What lessons do you have? · How many students are there? · Who are the teachers? · Do you have homework? · How many lessons a day and a week? …
  2. Notes only. Not sentences. Six minutes. A note is three words, and the presentation is built from it — that is the whole point.
  3. Roles are decided IN the group and written on the notes, before anybody speaks: who says where and what lessons, who says the day and the homework, who says the rules and the food, who says why it is better than other schools.
  4. One condition on the board that ties the block together: Your last sentence must be a comparison. «Our school is better because students learn more easily.»
📺 On screen: the eleven questions on the screen while they take notes (Backup: the same eleven questions are printed in the book on p.35 — put the four roles on the board and let the groups work from the page)
👦 Students do: make notes and share out the roles. Expect the food and the uniform questions to generate the most enthusiasm. Typical error: they write full sentences and then read them out — stop it at the note stage, not at the presentation

🎯 Speaking out · my perfect school
groups → class
Why: The productive outcome: a group presentation with everybody speaking, built on their own notes and using the comparatives from the last lesson for a reason.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.35: each group presents its school, everybody speaks, the class chooses

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Rehearse first, 4 minutes, inside the group and all groups at once — noisy on purpose. Kagan Stand Up–Hand Up–Pair Up: each student says their part to somebody from ANOTHER group first.
  2. Then the presentations: 90 seconds per group, four voices, no reading from the sheet. You point at the next speaker instead of asking.
  3. The audience has a job, or it is not an audience: each listening group writes down ONE question to ask, and asks it.
  4. Vote at the end: which school would you enrol in tomorrow? Winner explains in one sentence why they think they won.
  5. Log errors silently on your correction sheet; the clinic is the start of the next lesson.
📺 On screen: a 90-second timer on the screen while each group presents (Backup: time it on the wall clock and call «thirty seconds left» yourself; nothing else needs to be displayed)
👦 Students do: present in four voices and ask one question of another group. Expect the second half of the presentations to be visibly better than the first — say so. Typical error: one student takes over — point at the next speaker, do not negotiate

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I make an exam» — is that English? → no: you TAKE an exam. In English you take it and you pass it; you never make it.
«Take the second turning» — is this about a car or about a photo? → about the way: take here means go along.
«My favourite subject is maths.» — is that an answer? → it is half of one. An answer at this level has a reason in it.

Likely problemWhat you do

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
