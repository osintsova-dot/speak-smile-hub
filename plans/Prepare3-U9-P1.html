🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name twenty parts of the body, and say that the person doing the action and the person it happens to are the same — with myself, yourself, himself, herself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves, and with by + ‑self for «alone».
What is genuinely hard
Not the forms — the table takes thirty seconds. The difficulty is knowing WHEN English wants a reflexive and when it does not. Russian marks this on the verb itself, so a Russian speaker either puts a reflexive everywhere (I feel myself bad) or nowhere (I hurt me).
Typical error to expect
I hurt me / She saw her in the mirror instead of myself, herself — the object pronoun where the subject and the object are the same person [high priority, A2/B1]
I feel myself bad / I washed myself and dressed myself — a reflexive where English does not use one. Feel, wash and dress take no reflexive: I feel ill, I got washed [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary comes first because the reading, the grammar and the final conversation are all made of it, and because twenty body parts are learnt from a labelled picture in twelve minutes and from a word list never. The grammar then arrives inside Ben's blog, where four reflexives sit unmarked in a story about falling off a bike — the discovery stage asks one question about them and nothing else. by myself gets its own short slot rather than a footnote: it is the half students skip and the half that carries the meaning «alone». The final stage has pairs write and perform their own accident conversation, which is the only way to make the reflexive necessary rather than decorative. Errors are collected silently during the performances and repaired together at the end, with no names attached.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two sentences, one word different. Dan saw her in a film. Klara saw herself in a film. One of them is about two people and one is about one person. By the end of today you will hear which — and you will write a story in which somebody falls off a bike and nobody helps them.”

Vocabulary · body parts
class → pairs
Why: Twenty words in one stage is a lot, so meaning comes from a labelled picture and from the teacher pointing, not from translation. The recording carries the pronunciation, which is where stomach and tongue would otherwise be lost.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.98 (tracks 125 and 126): twelve labelled parts, then eight more

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. Point at yourself and elicit — start with the ones they have and move to the ones they do not: head, arm, hand … then What is inside here? (brain) And here? (heart, stomach).
  2. Books open, ex.1 in pairs, 4 minutes. Play track 125 to check and repeat chorally. Key: 1 brain · 2 blood · 3 finger · 4 toe · 5 ankle · 6 thumb · 7 neck · 8 ear · 9 heart · 10 stomach · 11 tongue · 12 back.
  3. ex.2 straight after — the eight easier ones, A to H, then track 126 to check and repeat. Key: A eye · B teeth · C arm · D hand · E foot · F mouth · G nose · H head.
  4. Drill the four traps deliberately, because the recording goes past them fast: stomach ends in /k/ · thumb has a silent b · tongue has no ‑ue sound · heart rhymes with start, not with hurt.
  5. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with picture cards, 3 minutes, five trades each.
📺 On screen: tracks 125 and 126 in the board app, with the labelled picture on the screen (Backup if the board is down: the picture is printed on SB p.98 — read the twenty words aloud yourself and have the class point at the number or the letter in the paper book, then repeat chorally; the Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards are printed in advance and need no technology)
👦 Students do: label, check, repeat, then mingle. Expect 14–16 out of 20 unaided. Typical errors: ankle confused with knee; toe pronounced as two; stomach read letter by letter

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4 p.98: the pair test on the picture, then twelve definitions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 first, 2 minutes: one student says a number or a letter, the partner says the body part. Then swap. Everybody produces all twenty.
  2. ex.4 in pairs, 4 minutes — the definitions. Some answers need a plural, which is the hidden work of the exercise. Key: 1 toes · 2 stomach · 3 brain · 4 ankle · 5 fingers · 6 blood · 7 neck · 8 thumb · 9 ears · 10 back · 11 heart · 12 tongue.
  3. Books closed for the last two minutes: you say the word, the class gives the definition. Reversing it is what shows whether the word is actually theirs.
  4. Mixed ability, TB p.193: TB p.193: for the body-parts test in ex.3, pair a stronger student with a weaker one and require the stronger one to explain WHY, not just say the word.

Why: The grammar arrives inside a story with blood in it. Four reflexive pronouns sit unmarked in a blog post about falling off a bike, which is far better input than four example sentences on a slide.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5–6 p.98–99 (track 127): Ben's blog, then six right/wrong statements

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the situation in one line and pre-teach exactly two words (TB p.193): A cyclist is a person who rides a bike. A brake is the part that makes it stop. Then: Ben got a new bike for his birthday. Two minutes — which parts of his body did he hurt?
  2. Key to ex.5: his ankle and his ear.
  3. Play track 127 so they hear the blog read at speed while they follow.
  4. ex.6, six right/wrong statements, in pairs, 3 minutes. Kagan RallyCoach: the partner must point at the line that proves it. Key: 1 ✓ · 2 ✗ — the path isn't usually busy · 3 ✗ — Ben fell off by himself · 4 ✗ — the cyclists rode away · 5 ✓ · 6 ✓.
  5. Two questions to close it (TB p.193): Was this Ben's fault? Why didn't the cyclists stop and help him? Let the argument run for one minute — the answer to the second one is in the text and it contains a reflexive.
  6. For your own reference, the whole story: SB p.99, a blog post by Ben09, Monday 13th May. Ben got a new bike for his birthday and posted a photo of himself with it «before the accident». He went out riding by himself on the bike path, which is usually quiet, but that day two cyclists were coming towards him. He braked hard, forgetting that the new bike's brakes were much better than the old ones, stopped dead and fell off, hurting his ankle badly. The two cyclists rode straight past — they were enjoying themselves too much to notice him — so he had to get up and pick the bike up by himself. He cycled home slowly with his heart racing, wondering why people were staring at him, and only at home discovered the blood all over his T-shirt from a cut on his ear.

👦 Students do: read, answer, then prove with a line. Expect statement 3 (whether the cyclists knocked him off) to be the one that is guessed. Typical error: they answer from what would be fair rather than from what the text says

Grammar · discovery
class → pairs
Why: Guided discovery aimed at one question and one only: is the object the same person as the subject? Everything else about reflexives is a table they can copy in thirty seconds.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 p.99: two sentences from the blog, the table, and the two meanings of by + ‑self

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. Take two or three students to the window or a mirror: Look. Can you see yourselves? Write the question on the board and ask the TB p.194 questions: What is the subject of this sentence? (you) What is the object? (yourselves) Are they the same people or different people? (the same).
  2. Books open, ex.1 — the two sentences from Ben's blog. Key: the same (person).
  3. ex.2, the table, in pairs, 2 minutes. Key: a himself · b herself · c ourselves · d yourselves. Then drill it: you say a subject pronoun, the class says the object and the reflexive — she — her — herself.
  4. Say the negative rule now, while the table is fresh, and keep it to three verbs (TB p.194): English does not use ‑self after feel, wash or dress. I feel ill. I got washed.
  5. ex.3 — the two sentences with by, matched to the two meanings. Key: 1 a (alone) · 2 b (without any help). Ask the follow-up that makes it stick: Name three things you like doing by yourself, and three things you cannot do by yourself.
📺 On screen: the two blog sentences and the empty table on the screen, filled in live as the class answers (Backup: draw the table in chalk in two columns before the lesson and fill it in with the class; the two sentences are printed on SB p.99)
👦 Students do: answer the two questions, fill the table, then sort the two meanings. Expect the table to take two minutes and the by distinction to take five — which is the right proportion. Typical error: themself invented for the plural

Why: Correcting other people's sentences is faster and safer than producing them, and the Showdown means every student answers every item instead of one student answering one.

🎯 Speaking out · your own accident
pairs → class
Why: The productive outcome: a conversation they write and perform, in which somebody hurts themselves and somebody else insists on coming along. The reflexive is not decoration in it — the story cannot be told without one.

WHAT WE DO: ex.7 p.99: write and perform a conversation about an accident, using the phrase pool

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Go back to the accidents you collected on the board at minute 8 — pairs choose one of those, or invent one. TB p.194 suggests keeping them not too serious: hitting your head on a low ceiling, slipping on ice.
  2. Show the phrase pool and ask, for each phrase, in what situation it could be used: SB p.99 ex.7, the phrase pool for the conversations: Help yourselves! · I tried it myself and it was delicious. · They didn't enjoy themselves that day. · He couldn't do it by himself. · We bought ourselves new clothes after that. · Did she cut herself?
  3. The rule of the stage, on the board: Your conversation must contain at least three ‑self words, and at least one of them must have «by» in front of it.
  4. Write in pairs, 5 minutes, using ex.6 as the shape. Then Kagan RallyRobin: practise it twice, swapping roles, so both students say both parts.
  5. Three or four pairs perform to the class. ⚠️ While they perform, do the thing that makes this stage worth twelve minutes: TB p.194, the instruction that makes the last stage worth doing: while pairs write and perform their accident conversations, note every reflexive-pronoun mistake you hear and put them on the board at the end for the class to repair together. Never with a name attached.

WB Unit 17 (p.68–71)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can use reflexive pronouns correctly when the subject and the object are the same person, and use by + ‑self to mean alone or without help.
  • Subsidiary. Twenty parts of the body; reading a personal blog for detail; the fixed phrase Help yourself!
  • Personal. Teach one question, not seven forms. If the class leaves able to ask themselves «is it the same person?», the lesson has worked even if yourselves needs another visit.
By the end, students can…
  • name twenty parts of the body, inside and outside
  • say that somebody did something to themselves
  • say that they did something alone or without any help
  • write and perform a short conversation about an accident

Structure
Have you ever fallen off your bike and hurt yourself? · They were enjoying themselves too much. · I went for a ride by myself. · I got up by myself. · Help yourselves!
Form
myself · yourself · himself · herself · itself · ourselves · yourselves · themselves. Used when the subject and the object are the same person. by + ‑self = alone / without help (SB p.99, reference SB p.163).
Pron. watch
the silent letters and the traps: stomach /ˈstʌmək/ with a /k/ at the end, tongue /tʌŋ/ with no ‑ue, thumb /θʌm/ with a silent b, heart /hɑːt/. Stress on the first syllable in every reflexive except the ‑selves plurals, where it stays on the ‑self.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I hurt myself.» Who did I hurt? → me. So the subject and the object are the same person.
«Dan saw her in a film.» Who did Dan see? → somebody else, Klara. So it is her, not herself.
«Klara saw herself in a film.» Who did Klara see? → Klara. Same person, so ‑self.
«I went for a ride by myself.» Was anyone with me? → no. by myself = alone.
«I got up by myself.» Did somebody help me? → no. Same words, second meaning: without help.
«I feel myself bad» — what is wrong? → English does not use a reflexive after feel. I feel bad.

Assumptions: SB p.98–99, syllabus lesson 56. The present perfect with ever and never is secure after blocks 7 and 8, and the About you questions use it deliberately. Of the twenty body words they usually know arm, hand, foot, head, eye, ear and mouth; ankle, thumb, tongue, stomach, brain and blood are the ones that need teaching. Object pronouns (him, her, them) are known and are the thing the reflexives will be confused with.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The whole class writes I hurt meDo not explain the rule again. Use the TB p.194 pair on the board: Dan knows Klara is an actress. He saw ___ in a film. (her) and Klara is an actress. She saw ___ in a film. (herself). Two sentences, one question — same person or different person? — and the contrast is visible without a word of theory.
Somebody produces I feel myself bad and the class agrees with themThis is the Russian verb showing through and it will happen. Write both versions up, say the English one twice and leave it on the board: I feel ill. Not «I feel myself». English does not use ‑self after feel, wash or dress. Do not make a rule out of it — make a list of three verbs.
She went herself instead of by herselfHold up one finger for by: No «by» — she went in person. With «by» — she went alone. Then ask the room which one Ben did (by himself, both times) and move on.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 20–26 (put the given word into the right grammatical form inside a text). Pronouns sit on the codifier at 2.4.16 and reflexives turn up in that task regularly.

The exam does not ask «what is the rule for myself». It puts he in brackets in a sentence that already says he hurt ___ playing football, and the meaning is the only clue that the answer is himself. Deciding whether the subject and the object are the same person — which is exactly today's discovery stage — is the whole skill.
