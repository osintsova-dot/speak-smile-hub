🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Produce the term's language in speech, under exam conditions: six speaking cards covering the five units — a one-minute monologue, a photo comparison, a negotiation, a personal-information exchange, a presentation opening and a speculation task — each with forty seconds of preparation and a real listener.
What is genuinely hard
Speaking under time pressure is a retrieval problem before it is a language problem: students who can do a gap-fill on the future perfectly will produce nothing but present simple when a clock is running. The lesson's difficulty is keeping the pressure real (timed, no notes read aloud) while keeping the room safe enough for the quiet half to speak six times.
Typical error to expect
Preparing in Russian and translating live — the single biggest cause of forty-second silences. Notes must be in English, in bullet points, or they are worse than no notes.
Answering the card in three sentences and stopping — an examiner's «and why?» is not a follow-up question, it is the mark.
Why the lesson is built this way
A carousel: six cards on six tables, students in pairs, four minutes per table, then everyone moves and changes partner. Everyone speaks at every table — there is no audience. The last twenty minutes drop the pressure deliberately: the paper is explained in detail, the marking scale goes on the board, and the class writes its own two-line revision plan for the evening. The lesson before an exam should end calm, not wound up.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
Today nobody writes anything except notes. Six tables, six tasks, six different partners, four minutes each — and forty seconds to prepare, exactly like the exam. At the end I'll tell you precisely what tomorrow's paper is, how it is marked, and what is worth doing tonight — which is less than you think.

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting at the door, then a check that the revision pages actually came back

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what was the last thing you bought? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — pages out. Everyone puts yesterday's revision pages on the desk, open. Walk round and look at them — that is the check, and it takes ninety seconds.
  3. On the board: Tell your partner the one thing on your page you are still least sure of. RallyRobin.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Today is speaking. Six tables, six tasks, everybody talks at every one of them.
📺 On screen: the prompt on the board. Backup: say it aloud; nothing else is needed.
👦 Students do: put their pages out, then name their weakest area to a partner.

5–14′
Warm-up · the language of buying time
whole class → pairs
Why: The single highest-return five minutes before any speaking exam: the language that prevents silence and the two moves that extend an answer.

WHAT WE DO: the survival kit for a timed speaking task, drilled before it is needed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board six fillers and phrases, and drill them chorally with a calm, unhurried tone: Well … · Let me think … · That's an interesting question … · OK, so … · What I mean is … · Sorry, could you repeat that?
  2. Explain why in one sentence: silence costs marks. A filler doesn't — it sounds like thinking, because it is.
  3. Drill the two follow-up moves that turn a three-sentence answer into an exam answer: … and the reason I say that is … · … for example, last summer …
  4. Timed Pair Share, forty-five seconds each: What did you do last weekend? The rule — you must use at least one filler and one the reason I say that is.
  5. Take one pair's answer as the model for the room.
📺 On screen: the six phrases and the two follow-up moves on the board — they stay up for the whole carousel. Backup: dictate them; eight lines takes ninety seconds.
👦 Students do: repeat chorally, then speak for forty-five seconds each with two required moves.

Set-up · six tables, six tasks, the rules
whole room
Why: A carousel lives or dies on its launch. Two minutes of rules and one demonstration buy forty minutes of uninterrupted speaking.

WHAT WE DO: the carousel is explained and demonstrated before it starts, because a badly launched carousel wastes ten minutes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Six tables, one card face up on each: 1 personal information — school and study (Unit 1); 2 a one-minute monologue: the best journey you have taken (Unit 2); 3 plans and predictions: your next twelve months (Unit 3); 4 compare two photographs of people (Unit 4); 5 negotiate: your class has 10,000 roubles — spend it (Unit 5 + Unit 3 language); 6 speculate: three strange photos, what must have happened (Unit 5).
  2. The rules, said once and written on the board: Forty seconds to prepare, in English, in bullet points. Both partners speak at every table. When I say change, you move one table clockwise and you change partner.
  3. Demonstrate the first card yourself for thirty seconds — badly at first (three sentences and stop), then well. The contrast teaches more than an explanation.
  4. Pairs are formed by you, and re-formed at each move: partner A moves clockwise, partner B stays. That way nobody works with the same person twice.
📺 On screen: the six table numbers and the three rules on the board. Backup: the cards are paper; the carousel needs no technology at all.
👦 Students do: listen to the demonstration, take a table and a partner, and start on your signal.

18–42′
🎯 Carousel · part one, tables 1–4
six tables
Why: Four cards × ninety seconds means six minutes of exam speaking per student in one block — more than most students get in a month.

WHAT WE DO: four minutes per table: forty seconds of preparation, then both partners speak with a real listener

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Start the timer. Four minutes per table: forty seconds preparing, ninety seconds for A, ninety seconds for B.
  2. Call change loudly and move them — do not let a table run over, or the last two cards get squeezed.
  3. You circulate with a sheet of paper divided into five columns, one per unit, and note errors under the relevant unit. Say nothing to anybody.
  4. Between tables 2 and 3, stop the room for sixty seconds and give one collective instruction based on what you have heard — most often: I am hearing three sentences and then silence. Add «and the reason I say that is».
  5. If a pair finishes early, they swap roles and do it again — never sit and wait.
📺 On screen: the four rules on the board and a visible timer. Backup: you call the times aloud; the cards are on the tables.
👦 Students do: prepare in English for forty seconds, then speak for ninety seconds at four different tables with four different partners.

42–48′
Break in the middle · stand, shake, one collective fix
open floor
Why: A midpoint reset in a speaking lesson is not lost time: the last two cards are noticeably better because of it.

WHAT WE DO: a deliberate reset at the midpoint of a speaking-heavy lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands, shakes out their hands, and takes ten seconds of silence. Teenagers who have talked for twenty-four minutes need it.
  2. Then one collective fix, chosen from your column sheet — the error that appeared in three or more pairs. Put two sentences on the board, one wrong, one right, and let the class say which is which.
  3. Stand Up–Sit Down for ninety seconds on that one point: you say a sentence, they stand if it is correct.
  4. Straight back to tables 5 and 6 while they are still standing.
📺 On screen: the two contrast sentences on the board. Backup: no equipment.
👦 Students do: stand, reset, then respond physically to ten correct/incorrect sentences.

48–62′
Carousel · part two, tables 5–6, then the best-of round
six tables → whole class
Why: A short performance round at the end gives the carousel a public conclusion and models the standard — but only after everyone has already spoken six times in private.

WHAT WE DO: the last two cards, then two volunteer pairs perform for the room

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Tables 5 and 6, four minutes each, same rules.
  2. Then the best-of round, six minutes: two pairs volunteer to do table 5 (the negotiation) in front of the class. Everybody else has a job — half the room listens for agreement language, half for a decision being reached.
  3. Feedback comes from the class, not from you, and it is specific: they agreed three times but never disagreed — an examiner wants both.
  4. Close with the one sentence that matters for tomorrow: everything you just did, you did without notes and without a dictionary. That is what you have.
📺 On screen: the two listening jobs written on the board before the volunteers start. Backup: none needed.
👦 Students do: speak at two more tables, then either perform or listen with a named job.

62–78′
The paper explained · exactly what happens tomorrow
desks
Why: Ambiguity is what makes a class anxious the night before a paper. The scale on the board today, and again tomorrow, removes most of it.

WHAT WE DO: the mid-year paper described part by part, with the marking scale on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board the four parts, with times: (1) Multiple-choice cloze, 8 items, 12 minutes. (2) Open cloze — one word per gap, 8 items, 12 minutes. (3) Word formation, 8 items, 12 minutes. (4) Listening, 8 short extracts, three options each, heard twice, about 15 minutes. Total 32 items, 55 minutes.
  2. Say where each part comes from in the term: part 1 is your vocabulary, part 2 is your grammar with nothing given, part 3 is Unit 4's suffixes, part 4 is listening for gist and detail.
  3. Put the scale on the board and leave it there, so that tomorrow nobody has to ask: 29–32 = excellent · 24–28 = good · 19–23 = satisfactory · below 19 = we work on it together in January, and nothing else happens.
  4. Answer procedural questions only — how long, what to bring, what happens if you are ill. Do not answer «will there be conditionals?» with anything except everything on it is from Units 1 to 5.
  5. One honest sentence about part 4 of the Cambridge paper, if anyone has seen it: I have taken the transformations out. Two of them need grammar we meet in January. You are not tested on what you have not been taught.
📺 On screen: the four parts, the timings and the marking scale on the board — photograph it yourself so it can go back up tomorrow. Backup: the same four lines dictated; it takes two minutes.
👦 Students do: copy the four parts and the scale into their revision page, then ask procedural questions only.

78–86′
Tonight's plan · two lines each, written in class
desks → pairs
Why: A narrow, small, written plan is the difference between revision that happens and revision that is intended.

WHAT WE DO: every student leaves with a specific, small, achievable revision plan

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The instruction is deliberately narrow: Write two lines. Line one: the ONE thing you will revise tonight. Line two: how long for — and it must be under twenty minutes.
  2. RallyRobin: read your two lines to a partner. The partner's only job is to ask is that really twenty minutes?
  3. Take four plans aloud. Push back on anything heroic: «revise all five units» is not a plan. Choose one.
  4. Say the sentence that gets said too rarely: Sleep is worth more than the sixth hour of revision. You have done the work in this room.
📺 On screen: the two-line frame on the board. Backup: no equipment.
👦 Students do: write a two-line plan, defend its size to a partner, and take it home on the revision page.

Wrap-up + homework
desks → door
Why: Self-assessment against the aim they heard at the start, so the lesson closes the loop it opened. Homework comes from their own workbook, not from a photocopy.

WHAT WE DO: self-check against today's aim, then the homework said once and written on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Self-check (60 seconds): Which of the six cards was hardest? Write the number. On the exit slip — it tells you what to build into January.
  2. Homework: nothing new. Reread your own revision pages aloud for ten minutes, plan one of the cards you did not reach, and bring a pen and a pencil tomorrow.
  3. Tomorrow: fifty-five minutes of paper, then we go through it together in this room, and then it is the holidays.
📺 On screen: the one-line homework and the word «pencil» circled on the board. Backup: the same message in the class chat this evening.
👦 Students do: write a card number on the slip and leave with the revision pages.

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will be better able to speak for one to two minutes on the topics and structures of Units 1–5 under timed conditions, with preparation in English and without reading notes aloud.
  • Subsidiary. Fillers and extension moves; peer feedback with a named listening job; understanding the format and marking of the mid-year paper.
  • Personal. Say nothing during the carousel. Every correction you make mid-task costs that pair thirty seconds of speaking, and the collective fix at the midpoint reaches everybody.
By the end, students can…
  • prepare a speaking task in English in forty seconds, in bullet points
  • speak for ninety seconds without stopping, using fillers instead of silence
  • extend an answer with a reason and an example
  • negotiate and reach a decision with a partner
  • state exactly what is on the mid-year paper and how it is marked

Structure
the term's speaking language: I'd rather … · I'm meeting … at six · Both photos show … · It must have been … · We could spend it on … · What do you think? · I see what you mean, but …
Form
no new form; the carousel samples the five systems of the term in unscripted speech, with the follow-up moves (the reason I say that is …, for example …) as the productive target.
Pron. watch
fillers must sound calm, not panicked: a falling tone on Well … and Let me think …. Rising, hurried fillers make a candidate sound less confident than silence would.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
Preparation: Do you write sentences or bullet points? In which language?
Fillers: Is «let me think» a mistake? — No. Is silence? — It costs marks.
Extension: You've said three sentences and the examiner is silent. What do you do? — Add a reason or an example.
ICQ for the carousel: How long to prepare? Who speaks at each table? Who moves when I say change?

Assumptions: Students have done all five units and yesterday's station revision, and have their revision pages. The six cards are prepared in advance; they are reusable every year.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Pairs finish a card in ninety seconds and chat.The instruction is prepared in advance: swap roles and do it again. Also, ninety seconds each means the table is never actually over early.
Notes are written in Russian and translated live.The rule is stated at the launch and enforced by inspection in the first two tables: bullet points, in English, or turn the paper over.
The quiet third of the class barely speaks.They speak at every table, in a pair, with no audience — the format removes the reason for silence. Put them with a different partner each time by moving A and keeping B.
The class is anxious about tomorrow and the carousel gets frantic.The last twenty-five minutes exist for exactly this: the paper is explained, the scale goes up, and the revision plan is capped at twenty minutes. End the lesson calm — that is a methodological decision, not kindness.
Somebody asks whether a topic «will be on the paper».One answer, repeated: everything on it is from Units 1 to 5. Do not hint further — hints reorganise the whole evening's revision around a guess.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 39–40 (the oral) and task 37.

The speaking carousel is the closest the course gets to the Russian oral: a card, forty seconds of preparation, a monologue with a fixed number of points, and a picture to describe. Doing it six times in one lesson with six different partners is worth more than one rehearsal of the whole exam.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
