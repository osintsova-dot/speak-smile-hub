🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Build person nouns with -er, -or, -ist and -ian (run → runner, music → musician, science → scientist) with the spelling under control, and discuss options and reach an agreement using the unit's functional phrases — What about…? · I'm not so sure · The problem with … is … · Yes, that's a good choice.
What is genuinely hard
The four suffixes make the same weak /ə/ or /ɪst/ sound, so nothing in the ear helps: the choice is memory plus spelling, and run doubles its n on the way. In the speaking half, the risk is the opposite — students agree instantly and the three-minute task dies in forty seconds.
Typical error to expect
«musicion», «scientis», «runer» — -er and -or sound identical (/ə/), so only the eye chooses; and run doubles its n. Word building is pure spelling marks in the exam.
Expect also I'm not agree for I don't agree — a Russian construction that survives right into the speaking exam.
Why the lesson is built this way
Last teaching lesson of the block, and it closes the spread: word building first (rules extracted, relay and mimes to fix them), then the whole second half is the exam speaking shape. Two recorded conversations give the listening discipline (true or false decided by a phrase), the recorded model shows two friends actually disagreeing politely, and then students run the same task themselves — B1 Speaking Part 3, three minutes, six options, one agreement — followed by the personal follow-up questions of Part 4. The advice clinic at the end reuses yesterday's If I were you inside today's functional language, which is exactly how the two lessons were designed to meet.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Allie never has any money. Evan has to choose his subjects and has no idea. Two problems, two conversations — and by the end of the lesson you can do what the people in them do: weigh six options, disagree politely, and actually decide.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a four-error clinic, one live second conditional, and the bridge into word building

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — delayed feedback. Four anonymous sentences from lesson 4's log on the board (a would in the if-half, a missing contraction, a mixed pair of halves, a was where were was better). Kagan RallyRobin: pairs fix all four.
  3. Then one live question to bridge: If you could earn money at the weekend, what would you do? Three answers, full sentences. Write the JOB words on the board — they are today's speaking task, and they do not know it yet.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): name the destination: Page one hundred and four, bottom. Four endings that all sound the same — then a conversation where two people have to decide something.
📺 On screen: four sentences in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: fix yesterday's errors, then answer one real second-conditional question. Expect the forms to be visibly steadier than yesterday — say so

Vocabulary · four suffixes, one sound
solo → pairs → class
Why: -er and -or sound identical, so the class is told the truth: only the eye chooses. The rules (doubling in runner, -ian on musician) are extracted from the book's own examples and then tested by spelling.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: build seven nouns; ex.2: suffix plus definition

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 solo, three minutes, from the box. Key: artist · cleaner · musician · receptionist · runner (the n doubles) · visitor — actor is the printed example.
  2. The two rules that actually exist go on the board: run → runner (short vowel, double the consonant) · music → musician (-ic words take -ian). Then the honest sentence: The rest is memory. -er and -or sound exactly the same — only your eyes can tell them apart.
  3. ex.2 in pairs, Kagan RallyCoach, five minutes: nine suffixes completed AND nine definitions matched. Key: 1 d blogger · 2 a competitor · 3 b scientist · 4 i football supporter · 5 g vegetarian · 6 f guitarist · 7 e novelist · 8 h comedian · 9 c goalkeeper.
  4. Feedback in full sentences: A goalkeeper is the player who stands in the goal. Every word then goes onto its colour column on the wall.
  5. Fast finishers: four more to place — banker, editor, psychiatrist, magician.
📺 On screen: SB p.104 in every book plus the two rules in chalk — paper and board are the media, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: build, match, define, sort onto the wall. Expect competitor and vegetarian to be the two nobody guesses — they are also the two the test likes

20–28′
Word-building relay
teams → pairs
Why: Retrieval as movement: the base word is called, teams build the person noun and spell it. The mime round from p.125 attaches each job word to an action before the listening needs them.

WHAT WE DO: a spelling relay, then the book's mime pairwork on p.125

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Relay first, four minutes: teams line up, you call the base word (act · clean · visit · science · politics · guitar · novel · goal · compete · run), the runner writes the person noun on the board and hands over the pen. One point for the word, one extra for perfect spelling.
  2. Read the mistakes aloud without naming anyone — runer, musicion, scientis. Three marks lost in three letters. Fix them on the wall.
  3. Then ex.3, the pairwork on p.125: two minutes, one partner mimes a job, the other names the person noun with its article. Swap after every correct guess.
  4. Close standing: Sit down when you've said one person-word that wasn't yours this morning.
📺 On screen: the board for the relay and SB p.125 for the mimes — board and paper are the media, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: run, write, spell, mime, guess. Everyone writes — the relay is built so that no team member can be skipped

Listening · two problems
solo → pairs
Why: Exam-shape listening done honestly: first the situation and who is being asked for advice, then true/false decided by a remembered phrase rather than a general feeling, with a second play to prove it.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2, track 87: who has the problem, who is being asked, and eight true/false items

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the scene before the first play: Two conversations. Two teenagers with a problem. Listen for TWO things only: what's wrong, and who they're asking for help.
  2. First play, then the table filled in pairs. Key: Allie never has any money and is asking her friend Ben · Evan doesn't know which subjects to choose for next year and is asking his teacher, Mrs Shaw.
  3. Before the second play, the eight statements are read and key words underlined — thirty seconds, silent.
  4. Second play, then pairs compare, then a third play ONLY for the items that split the room. Key: 1 false (he might get one in a year or so) · 2 false (she is afraid they might say no) · 3 true · 4 false (she decides to speak to them tonight) · 5 true · 6 false (Mrs Shaw suggests it and Evan agrees) · 7 true · 8 true.
  5. Feedback demands evidence: Number two is false. What did she actually say? … She's afraid they might say no. Exactly — she hasn't asked yet.
📺 On screen: track 87 in the board app (Backup: the audioscript is on TB p.277 — read the two conversations aloud in two voices with a student, and both tasks work identically)
👦 Students do: listen twice for different things, then defend answers with remembered words. Expect items 4 and 6 to be the split ones — both turn on a single phrase

WHAT WE DO: 90 seconds of jobs corners

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. Four corners: I'd love this job — I'd hate this job — I'd do it for money — I'd do it for free. I say the job, you walk. Walking dogs! Babysitting! Cleaning! Working in a café!
  2. One sentence from one corner per job, no more. Sit down. Somebody in a recording is about to have exactly this argument.

Speaking model · what about a café?
solo → pairs → class
Why: A recorded model shows the bar before students reach for it: two friends weigh six jobs, disagree politely and actually reach an agreement. Students tick the phrases they hear — noticing beats being told.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3, track 88: the six options, the recorded discussion, and the phrase hunt

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 first: the six pictures named as jobs. Key: the six jobs in the pictures: gardening · babysitting · doing housework · working in an office · waitressing in a café · walking dogs.
  2. ex.2, track 88, one play, one question: which job do they AGREE is best? Key: they discuss them in this order — walking dogs, gardening, doing housework, working in an office, babysitting, working in a café — and agree that WORKING IN A CAFÉ is best (a tip is extra money given to thank you for a service). One vocabulary check afterwards: A tip — extra money you give to say thank you for the service.
  3. ex.3: the Prepare to speak box read in its four groups (suggest · agree · disagree · decide), then track 88 again — tick every phrase you hear. Key: phrases you DO hear: What about…? · What do you think about…? · If I were you/him/her,… · Yes, you're right · That's true · Maybe you're right · I'm not so sure · The problem with … is … · … might be better · Yes, that's a good choice. NOT heard: I think … might be a good idea · No, I don't agree · But don't you think…? · I see what you mean.
  4. The four groups go onto the board as four columns and stay there for the rest of the lesson.
  5. Name the craft explicitly: They disagree four times and nobody gets offended. That's the skill — and it's why the task lasts three minutes instead of forty seconds.
📺 On screen: track 88 in the board app (Backup: the audioscript is on TB p.277 — read the dialogue with a strong student while the class ticks the phrases; the noticing task is unchanged)
👦 Students do: name the jobs, catch the agreement, tick the phrases they hear. Expect surprise that four of the box phrases are NOT used — that is a good thirty-second discussion

52–66′
🎯 Speaking out · deciding together
pairs
Why: The productive outcome, B1 Speaking Part 3 shape: three minutes to discuss all six options and agree on one, with the functional phrases compulsory and a partner tracking which ones were used.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4: three minutes, six options, one agreement — B1 Speaking Part 3 shape

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The rules before the task, all three: One, discuss ALL six jobs. Two, reject at least two out loud with «The problem with … is …». Three, you may only agree at the end.
  2. Kagan Timed Pair Share with a twist: one partner holds the phrase sheet and ticks every phrase used by BOTH. Three minutes, timed visibly.
  3. Swap roles and repeat with a new partner — second time round the phrase count always doubles, and saying so beforehand makes it happen.
  4. Feedback on process, not content: Whose sheet has more than eight ticks? Read me the disagreement you used. Two or three pairs perform their last thirty seconds for the class.
  5. You listen for the four columns and log silently — the delayed feedback lands in the review lesson.
📺 On screen: the four phrase columns in chalk plus the tick sheets — board and paper are the media, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: discuss, disagree, tick, decide — twice, with two partners. Every student both speaks and audits, so nobody drifts

66–76′
Follow-up questions
pairs → class
Why: B1 Speaking Part 4 shape: the personal questions that always follow the picture task. Short answers are refused — every opinion needs a reason and a reaction to the partner.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5: the personal questions that always follow — B1 Speaking Part 4 shape

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three questions on the board: Would you like a weekend job? Which job would you choose, and why? Should students have weekend jobs at all?
  2. New partners, four minutes. Two rules: every answer has a because, and every answer reacts to what the partner just said (That's true, but…).
  3. Open the third question to the whole class for three minutes — it is the one with two real sides, and it produces the best English of the day.
  4. One correction moment, chosen in advance: if I'm not agree appears, fix it publicly and kindly. It is the single most common phrase in the speaking exam.
📺 On screen: the three questions in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: answer, react, argue. Expect the third question to split the room along parental lines — which is exactly the discussion the exam wants

Advice clinic
groups of 3–4
Why: The TB's own cooler run as a full stage: real teenage problems drawn from a hat, advice given with If I were you… — the block's grammar and today's functional language in the same sentence.

WHAT WE DO: the TB cooler as a full stage: real problems, advice with If I were you

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Problem slips folded in a hat, written by you in advance: A friend borrowed money and hasn't paid it back. · You didn't do your homework and the lesson is next. · You want a weekend job and your parents said no. · You have to choose between two clubs at the same time.
  2. Groups draw one slip. Two minutes: each member gives ONE piece of advice, and it must start with a frame from the board — If I were you, I'd… · What about…? · I think … might be a good idea.
  3. Groups swap slips twice, so every group handles three problems.
  4. Best advice per problem read aloud and voted on. Two lessons in one sentence: yesterday's grammar inside today's phrases. That's what the exam calls fluency.
📺 On screen: problem slips in a hat and the frames in chalk — paper and board are the media, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: draw, advise, swap, vote. Expect the borrowed-money problem to produce the most heated advice — and the most accurate conditionals

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can form person nouns with four suffixes and spell them, and can discuss a set of options with a partner and reach an agreement using at least six functional phrases.
  • Subsidiary. Listening to two conversations for true/false detail (track 87); noticing functional language in a recorded model (track 88); giving advice with If I were you.
  • Personal. Open with 2 minutes of delayed feedback from lesson 4's log. Keep the functional phrases visible in four groups (suggest · agree · disagree · decide) for the whole second half — and insist that the disagreeing column gets used, or the task cannot reach three minutes.
By the end, students can…
  • build a person noun from a verb or a noun and spell it right
  • suggest an option and give a reason
  • disagree politely and say why
  • reach an agreement out loud: yes, that's a good choice

Structure

Form
-er / -or on verbs and nouns (runner with a doubled n), -ist on many nouns (guitarist, novelist), -ian mainly after -ic and -y words (music → musician, politics → politician); all four are unstressed.
Pron. watch
architect /ˈɑːkɪtekt/ with a /k/, never «arhitekt» · lawyer /ˈlɔːjə/ · vet is one short syllable · musician /mjuːˈzɪʃn/ and politician /pɒləˈtɪʃn/ both end /ʃn/ · -er and -or are the SAME weak /ə/ · comedian /kəˈmiːdiən/ takes four syllables and the stress on ME
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«run» → «runer» or «runner»? → runner. One short vowel, one consonant — the consonant doubles.
«music» → musicer? musicist? → musician. The -ic words take -ian, and the c changes its sound to /ʃ/.
«I'm not agree» — what's wrong? → agree is a verb, not an adjective. I don't agree. Say it twice.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students agree immediately and the task ends in 40 secondsThe rule is stated before the task: you may not agree until every option has been discussed, and at least two must be rejected out loud with The problem with … is … . Time the three minutes visibly.
The suffix is chosen by ear (and the ear is useless here)Say it honestly once: -er and -or are the same sound. Then the wall carries the words in four colour groups, and the spelling showdown at the end of the relay is where it is actually learned.
True/false answers guessed rather than heardTwo plays and a rule: after the first play, mark only what you are SURE of; in the second play, hunt for the two you left. Feedback asks for the words that decided it.
Quiet students disappear in a free discussionRoles rotate: in each round, one partner holds the phrase sheet and must tick every phrase used. The tick-holder cannot hide, and the role swaps every round.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
