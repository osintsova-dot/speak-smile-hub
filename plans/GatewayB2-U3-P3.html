🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Answer five three-option questions on a podcast where every wrong option contains words that were actually said, and use the future continuous, the future perfect simple and the future perfect continuous — what will be in progress, what will be finished, and how long it will have been going on.
What is genuinely hard
The listening trap is lexical: the distractors repeat the recording's own words, so a candidate who matches words instead of meaning chooses wrongly with complete confidence. The grammar is hard for a different reason — Russian has nothing that corresponds to «by 2030 I'll have finished», so students avoid the form entirely and say «I will finish», which is not the same sentence.
Typical error to expect
«By 2030 I will finish university» — «by» + a future point needs the future perfect: I will have finished. The Russian paper likes this sentence because Russian has no equivalent form at all.
«This time next year I will work in Moscow» for an action in progress at a future moment — English needs the future continuous: I'll be working.
Why the lesson is built this way
The listening comes first and it is chosen for its content: Elon Musk, reusable rockets, a colony on Mars and a tube that would take you from Los Angeles to San Francisco in thirty-five minutes. Sixteen-year-olds argue about this without being asked to. The three new tenses then come out of sentences from that same recording, which is why the discovery exercise on page 38 looks the way it does. The practice moves from a prediction text (2040) to Mark's actual morning commute (a two-hour journey with four kinds of transport) to the students' own life at thirty — which is the stage they will remember.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Thirty-five minutes from Los Angeles to San Francisco, in a tube, at 670 kilometres an hour. The idea is eleven years old and there is already a prototype. Today: listening where every wrong answer contains the right words — and the tense for what you will already have finished by 2035.”

Why: One demonstrated trap is worth ten warnings; replaying the exact ten seconds is what converts it into a habit.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.38 ex.2 (track 13): three options each, twice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Before the first play, name the trap: Every wrong option in this task contains words you will actually hear. Do not choose by words. Choose by meaning.
  2. Give ninety seconds of silent reading of the five questions and fifteen options — this is the stage the marks live in.
  3. Play track 13 straight through. Then thirty seconds in pairs, comparing only.
  4. Play again: Second time, check the two options you rejected.
  5. Check with Numbered Heads Together. Key: 1 c · 2 a · 3 b · 4 c · 5 b.
  6. Prove the trap on question 1: option (a) mentions the Iron Man film, and the film IS mentioned in the recording — but Musk said he will be making an electric jet, not that he got the idea from the film. Replay those ten seconds.
📺 On screen: track 13, played twice, plus a ten-second replay of question 1. Backup: read the script from the Teacher's Book p.126 in two voices, twice.
👦 Students do: read fifteen options first, listen twice, then answer for the team

WHAT WE DO: SB p.38 ex.3 (track 13 again): why does the speaker mention each of these?

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

47–58′
Three future forms
desks
Why: Meaning before form, and the examples are sentences they have already heard for content.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.38 ex.1a–1b: the tenses named, the rules built, the columns filled

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Warmer on the board: Tonight at nine o'clock, I'll be having dinner. — What tense, and what does it mean? Then: guess what the person next to you will be doing at ten tonight, and write it down.
  2. Ex.1a: five sentences from the recording — name the tense and the form. Key: 1 and 2 future continuous (will + be + -ing) · 3 and 4 future perfect simple (will + have + participle) · 5 future perfect continuous (will + have been + -ing).
  3. Ex.1b: match the four rules. Key: a future perfect simple · b future continuous · c future perfect continuous · d both perfects, with «by».
  4. Now fill the three columns on the board — students dictate, you write.
  5. Drill the contraction chorally, five times: I'll have finished /aɪləv/ · I'll have been working /aɪləv bɪn/.
📺 On screen: the three columns filling up as they answer. Backup: none needed — board and voice.
👦 Students do: name three tenses, match four rules, dictate the columns, repeat the contractions

Why: Converting a fixed timetable into future forms removes all the guesswork about meaning, so the attention goes entirely to the form.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.39 ex.3a–3d: a real two-hour journey turned into nine future sentences

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ex.3a: read Mark's morning. Check comprehension in three questions — how long is the commute (just over two hours), how many kinds of transport (four: bike, train, underground, on foot), how does he feel on arrival (exhausted).
  2. Thirty seconds in pairs: Would you accept that journey every morning? Why?
  3. Ex.3b, six minutes alone: nine sentences about tomorrow in the future continuous or future perfect. Key: 1 'll have got up · 2 'll be cycling · 3 'll have been cycling · 4 'll be getting on · 5 'll have had · 6 'll be travelling · 7 won't have arrived · 8 'll be walking · 9 'll have been travelling.
  4. Ex.3c–3d in pairs: write four questions about Mark's tomorrow and ask them WITHOUT looking at the book. The answer must use the same tense as the question.
  5. Fast finishers: find three places in the text where the present simple talks about the future, and say why.
📺 On screen: nothing new — the text is on p.39. Backup: not applicable.
👦 Students do: read a timetable, produce nine future sentences, then ask and answer four questions from memory

Structure
Companies will be developing electric planes in the next few years. · By 2030, the journey will have gone down to just half an hour. · By 2030 they'll have been working on the Hyperloop for over fifteen years. · By the time I'm thirty, I'll have left university.
Form
future continuous = will + be + -ing (in progress at a future moment) · future perfect simple = will + have + past participle (finished before a future moment) · future perfect continuous = will + have been + -ing (how long it will have been going on) · all three usually with by or by the time + present simple.
Pron. watch
word stress in the long unit words: photograph vs photographic · similarity · difference · between · whereas · the contracted future perfect is three sounds, not five: I'll have /aɪləv/ · I'll have been /aɪləv bɪn/
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«At nine tomorrow I'll be flying.» Will the flight start at nine? → no, it will already be happening.
«By 2030 the journey will have gone down to half an hour.» Is it finished before 2030 or after? → before.
«They'll have been working on it for fifteen years.» Which word tells you it is the continuous? → for fifteen years — the duration.
ICQ before the listening: if you hear the same words as in option A, is A right? → not necessarily. Usually the opposite.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
