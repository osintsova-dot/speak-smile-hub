🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use five weather nouns and six weather verbs (earthquake, flood, lightning, snowstorm, tornado · blew, fell down, flowed, poured, rose, shook) to describe what happened, and talk about the past with the past simple and with used to for repeated actions and past states.
What is genuinely hard
The vocabulary splits into nouns and irregular verbs and the verbs arrive only in their past forms — blew, rose, shook — so the infinitive has to be recovered, not assumed. The grammar breaks in three known places: didn't USED to, be used to confused with used to, and the extra syllable in created and needed that students do not hear and therefore do not write.
Typical error to expect
«I didn't used to like pizza», «I was used to play football» — after didn't the form is USE to, and used to never takes be. The d disappears in speech, which is exactly why it survives in writing.
«createded», «lifted» said as one syllable — a verb ending in /t/ or /d/ adds a whole extra syllable in the past: created /kriˈeɪtɪd/, needed, lifted. Students who do not hear it do not write it.
Why the lesson is built this way
The words arrive through four photographs of real events and four people who lived through them, and the book itself splits the box into nouns and verbs — that split is the lesson, so it goes on the board first. The reading is the hardest text of the block and the exam task on it is a multiple-choice cloze, which is a vocabulary task wearing a reading costume: the class is taught to look at what the word must PARTNER with, not what it means. Grammar last and in two halves, past simple then used to, with the pronunciation of -ed treated as a spelling issue because that is what it becomes on paper.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“One fire in Australia grew so powerful it made its own tornado, five hundred metres wide. Today: the words English uses when the weather stops being weather — and how to say what things USED to be like before.”

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that drills the irregular past forms physically

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. You call an infinitive (blow, rise, shake, fall down, pour, flow); the class answers with the past form in one voice and does the gesture — wind, water rising, ground shaking.
  2. Then reverse it: you call the past form, they answer with the infinitive. Faster each round.
  3. Last round, in pairs: A says the past form, B makes a whole sentence with it. Swap after four.
  4. Freeze and sit: Page twenty-five. Australia, and a fire that made its own tornado.
📺 On screen: the verb column on the wall behind them, deliberately visible — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone says every form aloud with a gesture, then produces sentences in pairs

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4 = B1 Reading Part 5: the example dissected, then six items

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 first, and do it properly — this is where the method is taught. The example (0) has four options and only one partners with higher. Say the question that decides every cloze item and write it on the board: What must this word go WITH?
  2. Then the three sentences that use the rejected options (B–D) — they teach the differences between sum, figure and total in context, in ninety seconds, better than any explanation.
  3. ex.4 individually, six minutes, clock visible. Weaker students get the Teacher's Book support: one option per item crossed out in advance (1 A, 2 B, 3 C, 4 C, 5 B, 6 A).

  5. ex.5 (three comprehension questions) in pairs, three minutes, to prove the text was understood and not just gapped.

📓 Interactive notebook — 10_U4_Grammar_Past_Simple_vs_Used_To_A5
A5 grammar card — PAST SIMPLE VS USED TO: the past simple for a completed event or an event at a particular time (The fire destroyed many homes last year.) against used to for a repeated past habit or a past state that is not true now (We used to spend every August with our grandparents.), with all three forms (used to + base verb · didn't use to + base verb · Did + subject + use to…?), the EXAM TRAP did/didn't + USE TO, never USED TO (Did you used to…? ✘) and the decision strip one finished event → past simple, habit or state that is different now → used to
  • Glue the card in and box the exam trap — the d disappears after did.
  • Walk the decision strip aloud before writing each sentence.
  • Write three sentences about your childhood, two with used to.
  • Remember: used to always looks back from now.

Why: 🎯 Speaking out · when I was seven

WHAT WE DO: used to used for real, in a Find Someone Who mingle

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Each student writes four true sentences about their childhood, two with used to and two with didn't use to: I used to be afraid of thunder. I didn't use to like winter. Four minutes, silent.
  2. Check the forms on paper while they write — this is the cheap moment to catch didn't used to.
  3. Then the mingle, six minutes (Kagan Find Someone Who): find someone whose childhood matches one of your sentences, write their name next to it. Rule: You may not write a name until BOTH of you have said the whole sentence.
  4. Sit. Report in the third person, which is where the form pays twice: Artyom and I both used to be afraid of thunder. Nobody here used to like getting up early.
  5. Log two or three errors silently; they open lesson 4.
📺 On screen: the four sentence frames on the board for slower writers — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail

Structure
The wind blew so hard that the power went off. · The river rose by two metres. · My family used to live just outside Canberra. · Forest fires didn't use to be as common as they are now. · Did people use to keep warm without electricity?
Form
past simple for single completed actions and for a particular time in the past · used to + infinitive for repeated past actions and for past states that are no longer true; after didn't and Did the form is USE to.
Pron. watch
the irregular past forms are a listening problem before they are a writing problem: blew /bluː/ against blow /bləʊ/, rose /rəʊz/, shook /ʃʊk/ · and the extra syllable rule that decides half the past-tense marks: created /kriˈeɪtɪd/, needed, lifted, succeeded — three syllables, not two
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I used to play football.» Do I play now? → no. That is the whole meaning of used to — it is over.
«He didn't used to like pizza» — what is wrong? → the d. After didn't the form is use to. You cannot hear it, which is why you must remember it.
«created» — how many syllables? → three. The base ends in a /t/, so the ending is a whole extra syllable: /kriˈeɪtɪd/.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students learn tornado and forget shookThe wall has two columns from minute one, and the verb column carries BOTH forms (shake / shook). Any answer that gives one form is asked for the other.
The cloze is answered by meaning aloneTeach the partner question and put it on the board: What must this word go WITH? The example (0) does the teaching — number is right because it partners with higher, not because it means the most sensible thing.
«didn't used to» survives everythingSay the honest reason aloud — you cannot hear the difference — and then make it visual: write didn't use to on the board and circle the missing d. Test it twice more before the end of the lesson.
Fast finishers in the readingThe Teacher's Book task: for each cloze item, write why the other three options are impossible. Dictionaries allowed. Their notes get used in the whole-class check.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 19–25 (grammar in a text — the present tenses are the most frequent single item) and tasks 26–31.

Present simple against present continuous is the exam's quiet workhorse: it appears in the grammar cloze every year, and the deciding factor is always a time expression (at the moment, every day, next month) or a state verb. Both are taught today as signals to look for rather than rules to remember.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
