🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Ask and answer twelve present perfect questions about real life experiences, and put the right grammar word into a gap in a short text.
What is genuinely hard
The gapped text asks for the words nobody notices — on, than, few, me. Students look for a noun or an adjective because those are the words that carry meaning, and the answer is never one of those. And the twelve questions need the third form of the verb at speed, in speech, with no time to think.
Typical error to expect
Have you ever saw a whale? · I have went there — the past simple form used where the participle is needed [high priority, A2/B1]
swimmed, breaked, eated, flied, growed — the regular ‑ed ending put on an irregular verb. In the exam a misspelt form scores nothing [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The third lesson of the unit is where the grammar gets used rather than learnt, so almost all of it is skills work. It opens with five true/false statements about the world, which revise yesterday's tense without an exercise and start an argument in English before anything is read. The gapped text comes early, while attention is fresh, because it is the hardest thinking of the lesson and because the strategy has to be taught before it is used: the class decides what KIND of word is missing before anyone writes anything. The twelve quiz questions are then invented by the students BEFORE the book is opened — six of their own first, so that the book's twelve arrive as confirmation rather than as instruction. The participle stage is deliberately short: this is the third time the twelve verbs have appeared, and by now it is a check, not a lesson. The listening gives the tense a real owner — an explorer who got lost in the Brazilian rainforest — and the productive stage is the unit's own task: their Life quiz, asked round the whole room.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Twelve questions about your whole life — and then you write your own and ask everybody in this room. Before that, one small exam task: six gaps in an email, and every missing word is a tiny one. Not a noun. Not an adjective. The little words nobody looks at.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, the third form recalled and one present perfect sentence from everybody

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. Two questions on the board: What comes after have? (the third form) · Swim — third form? (swum).
  3. Then the switch, on mini-whiteboards: Thirty seconds — write one thing you have never done. Reveal on Boards up! and read three of them out.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page seventy-eight. Twelve questions about your whole life. And six gaps where every answer is a word of two or three letters.
📺 On screen: the two questions written on the board before the lesson — chalk only, nothing to fail and no backup needed
👦 Students do: answer, then write one I have never… sentence. Expect two or three past simple forms to appear — note them, do not stop. Typical problem: they write only never been — ask for a different verb

Lesson frame

Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim and the one question that unlocks the reading task

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write on the board a sentence with a gap: I found this quiz ______ the internet.
  2. Ask for suggestions, take three, then say the sentence that frames the lesson: Nobody said «fossils» or «beautiful». Why not? Because this kind of gap never wants a big word. It wants a tiny one.
  3. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: six tiny words in an email, and then twelve questions about a whole life.

8–16′
Lead-in · true or false
pairs → class
Why: Five present perfect statements about the world, three of them surprising. It revises yesterday's grammar without an exercise and it makes them argue in English before they read anything.

WHAT WE DO: five present perfect statements about the world, argued about before anything is read

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.153, for the third lesson of the unit, and it is the best warmer of the unit because it is the grammar in disguise — five sentences on the board to be judged true or false: It has never snowed in Australia (false — it often snows in the mountains) · No one has ever travelled to Mars (true — only unmanned spacecraft have) · A teenager has never climbed Mount Everest (false — Jordan Romero was the first, at 13) · Nobody has ever walked from London to Paris (true — there is a sea between them) · An animal has never learned English (true — apes have learned some words).
  2. Dictate them or write them up, then give three minutes in pairs. Phones allowed for this one — the book says so, and the checking is half the fun.
  3. Check as a class and let the two surprising ones (Everest at thirteen, apes and English) have their thirty seconds.
  4. One question to close it: Which of these five sentences could YOU say about yourself?
📺 On screen: the five statements on screen (Backup: dictate them — five sentences takes forty seconds, and dictation is better here anyway because they have to hear the tense)
👦 Students do: argue in pairs, then check. Expect the Everest one to split the room. Typical problem: the argument happens in Russian — one English sentence per opinion is the minimum and it is enforced from the first pair

16–30′
Reading · one word in the gap
class → solo → pairs
Why: The one decision that unlocks this task — what KIND of word is missing — is taught before any gap is filled, and the book's own example is worked through out loud.

WHAT WE DO: Marco's email on p.78: what kind of word is missing, then the six gaps

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. SB p.78: an email from Marco to Nina about a life quiz he has found on the internet — twelve questions about people's different life experiences.
  2. TB p.153, before the gaps: «Who found this quiz?» (Marco) · «What is the quiz about?» (people's different life experiences) · and about the example: «Why is the answer a?» (there needs to be an article before the noun look).
  3. Read out what the task actually is and the tip that governs it. A short text — an email or a message — with six gaps. You write one word in each gap, and the missing words are grammar words: prepositions, pronouns, determiners. think of ONE word that completes it grammatically — not a noun, not an adjective;
  4. The first gap is worked through together (TB p.153): «What grammar word do we need before a noun?» (a determiner or a preposition) — «Is there an article here?» (yes, the) — «So what preposition do we need before the internet?» (on).
  5. Now the categories on the board, because they are the strategy in four words: preposition · pronoun · determiner · linking word. Every answer will be one of these.
  6. ex.1 — the remaining gaps, alone, 6 minutes, then compared with a partner. Key: 1 on · 2 and · 3 few · 4 than · 5 about · 6 me (TB p.153)
  7. Kagan Numbered Heads Together for the check: the student called says the answer AND which category it belongs to.
  8. Where marks go: two words written where one was asked for, and a content word — a noun or an adjective — written where a grammar word was needed. Both are avoidable and neither has anything to do with vocabulary.
  9. If a group finishes early: TB p.153: fast finishers write a short reply from Nina to Marco; then you take one of the texts, put gaps in it on the board and let the rest of the class complete it with the same strategy. The book even prints the example — Hi Marco, Thanks a lot ___ (for) the email! This quiz is ___ (a) great idea and I will do it tomorrow. See ___ (you) soon! Nina
📺 On screen: the email on screen with the gaps visible so the demonstration can be pointed at (Backup: it is printed on SB p.78 — hold the book up for the demonstration and write the four categories on the board, which is where they need to be anyway)
👦 Students do: watch the demonstration, then fill the gaps alone. Expect gap 3 (few) and gap 4 (than) to be the two that stay empty. Typical error: two words written into gap 5 — one word only, and the rule is on the board

Life quiz · twelve questions
pairs → class
Why: Twelve present perfect questions matched to twelve photographs, then asked for real. The matching is comprehension; the asking is the reason the comprehension was worth doing.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.78: invent six questions first, then match the book's twelve to the photos

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.153: books closed first, pairs invent six questions that could be in a life quiz — they must be in the present perfect, and you give two examples to start them off: «Have you ever walked on a frozen lake or river?» · «Have you ever stayed in a terrible hotel?» Only then open the book and match the photos to the twelve questions.
  2. Books open. Describe two of the photos as a class first — thirty seconds each — then match the twelve questions to the twelve pictures in pairs, 5 minutes. Key: 1 E · 2 F · 3 H · 4 L · 5 B · 6 J · 7 C · 8 A · 9 K · 10 I · 11 G · 12 D
  3. TB p.153, and this is the pronunciation that makes them sound fluent: drill all twelve questions and point out the linking in you ever — /juːwevə/. Then they ask one another and compare answers, with follow-up questions demonstrated first: Teacher: Fernando, have you ever swum with sharks? — Student: No, I haven't. Have you? — Teacher: No, I haven't and I don't want to because I'm afraid of them!
  4. Play track 103 if there is time — the reading text is recorded, and hearing the twelve questions said properly is worth a minute.
  5. Books closed: How many of the twelve can you remember? In pairs, one minute, then open and check.
📺 On screen: track 103 and the twelve photos on screen (Backup with no sound: the twelve questions are printed on SB p.78 and you read them aloud for the drill yourself — for a drill a live voice is better, because you can slow the linking down)
👦 Students do: invent six, match twelve, then drill. Expect their own six to overlap with the book's in two or three places, which is a good moment to point out. Typical error in their own questions: Have you ever go…

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 p.79: the twelve irregular verbs checked in all three forms

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

Match the first one together as an example, tell them these are all irregular, and drill all three forms. Key: be / was / been (6) · break / broke / broken (2) · eat / ate / eaten (5) · fly / flew / flown (11) · grow / grew / grown (9) · have / had / had (4) · make / made / made (7) · meet / met / met (3) · ride / rode / ridden (10) · send / sent / sent (12) · sleep / slept / slept (8) · swim / swam / swum (1)

Listening · the explorer
solo → pairs
Why: A long interview cut into nine questions, with a real person who has done the things on the list. Detail listening with a payoff: she got lost in the rainforest.

WHAT WE DO: the interview with Christina on p.79 (track 104): three places, then nine questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.154, two questions before you play anything: «How many different countries have you been to?» and «What do you call somebody who likes to go to places other people haven't visited?» (an explorer). Then: «Which places would you like to explore?»
  2. TB p.154: the Tour d'Afrique from the north to the south of Africa is one of the longest cycle races in the world. It runs from January to May every year and both professional and amateur cyclists take part. The start is always in Cairo and the finish always in Cape Town, but the exact distance and the number of countries change each year. A typical race covers around 12,000 kilometres and goes through ten countries, divided into about a hundred stages of 40 to 200 kilometres a day, with rest days in between.
  3. ex.1 — one job only on the first play: which three places does she talk about? Key: Brazil / the Brazilian rainforest · the North Pole · Africa
  4. ex.2 — the nine questions. Play track 104 again and pause it after every second answer. Key: 1 got lost on any of your travels? Yes, in the Brazilian rainforests. · 2 She was looking for insects. · 3 That she got lost in the rainforest. · 4 wanted to come home early from a trip? No, she hasn't. · 5 She was skiing. · 6 She wrote in a diary. · 7 She will be with other people, not alone. · 8 She will cycle / ride a bike. · 9 It'll take four months.
  5. Kagan RallyCoach for the check: A gives an answer, B coaches, swap every two.
  6. TB p.154: write the nine answers on the board in a different order — weaker students then match answers to questions instead of producing them from nothing.
  7. Two questions to close, and they are the reason the stage is here: Which of her trips sounds the most interesting? What would she have to do to get ready for it?

🎯 Speaking out · your own Life quiz
pairs → fours → class
Why: The productive outcome: eight questions of their own asked round the whole room, then reported back. Everybody speaks, everybody listens, and the tense is unavoidable.

WHAT WE DO: the Speaking task on p.79: eight questions of their own, asked round the whole room

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. SB p.79: in pairs, write down eight life experiences, some positive and some negative — learn to ride a bike, fail a test. Choose six and turn them into present perfect questions. Pairs combine into groups of four and choose the best eight questions; every student writes all eight down. Then, on a timer, everybody goes round the room asking different people. Back in the groups, report what you found, and finally tell the whole class using the phrases in the book's box.
  2. Give the shape on the board so nobody drifts: 8 experiences → 6 questions → in fours, choose 8 → everybody writes all 8 → ask 8 different people.
  3. 5 minutes to build the questions. Circulate and fix only the participles — the content is theirs.
  4. 6 minutes standing, asking. One rule: a different person for every question, and you write down the name and the answer.
  5. Back in the fours, 4 minutes: report what you found, in the third person. Ivan has never eaten sushi but he has ridden a horse.
  6. Kagan Showdown for the class round-up: each group writes its most surprising finding on a mini-whiteboard and everybody reveals at once.
  7. Log the errors silently and close with three of them on the board, no names, thirty seconds to repair.
📺 On screen: the five-step shape on the board (Backup: the shape is dictated and the questions are written in notebooks — this stage needs no technology at all)
👦 Students do: build eight questions, ask eight people, report back. Expect the reporting stage to be the hardest part, because it needs has and they have been saying have for twenty minutes. Typical error: Ivan have never…

Structure
Have you ever ridden a camel? · Have you ever eaten something you didn't like? · No, I haven't. Have you? · Yes, I have — I did it last summer.
Form
have / has + past participle in questions and short answers: Yes, I have · No, I haven't · Yes, he has · No, she hasn't.
Pron. watch
the linking in you ever — /juːwevə/. Without it the question sounds like two separate words and takes twice as long to say.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«What grammar word do we need before a noun?» → a determiner or a preposition. That question, asked before the gap is filled, is the whole strategy.
«Is fossils a possible answer for a gap in this task?» → no. It is a noun, and this task never asks for content words.
«Have you ever saw a whale?» — correct it. → seen. Third column, every time.
«Yes.» — is that an answer? → not yet. Yes, I have — and then one more thing.

Assumptions: SB p.78–79, syllabus lesson 42. The present perfect was taught yesterday with a whole lesson to itself, so the form is fresh and fragile: it works in writing and collapses at speed. The twelve participles have been met twice. The gapped-text format is new, though the class has done a different open cloze in block 3.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
They write a noun or an adjective into a gapOne question and no explanation: Is that a grammar word? Ask it every time and it stops happening by the fourth gap.
At speed the participle turns back into the past simpleThis is normal one day after the lesson and it is not a discipline problem. Repeat the question back correctly and let them repeat it. Log it; the block test has eight participles in it.
The quiz turns into a list of yeses and noesThe extra-information rule from yesterday is restated once and then enforced by the reporting stage: if you cannot say one extra thing about your partner, you were not listening.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
