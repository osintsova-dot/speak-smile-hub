🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Read three film descriptions for detail, hear five details in a phone call, and write an invitation that a friend could actually act on.
What is genuinely hard
Both halves of the lesson punish the same habit — answering from the first thing you notice. In the reading, one word in the question (a member of her family — one person) decides the answer. In the listening, all three options are said aloud every single time.
Typical error to expect
A word from the option was said in the recording → they tick it. A matching word is not a correct answer; meaning decides [exam report]
I will call you when I will have time — the future tense after when and if, where English uses the present [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The lesson opens standing up, with a mingle in which every question is a relative clause — yesterday's grammar revised without an exercise and the class talking before they sit down. The reading is detail work and it is front-loaded while attention is fresh; the seven questions are checked against the text, not against memory. The conjunctions come after the move break because they are exactly what the writing stage will need twenty minutes later — this is the difference between four short sentences and two real ones. The listening is the productive listening outcome of the unit and it is deliberately placed before the writing: the invitation they hear on the phone is the model for the invitation they write. And the writing runs the book's four beats — GET READY, PLAN, WRITE, IMPROVE — with the check reduced to one question: could somebody actually turn up?
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Today you invite a friend to the cinema — and the test is simple. If they read your message and still do not know where to be and when, the message failed, however good the English is. Before that: three films, and a phone call where all three answers are said out loud every time.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, the relative pronoun recalled in use and one written sentence from everybody

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. Two questions on the board: Person or thing — which word goes with a person? (who) · How many syllables in chocolate? (two).

  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page seventy. Three films, and by the end of the lesson you invite somebody to one of them.

👦 Students do: answer, then finish the sentence. Expect every board to have a relative clause on it, which is the point. Typical problem: films what — say nothing, just point at the crossed-out word still on the board

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim and the single test the writing has to pass

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write on the board: Come to the cinema! and next to it a big question mark.
  2. Would you go? You do not know which cinema, which film, or when. A message that nobody can act on is not an invitation, however good the English is.
  3. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: three films read for detail, a phone call heard for details, and an invitation somebody could actually act on.
  4. Set the bar: Your partner reads your invitation and tells me where to be and when. If they cannot, it goes back.
📺 On screen: a slide with the four things an invitation needs — where · when · what film · why (Backup: the same four words in chalk on the board, where they stay until 84′ and are pointed at during the writing stage)
👦 Students do: read and react. Expect somebody to say they would just ask — which is the perfect opening for «and if they are not online?»

Why: A stand-up mingle in which every question they ask is a relative clause. It revises yesterday's grammar without a single exercise and it gets them talking before they sit down to read.

WHAT WE DO: a stand-up mingle in which every question is a relative clause

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.137, a stand-up mingle and the best warmer of the unit: on the board, Find somebody who … has a birthday soon · can count to ten in more than three languages · has an interesting pet · went to bed late · needs a new phone · saw a film yesterday. Ten minutes to find as many answers as possible by asking each other, then they share. Every sentence they say is a relative clause.
  2. Model the first question yourself, standing, so the form is heard before it is used: Find somebody who saw a film yesterday. — Did you see a film yesterday?
  3. 8 minutes standing. Names written next to the prompts, nothing else.
  4. Round-up in one minute: Who found somebody who saw a film yesterday? What was it?
📺 On screen: the six prompts on screen (Backup: dictate the six prompts and let them write them down — thirty seconds of dictation, and the stage runs identically)
👦 Students do: stand, ask, write names. Expect the room to be loud and every sentence to contain who. Typical problem: they ask in Russian — the English question is written on the board as a model and stays there

Reading · three films
solo → pairs
Why: Three film descriptions and seven questions — detail reading where the trap is a single word in the question. Reading every word of the question is a skill, not a warning.

WHAT WE DO: the cinema web page on p.70: what kind of film each is, then the seven questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.134, before the cinema-of-the-future text: bean bag (a soft seat consisting of a large cloth bag filled with dried beans) and hot tub (a large, usually wooden container full of hot water in which more than one person can sit). TB p.137, before the three films: come after (try to get), forever (for all time in the future), swap (to give something to someone and get something from them in return) — and a note on game, set, match: a game and a set are parts of the scoring system of tennis, and the umpire says «game, set, match» when there is a winner; the phrase is also used more generally to mean that someone has won.
  2. Set the gist task BEFORE they read: ex.1 — Three minutes. What kind of film is each one? Key: A thriller/action · B comedy · C drama
  3. Three comprehension questions before the detail work (TB p.137): Where does Emily's adventure begin? (in a castle) · How can Chris and Hannah get their bodies back? (they have to learn to understand each other better) · What is Carly training for? (a tennis competition).
  4. TB p.137, the sentence that explains the whole answer key: read every word of the question. Question 7 says a member of her family — one person — which is exactly why text C is not the answer, because it says Carly is not close to her parents, and that is two.
  5. ex.2 — the seven questions, alone, 7 minutes, then checked with a partner. Key: 1 A · 2 C · 3 B · 4 C · 5 A · 6 C · 7 B
  6. Kagan Numbered Heads Together for the class check: answer plus the proving line, every time.
  7. If there is time: TB p.137 extension, and it is worth the four minutes: give two imaginary lines from the films and let them guess which film each came from — «One day you will be a champion!» (Game, Set, Match) · «I'm not going to the cinema with him!» (Body Swap). Then groups invent six lines for other groups to guess.
📺 On screen: the three film descriptions on screen so the underlining can be shown (Backup: all three are printed on SB p.70 — hold the book up for the demonstration and put the key words of question 7 on the board)
👦 Students do: read for gist, then answer the seven. Expect question 7 to split the class exactly as the Teacher's Book predicts. Typical error: choosing C for question 7 because the word family feels close enough

WHAT WE DO: the Talking points questions on p.70 — and the ending of each film invented

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.137 Talking points: «How do you think these films finish?» Get happy endings and sad endings for the same film. Then pairs discuss the advantages and disadvantages of watching a film at home and at the cinema — at home you can watch at any time on your laptop; at the cinema there is a big screen.
  2. Do the endings first, because they are fun and they warm the group up: How do you think these films finish? One happy ending and one sad ending for the same film.

  4. Collect two columns on the board and leave them up — the writing stage will borrow from them.

Vocabulary 2 · conjunctions
class → pairs
Why: Nine small words that decide whether two ideas become one sentence or stay two. This is the difference between A2 writing and A1 writing, and it costs ten minutes.

WHAT WE DO: nine conjunctions: ex.1–2 p.71

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. Write on the board: Is it more interesting to watch a film or read a book? Point at or and name it — a conjunction, a word that joins sentences or parts of a sentence.
  2. Compare it with Is it better to watch a film? Is it better to read a book? on the board underneath. Two sentences against one — that is the whole reason conjunctions exist.
  3. Elicit the ones they already know (but, and) with an example sentence each.
  4. Books open: find the conjunctions in the web page on p.70, then match the seven halves in ex.1. Key: 1 b · 2 c · 3 e · 4 a · 5 d · 6 g · 7 f
  5. ex.2 — complete the sentences and compare in groups, 3 minutes.
  6. One line of grammar that saves marks for years, said once and written on the board: I will call you when I will have time — the future tense after when and if, where English uses the present [high priority, A2/B1]
📺 On screen: the two versions of the sentence on the board (Backup: none needed — this stage is chalk and the book)
👦 Students do: find, match, complete. Expect while and where to be the two that need an example. Typical error: when I will come — the board line catches it

🎯 Listening out · the birthday invitation
solo → pairs
Why: The productive listening outcome: a phone invitation with five details in it, where all three options are mentioned every time. Waiting for the end of the exchange is the point of the stage.

WHAT WE DO: the phone call on p.71 (track 94): five three-option questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.138, before you play anything: in groups, «What is a great way to celebrate a birthday?» — give them a couple of starters (go to a café, visit the circus), collect the ideas and let the class vote for the best one.
  2. SB p.71, track 94: Finley is celebrating his birthday at the Star Cinema and phones his friend Ana to invite her. Five three-option multiple-choice questions. The recording is played TWICE.
  3. Read out the tip and put it on the board in six words. The tip printed on the page, and it is the trap of the task: all three options are usually mentioned in the recording, so a word you heard is not an answer. And they will hear it twice — a missed answer on the first play is not a disaster.
  4. Give them one minute to read the five questions before anything is played, and tell them to underline the question word in each.
  5. Play track 94 once. They answer individually. Confirm nothing.
  6. Pairs compare, then play track 94 again. Ask who changed an answer, which one and why. Key: 1 B · 2 C · 3 C · 4 C · 5 A
  7. TB p.138, for students who understand a text on paper but not in speech: take phrases from the audioscript and show on the board what happens to them in connected speech — it'll have to be Saturday (the /h/ in have disappears, to becomes /tə/) · Are we going to see (Are reduces to /ə/, going to becomes /gɒnə/) · Mum will come and get us in the car (come an(d) links, the /d/ is not pronounced; get us in links too).
📺 On screen: track 94 in the whiteboard app (Backup with no sound: the full audioscript is on TB p.297 — read the phone call aloud yourself in two voices, twice, at natural speed. The task works identically)
👦 Students do: read the questions, listen twice, compare. Expect at least two answers to change between the plays, which is exactly what you want them to notice. Typical error: answering question 4 with the first price they hear

Writing · an invitation to the cinema
solo → pairs
Why: Writing built on a model, a set of prepositions and a plan, started and finished in class — and checked against one question only: could somebody actually turn up?

WHAT WE DO: Prepare to write on p.71: GET READY → PLAN → WRITE → IMPROVE

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. GET READY, books closed (TB p.138): What is the best cinema where you live? How do you get there? How much do tickets cost? Books open: the poster and Leo's email, three questions. Key: 1 Leo · 2 Ghostbusters · 3 On Saturday at 6.45
  2. Then the prepositions, matched to their function. Key: a at · b on · c on · d at · e to — and drill it by calling out days, times and places for them to supply the preposition: Monday (on) · three o'clock (at).
  3. PLAN, books closed first (TB p.138): «When you are inviting someone to see a film, what do you need to think about?» Brainstorm it, then open the book and compare with the bullet points. Only then do they plan their own.
  4. WRITE: remind them of the prepositions and tell them to use the grammar and vocabulary of this unit — a relative pronoun and a conjunction each. 8 minutes of silent writing, timer visible.
  5. IMPROVE: underline every relative pronoun, conjunction and preposition in your own invitation, read it once more, then swap and check your partner's, looking only at the underlined words.
  6. Kagan RallyCoach for the swap: the partner's only job is to say out loud where to be and when. If they cannot, the invitation goes back for one more minute.
  7. Read out the model at the very end, not before: Model answer, TB p.138: Hi Sasha, We're going to Worldclass Cinema tomorrow afternoon at 3 o'clock. Would you like to come with us? We want to see a new film, Old Times. It's about two old people who want to build a time machine which can take them back to their school days. It sounds cool! We're meeting at Claudio's flat at 14.30 and we're going to walk there. I hope you can come! Marina
  8. Log the errors on your correction sheet; the clinic is at the start of the next lesson — which is the review lesson, so it lands exactly where it is needed.
📺 On screen: the poster and Leo's email on screen while they plan (Backup: both are printed on SB p.71 and the four-word frame from 5–8′ is still on the board — this stage needs no technology at all)
👦 Students do: read the model, plan, write, then check a partner's. Expect the four-word frame to be what saves the weakest three invitations. Typical error: the film is named and the time is not — the partner check catches it in ten seconds

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can answer detail questions on three short film texts and write an invitation with the time, the place and the film in it.
  • Subsidiary. Nine conjunctions (SB p.70); listening to a phone call for five specific details (SB p.71, track 94); prepositions of time and place in an invitation.
  • Personal. In the listening stage, do not confirm anything on the first play. The second play exists to check, and if you confirm early they will never learn to use it.
By the end, students can…
  • find the detail in a short text that answers a question exactly
  • use and, but, or, so, that, if, when, while, where to join ideas
  • hear the difference between what is mentioned and what is true
  • write an invitation with the time, the place, the film and a reason in it

Structure
I usually cry when I watch sad films. · Mum falls asleep while she's watching TV. · I can get the tickets if you give me some money first. · We visited the places where they filmed it.
Form
conjunctions join two clauses into one sentence. ⚠️ after when and if English uses the PRESENT for a future meaning: I'll call you when I have time, never when I will have.
Pron. watch
the weak forms inside connected speech: it'll have to be Saturday (the /h/ disappears, to = /tə/) · are we going to see (going to = /gɒnə/) · come an(d) get us in (the /d/ vanishes and the words link).
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
The question says a member of her family. The text says Carly is not close to her parents. Does that answer it? → no. A member is one person, parents is two. One word in the question decided it.
«I'll phone you when I will arrive» — what's wrong? → after when, English uses the present: when I arrive. Russian uses the future here, so this one has to be said out loud.
The recording said £5 AND £3. Which one is the answer? → the one after but: «it'll be £5, but it's only £3 until then».
«Come to the cinema» — is that an invitation? → not yet. Where, when, which film, and why they should come. Four things, or nobody turns up.

Assumptions: SB p.70–71, syllabus lesson 37. The relative pronouns of lesson 3 are one day old and will be recycled in the mingle without comment. The conjunctions are known one at a time and never as a set; while and where are the two that are genuinely new. Prepositions of time have been met every year and are still wrong half the time.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
They answer the reading questions from memory of the film, not the textThe rule from lesson 2 stands and is stated once: no answer without the line. If they cannot find the line, the answer is wrong even if it happens to be right.
They tick an option because they heard the wordSay it before the recording, not after: all three are mentioned, every time. Then after the check, ask which words in the recording proved the answer — that is the whole strategy.
The invitation has no time or no place in itOne check and no other: swap papers and try to turn up. If you cannot say where to be and when, hand it back.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
