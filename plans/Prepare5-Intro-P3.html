🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Retrieve and repair the four structures that carry Prepare 4 into Prepare 5 — present perfect, conditionals, the passive and reported speech — use each one in speech rather than in gaps, and take a twelve-minute diagnostic slice of this year's first four units.
What is genuinely hard
All four structures are known and all four are fragile in exactly the same way: the form survives, the choice does not. Students can build a present perfect and still put yesterday next to it; they can build a first conditional and still put will in the if-half.
Typical error to expect
«I have seen this film yesterday» — a finished time expression kills the present perfect. Russian has one past, so the two English pasts have to be chosen between consciously, every time.
«If it will rain, we will stay at home» — the if-half stays in the present. This is the single most repeated conditional error of Russian learners and it survives to B2 unless it is named.
Why the lesson is built this way
Four structures in one lesson is only possible if none of them is re-taught — so each gets a short, sharp, production-first treatment: an auction where teams buy sentences they believe are correct (which forces analysis without a single explanation from you), a Find Someone Who mingle on the present perfect, a passive relay on real objects in the room, and a reported-speech chain that cannot be done without the tense shift. The diagnostic slice at the end is about THIS year, not last year, and it is what turns the term plan from a guess into a decision.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Four structures today, and none of them is new. Two of them break in the same place every single year — and I'm going to let you find where. At the end, twelve minutes that tell me how fast we can go this term.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, placement papers back with private comments, and the shape of the day

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what's the best thing about today so far? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — the placement papers, given back quietly. Hand each paper back face down with one written comment, not a score read aloud. Say the class-level sentence only: Most of you are exactly where this book expects you to be. Two things came up that we're fixing today.
  3. Sixty seconds in pairs: find ONE question you got wrong and can now explain. Take two explanations aloud.
  4. Then name the two structures the papers exposed, and put them on the board — they will be two of today's four.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): set the day up: Four structures today. All of them yours already. Two of them break in the same place every year, and you're going to find where.
📺 On screen: the two exposed structures written on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: look at their own paper with a job rather than a mark, and hear the class result without hearing anyone's score

8–24′
Grammar auction
teams of four
Why: Grammar auction

WHAT WE DO: sixteen sentences, half of them broken, bought with fake money

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Teams of four, each with 1000 pounds of imaginary money. Sixteen sentences on a handout: four per structure, and roughly half are wrong.
  2. Rules, thirty seconds: You buy sentences you believe are CORRECT. Maximum 200 per sentence. A correct sentence doubles your money; a broken one loses it.
  3. Bids are WRITTEN and revealed together (Kagan Showdown) — no shouting, and every team commits before hearing anyone else.
  4. After each sentence, the team that bought it must say WHY it is correct — or, if it is broken, the room repairs it and the team explains what happened.
  5. The four planted errors are the ones that matter: I have seen this film yesterday · If it will rain, we'll stay in · The bridge was build in 1900 · He said me he was late. Let a team buy each one. An error you paid for is an error you remember.
  6. Write each repaired sentence under its heading on the board. By the end of the stage, all four headings have content — produced by them, not by you.
📺 On screen: the sixteen sentences on a handout and the four headings filling up on the board — printed in advance, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: teams analyse sixteen sentences, commit money to their judgement, and repair the four classic errors in public
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE tasks 19–25 (grammar in a text). Judging whether a sentence is correct IS the grammar-cloze skill, one step earlier.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: after the auction, put a six-gap mini-text on the board (tenses, one passive, one conditional) and give five minutes in pairs. It replaces the second half of the passive relay if time is short.

24–36′
Present perfect · find someone who
standing, whole class
Why: Present perfect · find someone who

WHAT WE DO: twelve real questions asked and answered across the room

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand out the grid: twelve lines, each one Find someone who has… — been abroad, never eaten sushi, already done today's homework, known their best friend since primary school, just changed their phone, lived in the same flat all their life.
  2. Rule before they stand: You may not write a name until you have asked the FULL question. Have you ever…? Have you already…?
  3. Six minutes of mingling, fast. Circulate and listen for the two errors: a finished time next to a present perfect, and for used with a point in time.
  4. Sit. Report in the third person — Kagan style, one fact each: Nobody in this room has been to Japan. Dima has known his best friend since he was five.
  5. Two repairs on the board, taken from what you heard, and the reason named each time.
📺 On screen: the twelve-line grid in their hands — printed in advance, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone asks about twelve questions and answers about twelve, standing and moving. The structure is produced dozens of times without a single gap-fill

36–40′
Move break · real or imaginary
standing, two walls
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that separates the two conditionals physically

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two walls: REAL — it might happen and IMAGINARY — it probably won't.
  2. Read out eight if-sentences, one at a time. Everyone walks to the wall they believe: If it rains tomorrow… · If I won a million… · If we finish early… · If I were the headteacher…
  3. After each one, one student on the correct wall says the second half of the sentence.
  4. Speed up. The last two come with no thinking time.
  5. Freeze and sit: That's the whole difference. Present plus will, or past plus would. Nothing else.
📺 On screen: two A4 signs on opposite walls — printed in advance, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone moves eight times and produces four second halves. The distinction is made physical before it is ever explained
two A4 signs
40–50′
Passive relay + reported-speech chain
teams → circle
Why: Passive relay + reported-speech chain

WHAT WE DO: two short, sharp production rounds, five minutes each

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Passive relay, five minutes. Point at real objects in the room and around the town: this board · your phone · that building · this book · the school. Teams write one passive sentence per object: This book was printed in Spain. My phone was made in China.
  2. One point per correct sentence, one extra for a by-phrase. Check on the board, fast, and name the two failure points: the missing BE and the infinitive used instead of the third form.
  3. Reported-speech chain, five minutes. Whole class in a circle. You whisper a sentence to the first student (I'm tired and I'll go home early). They report it to the next: She said she was tired and she would go home early.
  4. Each student reports what the PREVIOUS person said, so the tense shift happens fifteen times in five minutes.
  5. When it breaks — and it will — stop the chain, repair it as a class, and restart from that student. The breaks are the lesson.
📺 On screen: the five objects named aloud and the chain running round the circle — nothing to fail
👦 Students do: every team writes five passive sentences and every student performs the tense shift at least once, in front of everybody, with the repair done collectively
board · a circle

The diagnostic slice
individual, silent
Why: The diagnostic slice

WHAT WE DO: twelve minutes of this year's language, twenty items

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Frame it before the papers go out: the Prepare 5 Diagnostic Test — 100 four-option questions, five per unit of THIS year's book (1–5 Unit 1 · 6–10 Unit 2 · 11–15 Unit 3 · 16–20 Unit 4, and so on).
  2. today's slice is questions 1–20, about twelve minutes: the language of Units 1–4, which the group meets in blocks 1 and 2. It measures what is ALREADY there, not what has been forgotten. And the honest sentence that changes how it feels: This is next term's language, not last year's. You have not been taught it yet. I want to know what you already have.
  3. Rules: silence, no dictionaries, blanks are welcome.
  4. Twelve minutes, clock on the board, and you sit down.
  5. Collect face down. Do not discuss the answers: You'll see these again — I'll tell you in December how much you already knew in September.
📺 On screen: the clock and the finish time on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: work silently for twelve minutes on this year's language, knowing exactly what it is for

🎯 Speaking out · the carousel
two circles, standing
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · the carousel

WHAT WE DO: all four structures used in real conversation, four partners each

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Inside-Outside Circle, four rounds of two minutes, one structure per round, prompts on the board:
  2. Round 1 (present perfect): What's the most interesting place you've ever been to? How long have you known your best friend? · Round 2 (conditionals): What will you do if the weather is bad on Saturday? What would you do if you could take a year off school?
  3. Round 3 (passive): Tell your partner about something in your home — where was it made, when was it bought, who was it given by? · Round 4 (reported speech): Tell your partner what somebody said to you yesterday. Then report what YOUR last partner said.
  4. The outside circle moves one place between rounds. Round 4 is the hardest and it is placed last on purpose — by then they have listened to three partners and have something to report.
  5. Monitor with a pen: two excellent sentences written word for word, three errors written without names.
  6. Sit. Read the two excellent sentences aloud, then repair the three errors as a class in ninety seconds.
📺 On screen: the four rounds of prompts on the board while they rotate — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: every student produces all four structures in conversation with four different partners. Eight minutes of continuous speaking each

Structure
Have you ever been to London? · I've lived here since 2019. · If it rains, we'll stay in. · If I had more time, I'd learn the guitar. · This bridge was built in 1900. · She said she was tired. · He told me to wait.
Form
present perfect (have + third form) against past simple with finished time · first conditional (if + present, will) and second (if + past, would) · passive (be + third form) · reported speech with the tense moved back one step.
Pron. watch
the contracted forms are what make these structures sound natural and they are the first thing to disappear after a summer: I've, we'd, it's been. Drill them chorally, twice, at the auction stage
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I have seen this film yesterday.» What kills the present perfect here? → yesterday. A finished time takes the past simple, always.
«If it will rain, we will stay at home.» How many wills can a first conditional have? → one, and it lives in the second half.
«He said me that he was late.» Which verb takes a person? → tell. Say does not.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Four structures in ninety minutes becomes four lecturesNothing is explained today unless the class has first produced or judged it. The auction does the analysis, the mingle does the production, and you speak for less than eight minutes in total.
The auction turns into shoutingTeams write their bids on paper and reveal together (Kagan Showdown). No bid is accepted aloud. The reveal is what makes it quiet and fast.
The conditional error survives everythingPlant it in the auction on purpose, let a team buy it, and then have the class repair it in public. An error a team paid for is an error they remember.
The diagnostic feels like a punishment at the end of a good lessonIt is framed as planning, not testing, and it is genuinely short: twelve minutes, twenty items, of THIS year's language. Say what it changes: This tells me whether block one takes two weeks or three.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
