🏠HomeworkWorkbook p.72–73 — the opinion essay, 140–190 words
🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Write a complete opinion essay of 140–190 words in five paragraphs — introduction, three points each with reasons and examples, conclusion — on the title The world is a better place to live in today than it was a hundred years ago. Do you agree?, using two distinct classes of linker correctly: those that join two halves of one sentence (although, even though, whereas, while, since, so that, and so, despite the fact that) and those that introduce a new sentence (however, furthermore, in addition, as a result, consequently, on the other hand, in conclusion, to sum up).
What is genuinely hard
One thing above all: students use linkers as decoration rather than as grammar. However the country is far away, we must help and Although, we must help it are the two errors this lesson exists to kill, and they are grammatical, not stylistic — however cannot join two clauses and although cannot stand alone with a comma. Second difficulty: the essay wants three points with reasons and examples, and most students produce three assertions and stop.
Typical error to expect
«However the weather is bad, we will go» — however introduces a NEW sentence and is followed by a comma; the joining word is although or even though. Write both correct versions side by side and leave them on the board all lesson.
«Despite of the rain», «despite it was raining» — despite takes a noun or an -ing form, or despite the fact that + clause. Never despite of.
«In my opinion, I think that …» — one opinion marker per sentence, not two.
Why the lesson is built this way
The essay gets finished in the lesson. That single decision drives everything: the model is analysed fast (twelve minutes), the language is harvested from it rather than presented (ten), the linker grammar gets its own explicit block because it is the actual difficulty (twelve), planning is done jointly and on the board (seven), and then there are twenty-two uninterrupted minutes of silent writing with the teacher circulating. A homework essay is written at midnight by a tired student with a translation engine; an essay written here is written by the student, and you see it happen.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
Here is a sentence: «Even though the country is a long way away, we must help it.» And here is another: «The country is a long way away. However, we must help it.» Same meaning, and if you swap the two words round, both sentences are broken. That is not style — that is grammar, and it is where most of the marks in an essay are lost. Today you get the model, you get the linkers, and then you write the whole thing here, with me in the room. Nothing goes home half-finished.

WHAT WE DO: greeting at the door, then one statistic interpreted with the reporting passive

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — the last time the weather changed your plans. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — rewind, yesterday's data language, aimed at today's argument. On the board: The number of natural disasters recorded in the US rose from 157 in 1900–1919 to 8,067 in 2000–2019.
  3. RallyRobin, two minutes: One sentence each — what does that figure suggest, and what could explain it? Push for the reporting passive from lesson 3 as well: It is believed that …
  4. Take two aloud. Somebody will say better reporting and somebody will say climate change; both are the argument the essay needs.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): You have just started an argument. Today you finish one, on paper, in five paragraphs. Page 105.
📺 On screen: the figure written on the board. Backup: dictate it.
👦 Students do: interpret one statistic in pairs using the reporting passive, then two read aloud.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.105 exercise 1 — the model's task read and populated before the model is read

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Look at the photograph first (Teacher's Book warmer): teenagers in matching T-shirts collecting rubbish by a river. Where are they? What season is it? Why are they all wearing the same colour? Have you ever done anything like this? Two minutes, and it costs nothing to let them answer honestly.
  2. Read the writing task on the page aloud: How can we help to save the planet? with notes 1 recycling · 2 saving energy · 3 your own idea.
  3. Make the two demands of exercise 1 explicit, because students see one and miss the other: You need a THIRD idea of your own, and you need TWO practical examples for each of the three. Not one. Two.
  4. Pairs, four minutes, notes only. Circulate and push the vague: «Save water» — how? Give me the actual thing a person does on a Tuesday.
  5. Take two third ideas from the room and write them up — usually transport, food, or buying less.
📺 On screen: the task and the three note headings written on the board. Backup: the task is printed in their own book.
👦 Students do: describe a photograph, then brainstorm a third point and two examples for each of three points, in pairs.

13–25′
The model · what each paragraph is FOR
desks → pairs
Why: Genre knowledge is what B2 writing is actually short of. Naming the repeated internal shape of the three middle paragraphs converts a 190-word task into three small identical tasks.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.105 exercises 2 and 3 — structure before language

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 2: read the model essay, three minutes. One question only — Which of YOUR points from exercise 1 does the writer include? Hands up for each of the three.
  2. Ask the honest question straight afterwards: Do you agree with this writer? Why? Take two answers. An essay read as an argument is read better than an essay read as a specimen.
  3. Exercise 3, the structural analysis, and this is where the lesson turns. Pairs, four minutes: What is the PURPOSE of each of the five paragraphs?
  4. Answers: the five paragraphs of the model: 1 introduce the topic · 2 first reason with supporting arguments · 3 second reason with supporting arguments · 4 third reason with supporting arguments · 5 your opinion and conclusion.
  5. Now make them count, because it is the difference between a good essay and an average one: Look at paragraph two. How many sentences? What does the first one do? What do the other two do? The pattern — point, reason, example — is identical in paragraphs 2, 3 and 4. Write it on the board in three words and leave it up.
  6. Then the sentence that saves ten essays: Your three middle paragraphs are all the same shape. Once you have the shape, you are writing three of the same thing, not three different things.
📺 On screen: the paragraph purposes and the three-word pattern (point → reason → example) written on the board and left up. Backup: the model is in their own book.
👦 Students do: read the model against their own ideas, then identify the purpose and internal shape of five paragraphs in pairs.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.105 exercise 4 — the useful language taken out of the model rather than handed over

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 4: the underlined words and expressions in the model sorted into five lists — expressing opinions · adding ideas · putting ideas in order · contrasting ideas · concluding. Pairs, five minutes.
  2. Answers: expressing opinions: In my opinion, I believe that · adding ideas: What is more, Furthermore · putting ideas in order: The first point to make is that, The next thing to bear in mind is that · contrasting ideas: However, even though, Although · concluding: In conclusion. To bear in mind = to consider and remember.
  3. Elicit the meaning of the one nobody knows: to bear in mind = to consider and remember. Drill The next thing to bear in mind is that … chorally twice; it is the most useful phrase on the page and it is unsayable at first sight.
  4. Add the warning about doubling up, on the board: In my opinion, I believe that we should … crossed out. One opinion phrase per sentence. Choose one.
  5. Have everybody copy the five lists into the back of their notebook under a heading they will find again — OPINION ESSAY: MY TOOLBOX. This is the page they will use in Unit 10 and in the exam.
📺 On screen: the five function headings written on the board and filled in as the class gives them. Backup: no display needed.
👦 Students do: sort ten expressions from the model into five functions, then copy the toolbox into their notebooks.

Stir · the linker walk
open floor
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies. It also produces a silent, honest diagnostic of exactly which linkers this group cannot categorise, which is what the next twelve minutes then fixes.

WHAT WE DO: four minutes on their feet that sets up the grammar block that follows

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two signs at opposite ends of the room: JOINS ONE SENTENCE and STARTS A NEW SENTENCE.
  2. Read linkers out one at a time — however · although · furthermore · even though · as a result · whereas · in addition · while · therefore · since · to sum up · so that — and the class walks to the end they believe it belongs to. Five seconds each, no discussion.
  3. Do not confirm anything yet. Note which ones split the room; almost always while, since and whereas.
  4. Sit them down with the question the next stage answers: Three of those split the room. In ten minutes you will know exactly which end each one goes to and why.
📺 On screen: the two paper signs. Backup: shout the two categories instead of posting them.
👦 Students do: walk to one end of the room for each of twelve linkers, without discussion.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.105 exercises 5 and 6 — the difficulty of the lesson, taught head-on

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 5: look at how although, even though and however are used in the model, then read the Writing bank.
  2. Put the two shapes on the board and leave them up for the rest of the lesson, written out in full: Even though the country is a long way away, we must help it. / We must help the country even though it is a long way away. versus The country is a long way away. However, we must help it.
  3. Say the two rules in one line each: A joining linker connects two halves of ONE sentence and there is no full stop before it. An introducing linker starts a NEW sentence and takes a comma after it.
  4. The comma rule, from the Teacher's Book, because it is markable: Although my dad has a bike, he almost always uses his car (comma, because the clause comes first) versus My dad almost always uses his car although he has a bike (no comma).
  5. Exercise 6: the twenty-two linkers in the box sorted into J (join) and I (introduce). Pairs, five minutes. Answers: J (join two halves of one sentence): and so, despite (the fact that), even though, since, so that, whereas, while. I (introduce a new sentence): all things considered, as a result, as far as I'm concerned, consequently, firstly/secondly/lastly, furthermore, in addition, in conclusion, meanwhile, moreover, nevertheless, on the one/other hand, therefore, to sum up, what's more.
  6. Then the Teacher's Book's second sort, which is worth two minutes because it is how they will actually search for a word while writing: five function headings on the board — CONTRASTING · CONCLUDING · RESULTS · ORDER · ADDING — and the class assigns the linkers to them. Not all are used.
  7. Extra activity, ninety seconds (Teacher's Book): on the board, The planet is getting hotter. Some people deny that this is happening. Pairs write three different combinations — with although at the front, with although in the middle, and with however. Take all three and write them up.
📺 On screen: the two structural shapes and the comma rule written on the board and left up for the writing stage. Backup: dictate them; the stage is board-and-book only.
👦 Students do: sort twenty-two linkers into two grammatical classes and five functions, then join the same two ideas three different ways.

Joint planning · the real task, on the board

Why: A shared skeleton removes the blank-page problem without removing the thinking, and forbidding full sentences at the planning stage is what stops the essay being written twice.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.105 exercise 7a — the plan built together so that nobody starts from a blank page

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the actual essay title aloud, twice: «The world is a better place to live in today than it was a hundred years ago.» Do you agree? Notes: 1 health · 2 technology · 3 your own idea.
  2. Sixty seconds of hands: Who agrees? Who disagrees? Who is somewhere in the middle? All three positions are writable and say so — You are not marked on your opinion. You are marked on whether you support it.
  3. Build the skeleton on the board together, three minutes, taking ideas from the room: P1 introduce the topic and state your position · P2 health (point, reason, example) · P3 technology (point, reason, example) · P4 your own idea (point, reason, example) · P5 restate and conclude.
  4. Third ideas that work, if the room is stuck: education, travel, rights and equality, food, or — the interesting one — the counter-argument that the environment is worse.
  5. Then three minutes of silent, individual note-making on the actual plan. Notes only — say it explicitly: No full sentences yet. If you write sentences now, you will write the essay twice.
  6. Last instruction before the pens move, from the Teacher's Book, written on the board: Five paragraphs. Different kinds of linker. And your conclusion must follow from what you actually wrote.
📺 On screen: the five-paragraph skeleton built on the board and left up throughout the writing stage. Backup: no display dependency — the board is the tool.
👦 Students do: take a position, contribute to a shared skeleton, then make their own notes in silence.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.105 exercise 7b — the whole essay produced in class, with the teacher in the room

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Say the conditions once and then stop talking: Twenty-two minutes. Silent. A hundred and forty to a hundred and ninety words. Five paragraphs. Board stays up — use it. If you get stuck, put a wavy line and keep going; do not stop.
  2. Visible timer. Announce the halfway point once and the last five minutes once. Nothing else.
  3. Circulate with a pencil. Two rules for yourself: do not read over shoulders at the start (it freezes people) and do not correct grammar in the first ten minutes — only unstick people who have stopped. From minute ten, mark a light dot in the margin next to a line with an error rather than correcting it; the student finds it.
  4. At minute fifteen, one whole-class reminder that always pays: Check you have a linker at the start of paragraphs three and four. And check your conclusion says the same thing as your introduction.
  5. At twenty-two minutes: Pens down. Count your words and write the number at the bottom. The word count is a genre requirement in both exams and it takes twenty seconds.
📺 On screen: a visible timer on the desk and the five-paragraph skeleton still on the board. Backup: if there is no timer, write the finishing time on the board and call the halfway point aloud.
👦 Students do: write a complete 140–190-word essay in silence, using the board, and count the words at the end.

Why: Peer checking against a short explicit list teaches the criteria far better than receiving a marked essay does, and one sentence of advice is more useful to both students than twenty red corrections.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.105 exercise 7c with the checklist on page 141, then the exam link

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Peer check, three minutes. Swap essays. The reader checks five things only, written on the board: (1) five paragraphs? (2) three points each with a reason AND an example? (3) at least four different linkers, and are the joining ones joining and the introducing ones introducing? (4) does the conclusion follow from the three points? (5) is the word count between 140 and 190?
  2. One rule for the reader: You may write ONE sentence of advice at the bottom. Not corrections — advice.
  3. Send them to the Writing checklist on page 141 for anything they are unsure about; it is the book's own version of the same list and they should know it exists.
  4. EGE parallel, two minutes. Task 38 of the Russian paper is two hundred to two hundred and fifty words in six fixed paragraphs. Task 37 is a hundred to a hundred and forty. Different lengths, same competence — and the criterion that costs Russian candidates the most marks is not language, it is organisation: no paragraphs, or paragraphs with no linkers. What you did today is that criterion.
  5. Collect the essays. Mark them against the four criteria (content, communicative achievement, organisation, language), not with a number.
📺 On screen: the five checking points written on the board. Backup: dictate them.
👦 Students do: check a partner's essay against five explicit points and write one sentence of advice.

Workbook p.72–73 — the opinion essay, 140–190 words
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will have written a complete 140–190-word opinion essay in five paragraphs, using joining and introducing linkers with the correct punctuation and clause structure.
  • Subsidiary. Analysing the purpose and internal shape of paragraphs in a model text; harvesting functional language from a model; peer-checking against explicit criteria.
  • Personal. Do not talk during the twenty-two minutes of writing. Not once, except for the two timed reminders. Silence is the resource the stage runs on.
By the end, students can…
  • plan an opinion essay in five paragraphs before writing a word
  • build a middle paragraph as point, reason and example
  • join two clauses with although / even though / whereas and punctuate it
  • start a new sentence with However / Furthermore / As a result and punctuate it
  • write 140–190 words to a title in twenty-two minutes and count them

Structure
In my opinion, all of us can and should do something about this. · The first point to make is that … · The next thing to bear in mind is that … · Even though the country is a long way away, we must help it. · The country is a long way away. However, we must help it. · In conclusion, small actions can have a major impact.
Form
joining linkers (although, even though, whereas, while, since, so that, and so, despite the fact that) connect two clauses inside one sentence, with a comma only when the subordinate clause comes first · introducing linkers (however, furthermore, in addition, moreover, nevertheless, as a result, consequently, therefore, on the other hand, in conclusion, to sum up) begin a new sentence and are followed by a comma · despite and in spite of take a noun or an -ing form, never a clause and never despite of · one opinion marker per sentence.
Pron. watch
linkers carry the shape of the argument in the voice, not just on the page. However, and Furthermore, take a small pause after them — the comma is audible. Although is /ɔːlˈðəʊ/ with the stress on the second syllable and a voiced ð that Russian speakers turn into /z/. Read the model essay aloud once yourself and let them hear where the joins are
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
although vs however: «Although it is far, we must help» — how many sentences? — One. And «It is far. However, we must help» — how many? — Two. Can I say «However it is far, we must help»? — No.
despite: What comes after «despite» — a full sentence or a noun? — A noun or an -ing. Can I say «despite of»? — Never.
the middle paragraph: How many sentences at least? — Three. What does the first one do? — Makes the point. And the other two? — Give the reason and the example.
ICQ before the writing: How many minutes? How many paragraphs? How many words? What do you do if you get stuck — stop and think, or leave a wavy line and keep going?

Assumptions: Students have written an opinion essay once before, in Unit 3 (opinion essay 1), and know the basic five-paragraph shape. They will have a handful of linkers in active use — but, also, so, because — and will use however and although interchangeably. Nobody will supply an example without being required to.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Somebody writes about the model's topic instead of the real one.It happens every year, because both are on the same page. Say the title twice, write it on the board, and have one student read it back to the class before the planning stage.
The essay is not finished in twenty-two minutes.Most will finish; some will not. Anyone still writing at the bell hands in what they have and finishes at home in a different colour, so you can see which part was written where. Do not extend the stage — the peer check is where the criteria are learned.
Three points with no examples.The point-reason-example pattern goes on the board in stage 3 and stays there, and it is checking point 2 in the peer check. If a whole class still misses it, take one paragraph from a volunteer, project or read it, and add the example together in thirty seconds.
Linkers are used decoratively — four in one paragraph, none in the next.Checking point 3 asks whether the joining ones join and the introducing ones introduce, which catches decoration. Name the target out loud before writing: four to six linkers in the whole essay, spread out, not clustered.
A student refuses to take a position («both are true»).That is a writable position and the strongest essays often take it — but it has to be stated in paragraph one and concluded in paragraph five. Give them the frame: While it is true that …, I would argue that on balance …
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 38 (the opinion essay) and task 37 (the personal email).

The essay written today is 140–190 words with a five-paragraph plan, three points and a conclusion. The Russian task 38 is 200–250 words with a fixed six-point plan and a data table, and task 37 is 100–140 words with three questions answered and three asked. Different lengths, one competence: paragraphing on purpose and signposting with linkers.
