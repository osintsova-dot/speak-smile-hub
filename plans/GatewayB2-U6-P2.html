🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Build and spell compound nouns (homework, timetable, headteacher, blackboard, break time, school bus) and write a story of about a hundred words that begins with a given sentence, uses three past tenses, and carries adverbs and adjectives that make it worth reading.
What is genuinely hard
Compound nouns are a spelling problem with no rule: homework is one word, break time is two, and there is nothing to reason from. The story is a planning problem — teenagers write a beginning and a middle and run out of words before the end — plus a tense problem, because a story that uses only the past simple reads like a police report.
Typical error to expect
«home work», «time table», «head teacher» — half the compound nouns in this unit are written as one word and the test spells them letter by letter. It is not a grammar point, it is a mark.
The story that never ends. A hundred words disappear fast; the last sentence has to be planned before the first one is written, or the story stops rather than finishes.
Why the lesson is built this way
The compound nouns are done fast and physically because they are memory, not understanding — a two-minute combination game (BUS · WORK · HOME · HOUSE · JOURNEY · SCHOOL · LUNCH · BREAK · TIME · TICKET · TRAIN) produces more of them than any list. The writing half is built backwards from the marking criteria: first choose the opening sentence from three (and say why the other two fail), then hunt the three tenses in the model, then the adverbs, and only then write. The exam task is a story that MUST begin with a given sentence, so the first line is free and the plan is what matters — which is exactly the shape of the anxiety this task usually produces.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Your story has to start with a sentence I give you. You don't choose it, you don't change it — you continue it. That sounds like a restriction and it is a gift: the hardest sentence in any story is the first one, and today it is written for you.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a 90-second drill of yesterday's tense, and the bridge into compound nouns

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — how did you get to school today? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — yesterday's timeline, live. Two events on the board: 8.00 the lesson started · 8.10 I arrived. One sentence containing both, please.
  3. Kagan RallyRobin in pairs, 90 seconds: four more pairs of events, alternating, each one made into a past-perfect sentence. Give the pairs on the board (the train left / I got to the station · I did my homework / the teacher asked for it).
  4. Take three answers and check only the participle.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page sixty-six, the right-hand side. Words English decided to glue together.
📺 On screen: yesterday's timeline still on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: produce four past perfects each, in alternating turns

Why: Saying out loud that there is no rule stops the class hunting for one, and moves the effort to where it belongs — memory and spelling.

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and put up the two spellings that decide the section

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write two compounds on the board, correctly: homework · break time. Ask what the difference is. There isn't one — that is the answer, and saying so honestly saves ten minutes of searching for a rule.
  2. Say the shape in 30 seconds: fifteen minutes on compounds, then a model story, then you write your own with a clock running.
  3. Set the bar for the writing now, not later: Three tenses. Two adverbs. And an ending, not a stop.
  4. Say the exam fact plainly: The test spells ten of these words, one space per letter. One word or two is the mark.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: the three patterns, then eight compounds produced and spelled

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1, ninety seconds: three examples — classroom (noun + noun), whiteboard (adjective + noun), bus stop (noun + noun, but written as two words).
  2. ex.2 individually, three minutes: eight compounds from definitions — headteacher, homework, lunchtime, blackboard, textbook, timetable, school bus, break time.
  3. Check with Kagan Showdown, and the reveal is WRITTEN: everyone holds up their spelling. Any word that half the room splits goes on the wall in the correct form.
  4. Clap the stress on four of them: HEADteacher, TIMEtable, BLACKboard, SCHOOL bus. The stress is the audible half of the spelling and it is worth ninety seconds.
  5. Write the wall column in the exact test form and leave it up.
📺 On screen: the third wall column, written in the exact form the test demands — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: produce eight compounds alone and reveal the SPELLING simultaneously

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4: eleven words, two minutes, as many compounds as possible

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Eleven words on the board: BUS · WORK · HOME · HOUSE · JOURNEY · SCHOOL · LUNCH · BREAK · TIME · TICKET · TRAIN.
  2. Teams of three, TWO minutes, on paper: as many real compound nouns as you can make. Possible answers: home time, housework, lunch break, train ticket, train time, train journey, school work, schoolhouse, journey time, bus ticket.
  3. Scoring, announced before they start: one point per compound, minus one for an invented one. That penalty is what stops «homejourney».
  4. Check as a class and let teams challenge each other's words — the arguing is the vocabulary work.
  5. Then ex.4, the pairwork on SB p.136, three minutes, as a two-team scoring game exactly as the Teacher's Book suggests.
📺 On screen: the eleven words on the board and the teams' lists read out — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: generate ten compounds in teams under a two-minute clock and defend them against a challenge

Move break · glue it together
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer on the compounds

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. You say the first half of a compound (HEAD… TIME… BLACK… SCHOOL… LUNCH… HOME…) and the class shouts the whole word and then shows it: hands TOGETHER if it is one word, hands APART if it is two.
  2. Ten of them at speed. Anyone who gets the hands wrong spells the word aloud before standing back up.
  3. Last thirty seconds, in pairs: Three compound nouns you can see in this room.
  4. Freeze and sit: Page sixty-seven. A story about an exam, and the first sentence of it is already written.
📺 On screen: no technology — the words are said aloud and shown with hands, so nothing can fail
👦 Students do: everyone produces ten compounds and judges the spelling of each with their hands

Writing · the model story
individual → pairs → class
Why: Writing · the model story

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: choose the opening, then colour-code the three tenses

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1–2: three possible first sentences for Alex's story. Pairs, ninety seconds — which is best, and WHY are the other two worse? Answer: b, «It was the first day back at school after the summer holidays.», because it sets both scene and time in one line.
  2. Read the model story aloud yourself, once, at speed. Then three minutes silent, with three colours (or three kinds of underlining): past simple, past continuous, past perfect.
  3. Check on the board as three lists: was, came, took, didn't mention, carried on, said, handed in, got · was sitting, feeling · had asked, hadn't done, 'd forgotten.
  4. Then the observation that turns it into a writing rule: Three tenses in a hundred words. The past perfect is the one that carries the secret — what had already happened before the story started.
  5. ex.4, thirty seconds: why the adjectives and adverbs? To add interest and drama. A story without them is a report.
📺 On screen: the three tense-lists built on the board from the class's colour-coding — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: judge three opening sentences, then dissect a model into three tenses with three colours
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE task 38, criterion К2 (text organisation). A beginning, a middle and an end with the tenses under control is what К2 rewards, whatever the genre.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: spend three minutes on the model's SHAPE rather than its tenses: where does the beginning end? where does the ending start? Fits inside the colour-coding stage without extending it.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5–6: five sentences given their adverbs, and the Prepare to write box

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

  4. One extra line, added by you and worth more than the other four: Write the LAST sentence before you write the first one.

🎯 The exam story, in real time
individual, silent
Why: 🎯 The exam story, in real time

WHAT WE DO: ex.7–8 = B1 Writing Part 2: about 100 words, beginning with the given sentence

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the given first sentence on the board, in full: I had to set off for school a little earlier than usual that morning. Then the rule: copy it exactly, then continue.
  2. Notice aloud what the sentence already contains — a past perfect and a reason to keep reading. Why earlier than usual? Answer that, and you have a story.
  3. Three minutes, silent: the three-line plan, and the LAST line first. Walk the room and check that every plan has an ending before anybody starts writing.
  4. Fifteen minutes, silent, clock on the board. About 100 words. You sit down and answer no language questions.
  5. Announce the time at minute 10 and minute 13.
  6. Last two minutes: everybody checks their own story against the five-line checklist, with a pencil, one line at a time.
  7. Fast finishers: add two adverbs and two adjectives without adding a sentence.
📺 On screen: the given first sentence and the five-line checklist on the board, plus the running clock — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: plan backwards from the ending, write 100 words alone against a clock, then check against five lines

72–86′
Peer marking against five lines
pairs → class
Why: Peer marking against five lines

WHAT WE DO: the stories swapped and marked on exactly five criteria

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The checklist on the board is the whole mark scheme today: 1 the given sentence, copied exactly · 2 three different past tenses · 3 at least two adverbs · 4 a real ending · 5 about 100 words.
  2. Swap papers. Six minutes. The marker writes the five numbers in the margin with a tick or a cross, and underlines the three tenses in three different ways.
  3. Ninety seconds, spoken, in pairs and in English: one thing that worked and one number that got a cross.
  4. Then the best-endings round, three minutes: read out four or five final sentences only, anonymously, and let the class vote for the one that finishes best. Endings are the weakest part of teenage writing and this is the fastest way to teach them.
  5. Collect the stories. You mark them properly against the same five criteria plus language.
📺 On screen: the five-line checklist on the board during the whole marking stage — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: mark a partner's story on five fixed criteria and vote on the best ending in the room

Structure
It was the first day back at school after the summer holidays. · I was sitting at the back, feeling nervous. · She had asked us to prepare it, and I hadn't done it. · I handed it in quietly and walked out slowly.
Form
compound nouns (noun + noun, adjective + noun) written as one word or two · narrative tenses working together: past simple for events, past continuous for background, past perfect for what happened before · adverbs of manner after the verb.
Pron. watch
compound nouns take the stress on the FIRST part and that is what makes them sound like one word: HEADteacher, TIMEtable, BLACKboard, SCHOOL bus. Drill four of them by clapping — the stress is the audible half of the spelling
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«home work» or «homework»? → one word. And «break time»? → two. There is no rule; it is a list.
«I was sitting at the back when the teacher came in.» Which verb is the background? → was sitting. Which is the event? → came in.
How many words does the story need? → about a hundred. And how many of them are the given first sentence? → about twelve, free.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Compound nouns are split or joined at randomThe wall's third column is written in the EXACT test form, and the check is written, not oral. Any word that appears wrong twice is circled and stays on the board until the test.
The story is all past simpleThe model on the page is colour-coded by the class before they write: three colours, three tenses. A story with only one colour is handed back.
The story stops instead of endingThe plan is three lines and the LAST one is written first. Nobody starts writing until the last line exists — check the plans in the first ninety seconds.
Fast finishers in the writingThe Teacher's Book task: add two adverbs and two adjectives to a story that is already finished, without adding a single new sentence.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 38 (the written piece) and tasks 19–25.

A story is not on the Russian paper, but everything that makes one work is: a controlled chronology, past tenses that do not collapse into one, and adverbs that carry the drama. The compound nouns are quieter but just as useful — homework, timetable, headteacher and break time are the words a candidate needs the moment the topic is school, and half of them are written as one word, which is a spelling mark.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
