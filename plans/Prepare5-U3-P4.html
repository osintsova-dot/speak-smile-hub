🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use the twelve compounds with some, any, every and no for people, things and places, answer seven short three-picture listening questions (B1 Listening Part 1), and discuss options with a partner and agree on one (B1 Speaking Part 3).
What is genuinely hard
some- and any- do not divide the way Russian does: negatives and questions take any-, and no one means the same as not anyone but cannot be used after a negative verb. In the speaking task the difficulty is not language but behaviour — students state an opinion and stop, instead of asking the partner and reaching an agreement, which is what the criteria reward.
Typical error to expect
«I didn't buy something», «Is there something to eat?» — negatives and questions take any-. The Russian equivalent does not change shape, so this survives into B2 unless it is drilled.
«I didn't see nobody» — one negative per sentence, so it is I didn't see anybody or I saw nobody.
Why the lesson is built this way
Vocabulary first and short, taught as a three-by-four grid because the grid IS the rule — people, things, places across the top, the four determiners down the side. The listening is run the exam way with the pictures described before the first play, because in this task the pictures are the question. The speaking half is the point of the lesson: two candidates, a real decision to make, and three behaviours that have to appear — give an opinion, ask for your partner's, agree on one option. The rotation makes everyone do it twice with different partners.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Your sister comes home from hospital tomorrow. Five things you could do for her first day — you have three minutes to agree on ONE. That's an exam task. Today, also: the twelve words English uses for somebody, anybody, everybody and nobody.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, the promised spelling check, and three minutes of repairing the class's own modal errors

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what's the best thing about today so far? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — delayed feedback plus a spelling check. Six health verbs dictated: ache, bleed, blink, breathe, cough, recover. Students write, partners mark against the wall.
  3. Then two sentences on the board to repair, taken from yesterday's house rules: You must to switch off your phone. · You don't must eat late. Sixty seconds in pairs, then the repairs said aloud twice.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page thirty-eight, bottom half. Twelve words in a grid — and then a conversation you have to finish by agreeing.

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and draw the empty grid

Vocabulary · the twelve-word grid
class → individual
Why: Vocabulary · the twelve-word grid

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: two examples from the article, then the whole grid built

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: the two examples come from the article they read last lesson — someone and Nothing. Check them in the text on page 37, which takes ten seconds and makes the point that grammar lives in reading.
  2. ex.2 (EP): fill the grid. Do the first row as a class, then pairs complete it and two students come and write it up.
  3. Add the three rules underneath as the class discovers them: any- in negatives and questions · every- takes a SINGULAR verb (Everyone hates bad news) · no- already contains the negative, so the verb stays positive.
  4. Drill the stress, because the test marks it indirectly through spelling: EVeryone, ANYone, SOMEthing — first syllable, always.
  5. Note the alternative forms out loud: everybody = everyone, somebody = someone. Both are correct and both appear in exams.
📺 On screen: the grid filled in on the board, cell by cell, by the students — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: build the grid together, then copy the completed version. Everyone leaves with all twelve words in one picture

standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer built out of the grid

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. You call out a sentence frame with a gap: I didn't tell ___ . · ___ knows the answer. · There's ___ to eat in this house. · Let's go ___ warm.
  2. Everyone says the completed sentence to the person next to them, then takes one step and finds a new neighbour for the next frame.
  3. Six frames in three minutes. Take one answer aloud after each and correct on the spot.
  4. Freeze and sit: Page thirty-nine. Seven short conversations, three pictures each — and only one of them answers the question.
📺 On screen: the grid on the board behind them, deliberately visible — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone speaks six times in three minutes and hears six different partners

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: the pictures described and the question words underlined

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 in pairs, four minutes: for each of the seven questions, say what the three pictures show. This is not a warm-up — in this exam task the pictures ARE the options.
  2. Collect quickly, question by question, and supply the words the class does not have (a reception desk, a running machine, a towel).
  3. Then underline the question word in all seven items together: Where … left? What's wrong … at the moment? What do they decide to eat? What does the doctor say he should do NOW?
  4. Read the exam information aloud: seven short unrelated extracts, three pictures each, heard twice. And the warning: All three pictures get mentioned. Only one answers the question.

Speaking · the phrases that make a discussion
class → pairs
Why: Speaking · the phrases that make a discussion

WHAT WE DO: the Prepare to speak box, modelled and drilled before the task

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the task on the page aloud: a boy's sister is coming home from hospital, and there are five things they could do for her first day.
  2. Give them ninety seconds of silent thinking time with a pencil — one note per option. The exam gives thinking time too; using it is a skill.
  3. Read the Prepare to speak box and drill the six phrases chorally, with the intonation that makes them sound like a conversation rather than a list: In my opinion… · In my view… · What do you think? · Do you agree?
  4. Model the whole thing for ninety seconds with a strong student, in front of the class, deliberately including all three behaviours. Then ask the class which three things they just saw.
  5. Write the three behaviours on the board as a scoring line: OPINION · ASK · AGREE.
📺 On screen: the six phrases and the three behaviours on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: think silently, drill the phrases, then watch a model and name what made it work

A5 speaking card — SPEAKING PART 3: DISCUSSING OPTIONS on the exam task «a girl is coming home from hospital — what could they do for her first day at home?»: six option pictures (play a board game · listen and dance to music · prepare a special meal · watch a film together · play video games · give her a present), a bank of OPINION PHRASES (I think… · In my opinion… · What do you think?) and COLLABORATION PHRASES (What about…? · That's a good point. · I'm not sure because… · Shall we choose…? · So we agree that…), a model dialogue, a grid to fill in (option · advantage · possible problem) and a SELF-CHECK (discussed all ideas · asked the partner · gave reasons · agreed)

🎯 Speaking out · discussing options
two circles, standing
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · discussing options

WHAT WE DO: the exam task done twice, with different partners and different tasks

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Inside-Outside Circle. Round one: the book's task (the sister coming home from hospital), three minutes per pair.
  2. The listener does not exist here — both partners talk — so the accountability is the report: at the end of three minutes each pair announces its decision to the room in one sentence, and says which option they rejected last and why.
  3. Outside circle moves two places. Round two is a new task, written on the board: Your friend is ill and has missed two weeks of school. Here are five things your class could do to help. Decide which is best.
  4. Three minutes again, then the same one-sentence report.
  5. Monitor with a pen: note two pairs who genuinely negotiate, and three errors without names.
  6. Read the two best closing sentences aloud and name what made them good — they didn't just choose, they said why the other one lost.
📺 On screen: the three behaviours and the second task on the board while they talk — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: every student does the exam task twice, with two different partners and two different problems. That is six minutes of real negotiation each
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE tasks 39–40 (the spoken part). Giving an opinion, asking your partner and reaching an agreement is the behaviour the oral criteria reward.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: after round two, run one three-minute pair task in the Russian format (a photo described, then a choice justified) and record two pairs on a phone. The recording is worth more than any feedback you give live.

Structure
I think someone is trying to call you. · I didn't buy anything. · I'd love to live somewhere warm. · Everyone is waiting to hear the news. · There's nothing to eat! · In my opinion… What do you think? Do you agree?
Form
some- in positive sentences and offers · any- in negatives and questions · every- with a singular verb · no- carries the negative itself, so the verb stays positive.
Pron. watch
mustn't /ˈmʌsnt/ — the first t disappears completely · ought to runs together as /ˈɔːtə/ · ache /eɪk/ with a hard k, not «atch» · cough /kɒf/ and yawn /jɔːn/ · and the everyone / anyone pair is stressed on the FIRST syllable, always
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«There isn't anything in the fridge» and «There's nothing in the fridge» — same meaning? → yes. Different grammar: one negative each time, never two.
«Everyone is waiting» — is that one person or many? → many, with a singular verb. English decided this a long time ago and did not consult us.
In the speaking task, is it enough to give your opinion? → no. You have to ask your partner and agree on ONE. That is what is being marked.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students produce «I didn't see nobody»The grid on the board carries a warning line: ONE negative per sentence. Any double negative is repaired by the class in two versions — with any- and with no-.
In the listening they choose the picture they heard mentionedSay the rule before play one, and it is the same rule as block 2: all three get mentioned, only one answers the question. Underline the question word in all seven items before listening.
The speaking task becomes two monologuesThe three behaviours are on the board and they are marked: give an opinion · ask your partner · agree on ONE. The listener at the end reports which of the three actually happened.
A pair agrees in twenty secondsThen they have to justify the rejected options: Why not the flowers? Why not the film? The rejection is where the language is.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 1–9 (short-conversation listening) and the speaking part (the dialogue where two candidates choose together).

Seven short extracts with three pictures each is the shape of the exam's first listening task, and the trap is identical: all three pictures are mentioned. The discussing-options speaking task is even closer to the Russian oral — you have to give an opinion, ask for your partner's, and reach an agreement, which is precisely what the assessment criteria reward.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
