🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Say how long something has been going on with the present perfect + for or since, and ask the question that gets that answer: How long have you …?
What is genuinely hard
One choice, made every single time: for takes an amount of time, since takes the moment it started. Russian covers both with one word, so there is nothing to transfer and the choice has to be conscious. The second difficulty is quieter — the tense itself, because Russian describes an action that is still going on with a present, and English does not.
Typical error to expect
I've lived here since three years / for 2015 — for and since swapped. Russian uses one word for both, so the choice has to be made consciously every single time [high priority, A2/B1]
I live here for five years instead of I've lived here for five years — Russian uses the present for an action that is still going on, English does not [high priority, A2/B1]
How long time do you play the guitar? instead of How long have you played the guitar? — the question that the whole unit is built on [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The grammar arrives inside speech first: three teenagers describe their hobbies and the forms go past the class before anybody is asked to notice them. Then the discovery is deliberately narrow — four dictated sentences, one question, and the rule falls out. Controlled practice is nine items in five minutes rather than three items in fifteen, because a binary choice is fixed by volume and not by explanation. The reference page gets its own slot because How long have you …? lives there and the whole final task runs on it. Pronunciation is placed immediately before the speaking, not after it, so that the weak forms are in their ears at the moment they open their mouths. The lesson ends with them writing about each other, which forces the third person and makes accuracy somebody else's business.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“I've played the guitar FOR two years. I've had this guitar SINCE January. In Russian both of those are the same little word. In English they are two, and choosing the wrong one is the most common mistake at this level in the country. By the end of the lesson you will ask each other five questions and every single one will start the same way.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, thirty seconds of anonymous error repair, and the bridge into the grammar lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — yesterday's error, repaired in public and anonymously. One sentence on the board, collected from yesterday's Talking points: I'm interesting in geocaching.
  3. One word is wrong. Which? Take it, write interested in next to it in a second colour, and leave both up. Thirty seconds, no names, and it is the most reliably repeated error of the year.
  4. Then two fast rounds of yesterday's vocabulary: One activity you do. One activity nobody in this class does.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page eighty-seven. Yesterday you found out WHAT this class does. Today: HOW LONG.

WHAT WE DO: name the one choice of the lesson and make it visible before anything is taught

Why: Guided discovery from sentences they have just heard. There is exactly one decision to make — amount of time or starting point — so the discovery aims at that and nothing else.

WHAT WE DO: the dictation, then ex.1 p.87: two rules completed from six example sentences

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. Dictate the four TB p.170 sentences and let them guess the hobby after each: I've done it for a long time. You need water but you don't get wet. (fishing) · My friend Boris has played it since he was a teenager. It's the only time he can be a king. (chess) · We've only done it for a year but the fridge door is already full of them. (collecting magnets) · My brother has played these for a month and nobody has slept since he started. (the drums)
  2. They have now written four sentences without noticing. Underline the words «for» and «since» and everything after them.
  3. Three questions, in this order (TB p.170): What tense are these sentences? (present perfect) Which word says from WHEN? (since) Does «for» say how long or how much? (how long).
  4. Books open, ex.1 in pairs, 2 minutes: six examples, two rules. Key: 1 for · 2 since.
  5. Draw the timeline on the board and leave it there all lesson: a horizontal line with NOW at the right end, since as an arrow to one dot, for as a bracket under the whole stretch.
  6. Then the CCQs, fast, the whole class answering together: «three years» — for or since? → for. It is an amount of time. «January» — for or since? → since. It is the moment it started.
📺 On screen: the four dictated sentences and the timeline in chalk — the board is the medium here and both stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write four sentences from dictation, guess the hobbies, then complete the rules. Expect the hobby guessing to carry the stage and the rule to fall out in under two minutes. Typical error: they write I have did — the participle list on SB p.167 should be open

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.87: nine sentences, one word in each — for or since

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Do the first two together, out loud, exactly as TB p.170 sets it out: Is number one about a start date? (yes) So we need …? (since) What about number two? (no, it is about how long — for).
  2. Solo, 4 minutes, all nine. Key: 1 since · 2 for · 3 since · 4 for · 5 since · 6 since · 7 for · 8 since · 9 for.
  3. Kagan RallyCoach: the partner may only accept an answer if the reason comes with it — Amount or starting point?
  4. Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Number seven — number threes, boards up!
  5. Then the sorting task from TB p.170 that makes the rule visible: put the since expressions in order from the most distant to the most recent — I was three years old · 2019 · last month · Monday · yesterday.

WHAT WE DO: the reference page: the question form and the sorting table

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Send them to SB p.161 and give the page thirty seconds of orientation: the top half is the rule, the bottom half is practice, and the useful thing is in the middle.
  2. The question form, drilled chorally three times: How long have you had your dog? — Since last year. — For eleven months. Both short answers are correct and neither needs a verb.
  3. Practice ex.1 on p.161, 3 minutes: sort the time expressions into the since column and the for column. Two are already done as examples.

  5. Set practice ex.4 (write How long …? questions) as the bridge into the speaking half — they will need those question shapes in twenty-five minutes.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.87: seven half-sentences finished so that they are true for you

Why: Correcting other people's sentences is faster and safer than producing them, and the Showdown means every student answers every item instead of one student answering one.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 p.87: five wrong sentences, corrected as a team against the clock

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed, one demonstration on the board (TB p.170): We've collected badges since a long time. Cross out since, write for above it, and ask one student to say why.
  2. Books open, five sentences, 4 minutes. Kagan Showdown: the team agrees, writes on the mini-whiteboard, and on Showdown! every board goes up at once.
  3. Key: It's been my hobby since I was ten years old. · I've needed one for a month. · I've had it for a year. · I've wanted to buy this T-shirt for several weeks. · It has been open for one month.
  4. For every item the team says WHY, not what: Amount or starting point?
  5. Item 1 is the one that matters most — from I was ten instead of since — because it is the Russian preposition translated straight through. Name it, and write both versions up.

👦 Students do: correct as a team and reveal together. Expect 4 of 5. Typical error: item 4 (several weeks) — it has no number, so they read it as a moment and choose since

Pronunciation · weak forms
class
Why: Eight minutes on the reason they cannot hear the grammar: for, was, from, to and some are never stressed, so in real speech they nearly disappear.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5 p.87 (track 114): why they cannot hear the words the grammar depends on

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. SB p.87 ex.5, track 114 — five sentences with one weak word underlined in each: I've had this since I was three. · He's played basketball for two years. · I've just walked home from school. · They haven't been to your house since Saturday. · I've bought some nice shoes. Function words — auxiliaries, prepositions, articles — are said with a schwa /ə/ and never stressed. TB p.170 cooler: chant «one and two and three and four» clicking your fingers on the numbers only, so the class hears that and becomes /ən/ and takes no time.
  2. Play track 114 and ask one question before anything is explained: Which word in each sentence is the quietest? It is always the underlined one.
  3. Do the chant from TB p.170: say one, two, three, four and have the class chant it with you, clicking your fingers on the numbers. Then one AND two AND three AND four — same speed, same clicks. The and takes no time at all.
  4. Say why it matters, in one sentence: «For» sounds like /fə/. That is why you cannot hear it — and it is the word that decides the whole sentence.
  5. Play track 114 again and repeat all five chorally, exaggerating the weakness.
📺 On screen: track 114 in the board app (Backup: say the five sentences yourself, twice each — once with the weak form and once wrongly, with the word stressed — and have the class tell you which version sounded like real English)
👦 Students do: listen, chant and repeat. Expect the chant to be the thing they remember. Typical error: when they repeat, they stress the weak word to be clear — which is exactly the habit being unlearnt

Why: The productive outcome: the book's own flow chart turns the grammar into a real conversation, and every branch of it needs How long have you …?

WHAT WE DO: ex.6 p.87: the flow chart — five questions each, following the yes and no branches

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Show the flow chart and read one path through it out loud so the shape is obvious: Do you have a hobby? — Yes. — What is it? — How long have you enjoyed doing it? — Do you need anything special?

  5. Walk and log errors silently. Do not correct during the interview.

WHAT WE DO: ex.7 p.87: four sentences about your partner, then read them to the group

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can use the present perfect with for and since to say how long, and ask How long have you …? and answer it.
  • Subsidiary. Listening to three speakers for the activity and for detail; the weak forms of for, was, from, to and some; writing about a partner in the third person.
  • Personal. Teach the choice, not the tense. If they leave the room able to pick for or since without thinking, the lesson has worked even if the third person singular needs another visit.
By the end, students can…
  • say how long they have done something, with for or since
  • ask How long have you …? and understand the answer
  • hear the difference between a stressed and an unstressed word
  • write four true sentences about a partner in the third person

Structure
I've had my own computer for three years. · I haven't bought any football cards for a long time. · I've played the guitar for two years. · I've had this guitar since January. · I've had one of them since I was ten. · My dad's had his camera since he was a teenager.
Form
have / has + past participle + for + an amount of time, or since + the moment it started. Question: How long have you …? Short answer: For two years. / Since January.
Pron. watch
the weak forms — for is /fə/, was is /wəz/, from is /frəm/, to is /tə/, some is /səm/. They are never stressed, and that is why students cannot hear them.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I've played the guitar for two years.» Do I play the guitar now? → yes. That is why it is not the past simple.
«three years» — for or since? → for. It is an amount of time.
«January» — for or since? → since. It is the moment it started.
«I've lived here since three years» — what is wrong? → since needs a starting point; three years is an amount, so it is for.
«It's been my hobby from I was ten» — what is wrong? → from is the Russian preposition translated straight through. English uses since. English uses since.

They produce the present simple: «I live here for five years»Ask one question back, every time, without correcting: Do you live here now? (yes) Did you live here last year? (yes) So the sentence needs both. Which tense holds both? They repair it themselves in about four seconds.
The interview at the end turns into single-word answersThat is what the flow chart on SB p.87 is for — it has follow-up branches printed on it. Point at the branch rather than at the student: You are on the yes branch. What is the next box?
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
