🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Give advice on a real problem, and choose between the ‑ing form and the to-infinitive after a preposition, after a verb, and when the verb starts the sentence.
What is genuinely hard
There is no rule to work it out from. Decide to go and enjoy going are both perfectly logical and the pattern is simply different. It is a list, and the honest thing is to say so.
Typical error to expect
I enjoy to play / I want playing / I finished to write — the wrong pattern after the verb. There is no rule to work it out from; it is a list [high priority, A2/B1]
I'm worried about lose my phone / Thank you for invite me — a bare infinitive after a preposition instead of the ‑ing form [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The lesson is built around a reason to speak: three teenagers with problems the class recognises. They read to give advice, not to answer comprehension questions, and the expert's recorded advice becomes the model for their own. The grammar arrives from that same text — every example sentence in the rules box is a line the students have already read as advice. The three patterns are taught as three SORTING boxes rather than as rules, because sorting is what you can actually do with a list. Pronunciation sits at 40′ as a short, self-contained block: four sounds behind two letters, and eight very common words unlocked.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Three people your age have a problem. One is being copied by her best friend. One is being left out. One is losing her class. Today you are the expert who answers them — and the English that gives advice has one trap in it, which we are going to disarm first.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a spelling round on ‑er and one opinion each, then the bridge into Unit 10

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. Two rounds: first, six verbs to turn into people — win · swim · run · dance · climb · photograph, written on mini-whiteboards, spelling counts.
  3. Second, one opinion each with a phrase from last lesson's box: Chess should be in the Olympics. Agree or disagree, plus because.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the new unit: Books open, page fifty-eight. Three people your age have written to a website with a problem. Today you are the expert who answers them.
📺 On screen: the six verbs in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write the people words, then give one opinion each. Expect the doubled letters to be right more often than last lesson. Typical problem — I'm agree: write I agree up in coloured chalk and move on

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two sentences on the board: I decided to join a club. and I enjoy joining clubs.
  2. Both correct. Why is one 'to join' and one 'joining'? What is the rule? Let them try for thirty seconds and fail.
  3. Then the honest answer: There is no rule. It is a list, and today you sort the list into three boxes. Draw the three boxes empty and leave them there.
  4. Set the bar: Every piece of advice you give today has to be a whole sentence somebody could actually follow.
📺 On screen: the two model sentences and the three empty boxes in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: try to find a rule and discover there isn't one. Expect frustration for about ten seconds and then relief. Typical problem: someone insists there IS a rule — thank them and put the three boxes up

Reading · Teen Troubles
solo → pairs
Why: Three real teenage problems: they read to give advice, not to answer comprehension questions. That is what makes the reading feel worth doing.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.58 (track 74): three problems on an advice website, read in order to answer them

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pre-teach two things in the first ninety seconds (TB p.113): troubles — problems you have; and advice is uncountable, so some advice or a piece of advice, never an advice. Write the second one on the board and leave it there.
  2. Read the three problems, 3 minutes. SB p.58, the three problems on the Teen Troubles website: Andrea, 13 — the dance teacher has moved her to a higher group and her close friends have to stay in the lower one. Ben, 15 — he is homeschooled, is friends with his neighbours and with old friends from primary school, but they often forget to invite him. Katy, 14 — her best friend has started copying her clothes and now they look exactly the same.
  3. Three comprehension questions (TB p.113): What sport does Andrea do? (dancing) How does Ben feel? (lonely) Is Katy pleased with her friend? (no).
  4. Kagan Think-Pair-Share: Choose ONE of the three. What would you tell them? Twenty seconds to think, then tell your partner.
  5. Play track 74 so they hear the problems read at speed while they follow.
📺 On screen: the three problems on the screen so the class reads the same one together (Backup: they are printed on SB p.58 — everyone works from the paper book and you read Andrea's problem aloud as the model)
👦 Students do: read, answer, then give advice in pairs. Expect Katy's problem to get the strongest reactions. Typical error: I can give you an advice — the pre-teaching is what stops it becoming the error of the whole lesson

Listening · Dr Mandy's advice
solo → pairs
Why: Note-taking rather than gap-filling — a harder, more useful listening sub-skill, and the notes become the model for the advice they give in the last stage.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2–3 p.58 (track 75): who gets which piece of advice, then notes on each

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.2: play track 75 once. Three pieces of advice. Write the name next to each number. Key: 1 Ben · 2 Katy · 3 Andrea.
  2. Before the second play, teach note-taking rather than assuming it (TB p.113): show three notes on the board and ask what is wrong with each — studying alone isn't easy (that is not advice) · think about joining some clubs (could be shorter: join clubs) · remember to wait for people to call you (wrong: the opposite is true).
  3. Play track 75 again. Students make their own notes for all three, then compare in pairs.
  4. Was any of Mandy's advice the same as yours? Take three answers, no more.

👦 Students do: match, then take notes and compare. Expect the notes to start as full sentences and shorten after the model. Typical error: they copy the whole sentence they hear instead of the point of it

Vocabulary · relationships
pairs → class
Why: Ten words for people, learnt through definitions and then used in questions about the students' own lives within five minutes.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–6 p.58 (track 76): ten people, then questions about the students' own lives

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Before the matching (TB p.113): Make a list of the last ten people you wrote to or spoke to. Then: Put them in groups — friends, relatives, teachers. One minute, and the words arrive with a use already attached.
  2. ex.4 in pairs, 4 minutes. Play track 76 to check and repeat. Key: 1 b · 2 f · 3 a · 4 e · 5 d · 6 c · 7 h · 8 i · 9 j · 10 g.
  3. Cover the descriptions and go backwards: you say the word, they give the definition. Then: Give me an example — when did you last have a guest?
  4. ex.5: four questions, plus three more written by the students themselves. Demonstrate a full answer yourself first (TB p.114): How many contacts do you have online? — I've got a lot, but most of them are not really close friends.
  5. ex.6 as Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade: each student mingles with their own three questions and asks them of five different people.
📺 On screen: track 76 in the board app, with the ten words on the screen (Backup: read the ten definitions aloud yourself and let the class call the word from the paper book; the question round needs no technology at all)
👦 Students do: match, reverse, then write and ask their own questions. Expect contact and member to be the two new ones. Typical error: a one-word answer to the questions — the modelled full answer is what prevents it

Pronunciation · gh
class → pairs
Why: Four different sounds hiding behind two letters. It looks like a curiosity and it is actually the reading of eight very common words.

WHAT WE DO: ex.7 p.59 (tracks 77–78): eight words, four sounds, two letters

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write one sentence on the board and ask who can read it: I thought my neighbour on the right had eight daughters. Let two students try. How many different sounds does 'gh' have here? (three).
  2. Play track 77 and have them sort the eight words into the four columns. Key: /aɪ/ night — bright, flight, right · /eɪ/ eight — neighbour, straight · /ʌf/ rough — enough · /ɔː/ bought — caught, daughter, thought.
  3. TB p.114: in all these words except enough, the rule is that gh is silent after a vowel. More words for each column: /aɪ/ light · /eɪ/ weigh · /ʌf/ tough · /ɔː/ ought.
  4. Play track 78 to check, then repeat all eight chorally.
  5. Close it by reading the board sentence again, all together, twice.
📺 On screen: tracks 77 and 78 in the board app, with the four columns on the screen (Backup: say the eight words yourself, twice each, and let students come to the board to write each word under one of the four headings written in chalk)
👦 Students do: sort by ear, then repeat. Expect enough to be the odd one out that they notice themselves. Typical error: daughter put with neighbour because both look the same on paper

46–58′
Grammar · verb patterns
pairs → class
Why: There is no rule to reason out here, so the stage is built as noticing and sorting rather than as explanation. What they sort themselves, they keep.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.59: sort the patterns out of the advice they have just read

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Say the honest thing first, in one sentence (TB p.114): When one verb follows another, the second one is either 'to do' or 'doing'. There is no rule. You learn which verbs take which.
  2. ex.1 in pairs, 6 minutes: the example sentences are all from the website they read at 8′. Complete the four rules. Key: 1 about, at, for · 2 try, decide, forget · 3 stop, miss · 4 (start, begin, enjoy, like, love, prefer).
  3. Fill the three boxes on the board from the lesson frame as they call the answers out: after a preposition → ‑ing · after try, decide, forget → to · after stop, miss → ‑ing, plus the fourth box either for start, begin, enjoy, like, love, prefer.
  4. ex.2: three more examples where the verb is the SUBJECT. Key: verb + ‑ing (the gerund is used when the verb is the subject of the sentence). Add it as the fifth box.
  5. Concept-check with the CCQs before any practice — especially I'm worried about ___ my phone.
📺 On screen: the rules box on the screen, completed live, next to the five boxes drawn on the board (Backup: the rules box is printed on SB p.59 — copy the five boxes onto the board and let students come up and write the verbs into them)
👦 Students do: sort the verbs into boxes and complete the rules. Expect the preposition box to be the easiest and the «either» box to be the one they distrust. Typical error: they want a reason for each box — say again that there isn't one
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare3_Unit10_Grammar_Gerunds_And_Infinitives_A5
A5 grammar card — WHICH VERB ROUTE?: four routes after the first verb — VERB + -ING (enjoy · finish · don't mind · miss · stop) · VERB + TO + BASE VERB (choose · need · plan · try · decide · forget · hope · learn · want) · PREPOSITION + -ING (good at · interested in · worried about · think of · by) · -ING AS THE SUBJECT (Waiting is boring.) — with the like/love/hate/start box (both possible) and the ERROR CHECK I enjoy playing ✔ / I enjoy to play ✘
  • Glue the card in — the route belongs to the FIRST verb, learn them together.
  • Colour the two main lists in two colours and cover them to test yourself.
  • Write four sentences about yourself, one per route.
  • Run the error check on a partner's sentences: find verb 1 → choose the route.
When: ~5 min at the start of the “Grammar · verb patterns” stagePrep: 1 × A5 per pupil. Scissors, glue sticks, pencils.

Grammar · practice
solo → pairs
Why: Controlled practice in two directions: rewriting rules, then shifting them into the past. The past forms take two minutes to teach and are forgotten in every exam paper.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.59: ten sentences, choose the form — and one of them takes both

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Tell them the trap before they start, exactly as the book does: In ONE sentence both forms are possible. Find it.
  2. Solo, 5 minutes. Then Kagan RallyCoach: partner A explains 1–5, partner B explains 6–10, and the explanation must name the box.
  3. Key: 1 to buy · 2 Waiting · 3 carrying · 4 to be · 5 Swimming · 6 sitting · 7 running · 8 to get / getting · 9 getting · 10 Saving
  4. The sentence with two answers is number 8 (Everyone loves to get / getting presents!) — love is in the «either» box.
  5. Collect the hardest items with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Sentence ten — number threes, boards up! (Saving).
📺 On screen: the ten sentences on the screen, one at a time (Backup: they are printed on SB p.59 — uncover them on the page with a ruler and keep the five boxes in chalk on the board)
👦 Students do: choose and justify by box. Expect 7–8 out of 10. Typical error: items 2, 5 and 10, where the verb is the subject and looks like a command — Wait for people makes me angry

Grammar · error hunt
teams → solo
Why: Correcting other people's sentences is faster and safer than producing them, and the Showdown means every student answers every item instead of one student answering one.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 p.59 (six sentences to correct) and ex.5 (ten gaps to fill)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4, 4 minutes, Kagan Showdown: the team writes the corrected sentence and every board goes up at once. Key: I hope to see you very soon, and I hope that you like my mobile phone. · I want to write about my life. · I like to play / playing computer games best. · You can get to my house by taking the number 6 bus. · I think it's better to finish studying before we go out. · Would you mind coming with me to the shops?.
  2. Variation from TB p.114 if the group is strong: books closed, you read the wrong sentences out and they correct them by ear.
  3. ex.5 — ten gaps with a verb from the box, solo, 4 minutes. Demonstrate the first one on the board: I'm worried about ______ a lot of money. (spending). Key: 1 to make · 2 Eating · 3 to improve · 4 Watching · 5 playing · 6 Passing · 7 to come · 8 Chatting · 9 going · 10 Spending.
  4. Do not check ex.5 as a class in full — take items 4, 6, 8 and 10 (the four where the verb is the subject) and leave the rest to the workbook.
📺 On screen: the six wrong sentences one at a time on the screen (Backup: write them on the board before the lesson and uncover them one by one with a sheet of paper)
👦 Students do: correct as a team, then fill the gaps alone. Expect item 3 of ex.4 (I like play / playing) to produce an argument about whether both are right — they are. Typical error: ex.5 item 8 (Chatting with friends is really good fun) written as Chat

🎯 Speaking out · problem and advice
groups of 4
Why: The productive outcome: each student writes a real problem and the group answers it. The advice almost cannot be given without a verb pattern, which is the point.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6 p.59: each student writes a problem, the group answers it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Each student writes four or five sentences about a problem — real or invented, their choice. 4 minutes, silent. The rule: Use 'I'm worried about …' or 'I can't stop …' somewhere in it.
  2. In fours, the problems go round. Everybody listens to all three and gives one piece of advice each.
  3. The standard from the lesson frame: Advice somebody could actually follow. And a whole sentence.
  4. Each group chooses the most interesting problem and the best advice, and reads them to the class. Put them on the wall (TB p.114) — other groups add extra advice underneath during the week.
  5. Log errors silently on your correction sheet; do not correct aloud.
📺 On screen: the two required phrases and the five boxes on the board (Backup: the same checklist dictated and copied into notebooks — the task needs no technology at all)
👦 Students do: write a problem, then advise three other people. Expect the advice to be much better English than the problems, because the model is fresh. Typical error: Try talk to her — note it, say nothing

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can use the gerund or the to-infinitive correctly after a preposition, after common verbs and as the subject, and give advice on a problem.
  • Subsidiary. Ten words for people; note-taking from a recording; four pronunciations of gh.
  • Personal. Do not pretend there is a logic. Say out loud that this is a list, give them the three boxes to sort it into, and spend the time on sorting rather than on explaining.
By the end, students can…
  • name ten kinds of people, from a classmate to a penfriend
  • take short notes from spoken advice
  • read bright, flight, neighbour, straight, enough, caught, daughter and thought
  • give a piece of advice with the right verb pattern

Structure

Form
preposition + ‑ing (about, at, for, by, of, with) · try / decide / forget / choose / learn / hope / plan / need / want + to-infinitive · stop / miss / finish / don't mind + ‑ing · start, begin, enjoy, like, love, prefer — either · subject of the sentence → ‑ing.
Pron. watch
gh — four sounds: /aɪ/ bright, flight, right · /eɪ/ neighbour, straight · /ʌf/ enough · /ɔː/ caught, daughter, thought. Also: advice is uncountable, so never an advice.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I'm worried about ___ my phone (lose).» Which form? → losing: about is a preposition.
«I decided ___ (go) to the party.» Which form? → to go: decide takes the to-infinitive.
«___ alone isn't easy (study).» Which form? → Studying: the verb is the subject.
«I enjoy swimming» / «I enjoy to swim» — is the second one possible? → no. Enjoy is in the «either» list for start and begin, but enjoy itself takes ‑ing. Learn the list, don't reason it out.

Assumptions: SB p.58–59, syllabus lesson 30. Verb patterns have appeared incidentally (I want to go, I like swimming) but have never been named. Of the ten relationship words, guest, neighbour, classmate and relative are the ones that need real teaching.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
“How do I know which one to use?”Answer honestly in one sentence: You don't work it out. You learn the list. Then give them the three boxes and make the lesson about sorting, not about reasoning.
An advice, all lesson, from everybodyPre-teach it in the first two minutes of the reading stage, exactly as TB p.113 does: advice — some advice, a piece of advice. Never 'an advice'. Written on the board, left there.
The notes in the listening stage are full sentencesUse the TB p.113 model: show three notes on the board, two of them bad, and ask what is wrong with each. Notes are shorter than sentences and they must be true.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
