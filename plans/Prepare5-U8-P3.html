🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use six phrasal verbs (catch up with, end up, move in, move out, show someone around, stay in) with the right word order, and write a 100-word email that answers all four notes on the task — Cambridge Writing Part 1.
What is genuinely hard
Two mechanical problems and one strategic one. Word order: a pronoun object goes INSIDE the phrasal verb (show me around) while a noun can go either side. Three-part verbs: catch up WITH somebody, and the with vanishes in half the class's sentences. And strategically — Writing Part 1 marks whether all four notes were answered, and the commonest B1 failure is answering three of them beautifully and forgetting the fourth, which is exactly what the model writer does.
Typical error to expect
«show around me», «catch up him» — a pronoun object of a phrasal verb goes between the verb and the particle (show me around), while a noun may go either side. And catch up WITH somebody is a three-part verb whose last word disappears in half the class's sentences.
The writing error is structural: extra material nobody asked for (the model email ends with a boast about a maths mark) and one note left unanswered.
Why the lesson is built this way
The phrasal verbs are harvested from yesterday's reading, where two of them already appeared, then produced in the pair activity on p.138 — which is what makes them stick, not the gap-fill. The writing half is model-first and deliberately uses a model with a FAULT in it: exercise 2 asks whether Inês covers all the notes, and she does not. Finding somebody else's missing note is the single most useful thing a candidate can practise for this task. Then the linking words are matched to meanings, the adjectives are collected as a team game, and only then do they write — with the four-note checklist on the board and their partner ticking it afterwards.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“This email is good. It is friendly, it is well written — and it would lose marks, because one of the four notes is not answered. Today: find her mistake, then write one hundred words that do not make it.”

Why: Vocabulary · six phrasal verbs

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: harvested from yesterday's text, then the word-order rule made explicit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: find the two phrasal verbs in the sentences taken from yesterday's reading — showed … around and stay in. Write them on the board and label the parts: verb + particle.
  2. Then the rule that costs marks, and it is worth three minutes. Ask which of the two has an object (show … around). Point at the pronoun: I showed HIM around — a pronoun always goes between the verb and the particle. Now write show our friends around and show around our friends — a noun can go either side.
  3. ex.2 individually, four minutes: six gaps with the correct form. Note that catch up with has THREE parts — ask what they notice about it before they start. the six phrasal verbs: 1 moved out · 2 stay in · 3 showed … around · 4 ended up · 5 catch up with · 6 moved in
  4. Check by reading whole sentences. For each one, ask which words in the sentence pointed to that verb — that is the skill, not the meaning.
  5. Thirty-second drill of the killer pattern, three times: Show me around. Catch up with me. I'll show you around.
📺 On screen: the two verbs labelled on the board and the pronoun rule beside them — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: find the verbs in a known text, then produce six forms alone and justify each choice

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: the six verbs used about their own lives, out loud, twice

Move break · inside or outside

Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer aimed at the one rule that breaks

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. You say a sentence with the phrasal verb wrong or right; the class shows a thumb up for right and a thumb down for wrong, all at once, on your count.
  2. She showed around me. · I'll catch up you later. · They moved in last week. · He showed the visitors around. · We ended up in Turkey. · Can you show around my cousin?
  3. For every wrong one, the whole class says the corrected version aloud together — chorus, not one volunteer.
  4. Six sentences, six votes. Then sit: Page eighty-nine. One email, four notes, and one of them is missing.
📺 On screen: no display at all — the sentences are read aloud and the whole room votes with thumbs
👦 Students do: every student judges six sentences simultaneously and says three corrections in chorus

Writing · linkers and adjectives
individual → teams
Why: Writing · linkers and adjectives

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–5: the linking words matched and used, then the adjectives collected as a game

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3, three minutes: the four highlighted linking words matched to their meanings. 1 Actually · 2 However · 3 Finally · 4 despite
  2. Take the observation the Teacher's Book asks for, because it is a punctuation mark: however, actually and finally all come at the START of the sentence and take a comma. Despite does not — it sits in the middle and is followed by a noun or an -ing form.
  3. ex.4, three minutes individually: the four sentences. 1 despite · 2 However · 3 Actually · 4 Finally At the check, ask what the relationship between the two halves is each time — that is what a linker encodes.
  4. ex.5 as a team game, four minutes: four columns on the board (the city · the café food · the museum · the art and films). Teams find Inês's adjectives first, then add as many of their own as they can. One point each, two if nobody else has it. the adjectives Inês uses: 1 lively, small (others: crowded, busy, exciting) · 2 amazing (tasty, terrible) · 3 famous (historic, impressive) · 4 cool, interesting (lively, busy, modern)
  5. Everyone copies three adjectives and two linkers they intend to use into the margin of their notebook. Not the whole board — five words.
📺 On screen: the four adjective columns filling up with team answers — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: match and use four linkers alone, then compete in teams to collect adjectives

Peer check + the model answer
pairs → class
Why: Peer check + the model answer

WHAT WE DO: the partner ticks the notes, then the Teacher's Book model goes up

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap notebooks. Kagan RallyCoach, three minutes: the partner ticks the four notes — and only the writer's OWN ticks can be wrong, which is the point — then writes one sentence that could be better, with a reason.
  2. Two minutes: the writers repair that one sentence. Only that one.
  3. Now the model goes up, and only now: the Teacher's Book model, about a hundred words, for the board AFTER they have written their own: «Hi Jon / Thanks for your email and I'm happy to help with your interesting school project. I live in a quiet little town near the sea. In the summer young people usually meet on the beach… It's freezing here in the winter so people usually stay in and they play games or watch films. For teenagers coming to my town I suggest doing cool activities like surfing or paddle-boarding. It's great that you're coming to visit me because I want to show you round the town. Any time in April's good for me. Can you come then? / Mishi» — notice that the model uses this unit's phrasal verbs: catching up with, stay in, show you round
  4. Thirty seconds on one question: Which phrasal verbs did the model use, and did you use any? It uses three of today's six, which is not an accident.
  5. Collect the notebooks; these are marked against the four notes and the Prepare to write box, and nothing else.

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Show around me» — possible? → no. Where does the pronoun go? → inside: show me around. And with a noun? → either side.
«I caught up my friend» — what is missing? → with. Catch up WITH somebody, always three parts.
The task has four notes. If your email answers three of them perfectly, how many marks do you lose? → enough to matter. What do you do while writing? → tick each note off.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
