🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Choose between the present perfect and the past simple by the time marker — and ask and answer How long…? with for/since chosen correctly.
What is genuinely hard
The tenses exist separately after lessons 2–4; what is genuinely new is the CONTRAST: «I've tried sushi» (experience, no date) against «I tried it on Saturday» (dated). Russian uses one past for both, so the decision has no L1 anchor — the time marker must become the anchor. And for/since: one Russian preposition, two English ones.
Typical error to expect
Yesterday I have left my phone at your house / Two weeks ago I've watched a match — a past time phrase glued onto the present perfect; and the mirror error, I didn't see you for a long time for haven't seen [high priority, A2/B1]
I know her since 2010 — Russian present tense pushed into an English unfinished-past slot; plus for/since swapped after the right tense is finally chosen [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The lesson opens the gate lesson 2 kept closed: dates are now allowed, and the whole hour is about what they do to the tense. The TB's two-column warmer (LAST WEEK / THIS WEEK) makes the contrast visible before it is named; the rules are then extracted from Ollie's sentences, which the class read two days ago. The ever→follow-up drill (Have you ever…? → Where did you…?) is the real-life shape of the contrast and gets double time. The Corpus hunt is six real learner errors — the exact ones the block test plants. The productive stage is a timeline interview: How long / Have you ever / When in one conversation, which is how the tenses actually live.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two sentences: I've eaten sushi. I ate sushi on Saturday. Same fish, different grammar — and by the end of today you'll never mix them up again, because the date decides, not you. Plus one question that unlocks any biography: How long have you had that phone?”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a three-sentence repair clinic, and the repeal of the no-dates law

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — error clinic from the taste panel. Three anonymous sentences from your lesson-4 log (a «most tasty», a missing because, an adjective misuse). Kagan RallyRobin in pairs: find and fix all three, hands up, repair together.

  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page sixty, top half. Two tenses, one decision — and the date makes it for you.
📺 On screen: three faulty sentences in chalk — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: hunt the errors, then react to the repeal — someone will remember the promise from lesson 2; let them say it

WHAT WE DO: the TB two-column warmer + ex.1 rules from Ollie

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Run the TB warmer: TB warmer — two board columns, LAST WEEK / THIS WEEK: Last week I went to the cinema · This week I've marked your homework. Students add their own; the class names the difference (last week is finished, this week is not).

  3. Point back at lesson 4: «Ollie's eaten everything from ants to zebra» — when exactly? The text never says. «I made an ostrich curry LAST WEEK» — dated.
  4. Concept checks from the plan header — the sushi pair, cold-called.
📺 On screen: the two columns fill in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: add their own column sentences, extract the rules. Expect the columns to do the teaching — the rules just name them. Typical error: «I've marked» read as present — one line of concept check

Grammar · controlled choice
solo → pairs
Why: Choose-and-justify practice: the time marker decides, not the feeling. The follow-up pairs (ever → past simple detail) rehearse the exact tense handover real conversation uses.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 choose by marker + ex.3 the ever→follow-up shape

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.2 solo against the clock (3 minutes), pair check with the reason said aloud. Key: 1 wasn't (the party finished) · 2 had (yesterday) · 3 've been (experience) · 4 Have you ever cooked · 5 went (Sunday) · 6 've never ordered.
  2. Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Item five — number threes! And the reason!
  3. ex.3 — the real-life shape, built on the model first: 1 Have you ever made anyone angry? — Who was it? · 2 Have you ever eaten out with your friends? — Where did you go? · 3 Have you ever won anything? — What did you win? (the model: Have you ever been to a concert? — Yes. — Who did you see? — I saw Calvin Harris last year.)
  4. Drill the handover chorally: Have you ever…? — Yes, I have. — WHEN DID you…? The gear change is the point.
  5. Pairs run the three items both ways; you listen for the follow-up tense.
📺 On screen: the handover chunk in chalk under the gate rule — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: choose with reasons, then drill the handover. Expect the follow-up to slip back into the perfect once or twice — the slogan fixes it: specific story, past simple. Typical error: Where have you gone last summer?

How long? · for and since
class → standing → solo
Why: The question and its two little words get their own stage because Russian collapses them into one: the sort is done fast and physically, and the About-you sentences make it personal.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 rules + ex.5 standing sort + ex.6 about you

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4 — the two Ollie questions fill the fork. Key: a How long · b since · c for. The fork gets its labels: for + LENGTH · since + STARTING POINT.
  2. ex.5 STANDING: I call the phrase — for walks left, since walks right: three weeks! this morning! 2018! a long time! Monday! four o'clock! a few years! midday! Key: for: three weeks, a long time, a few years · since: this morning, 2018, Monday, four o'clock, midday.
  3. ex.6 solo, 4 minutes — six true sentences about themselves. Key: 1 haven't missed · 2 've been · 3 haven't eaten · 4 has worked · 5 haven't done · 6 have lived (+ students' own endings).
  4. Three or four read one sentence aloud; the class shows thumbs if the for/since is right.
  5. Signpost: Grammar reference page one four seven — tonight, two minutes, if you want it as a table.
📺 On screen: the fork fills in chalk; the sort is bodies in a room — the board and the floor are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: extract, walk the sort, personalise. Expect «this morning» to send half the room left — the starting-point question turns them around. Typical error: since three weeks

Why: Six real learner sentences, all of them errors this class will otherwise make in the test: find, fix, and say WHY — the why is what transfers.

WHAT WE DO: ex.7: six real learner errors — find, fix, say why

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.7 in pairs — Kagan RallyCoach: one pen, the partner asks for the WHY before anything is written. Key: Corpus: 1 Yesterday I LEFT · 2 I HAVEN'T SEEN you for a long time · 3 I've KNOWN her since 2010 · 4 I GOT a lot of presents · 5 Have you ever VISITED London? · 6 Two weeks ago I WATCHED a tennis match.
  2. Score on the board per pair; every explanation must name the marker (yesterday → past simple; since 2010 → perfect).
  3. The two most-missed items get thirty calm seconds each: the pair that got it right explains.
  4. Land the warning kindly: All six are real students' sentences. Two of them will try to visit your test in two weeks. Now they can't.
📺 On screen: the six sentences are in every book; scores in chalk — paper and board are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: hunt, fix, name the marker. Expect items 1 and 6 (the dated perfects) to be found first, item 3 (know→'ve known) last — it is the deepest one. Typical error: fixing item 3 by deleting since

WHAT WE DO: ex.8: the book's closing interview, plus two questions of their own

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

  2. Pairs interview both ways; every answer must carry for or since — the partner polices it.

🎯 Speaking out · the timeline interview
pairs, then swap
Why: The productive outcome: a partner's life timeline built from How long / Have you ever / When questions — the three question shapes of the unit in one conversation, reported to the class.

WHAT WE DO: a partner's life timeline from three question shapes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The mission on the board: Build your partner's timeline: one thing they HAVE DONE (no date needed), one thing they DID (with the date), one thing they HAVE HAD for ages (how long?).
  2. One minute solo to plan questions.
  3. Kagan Timed Pair Share, 3 minutes each way: interview, note the three facts, follow up once per fact.
  4. Swap partners once and repeat faster.
  5. You walk with a paper: log tense and for/since slips SILENTLY — they open lesson 6.
  6. Debrief in the third person, two or three reporters: She's never eaten raw fish. She tried curry in Moscow last year. And she's had her phone since her birthday. — three tenses, one breath; name it when it happens.
📺 On screen: the three-line mission in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: interview, note, report in three tenses. Expect the report step to be the hardest and the most valuable — third person has/have plus the handover. Typical error: dated perfect in the report — logged, not corrected

Structure
Ollie's eaten everything from ants to zebra. · I made an ostrich curry last week. · Have you ever eaten out with friends? — Where did you go? · How long have you had your phone? — For two years. / Since my birthday.
Form
present perfect = experience, no stated time; past simple = dated (yesterday, last week, two years ago); past time phrases never travel with the perfect; How long? + for + length / since + starting point.
Pron. watch
the weak have in questions: How long /həv/ you had…? · the contraction I've /aɪv/ must survive · since /sɪns/ — one syllable, no /aɪ/
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I've tried sushi» vs «I tried sushi in Moscow last summer» — which one says WHEN? → the second; that is why it is past simple.
«I've had my phone for/since two years» — is two years a length or a starting point? → a length: for.
«I know her since 2010» — what's broken? → the tense: unfinished past needs the present perfect — I've known her since 2010.

Assumptions: SB p.60 (top), syllabus lesson 30. The present perfect is three lessons old (lesson 2) and warm; the past simple is solid since block 1. The «no dates» rule from lesson 2 is about to be repealed — the class was promised this moment. Lesson 4's error log is in your folder.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«Yesterday I have eaten…» — the perfect glued to a dateThe gate rule, boxed on the board: A DATE IN THE SENTENCE = PAST SIMPLE. Point at the box silently every time; the Corpus hunt drills it six ways.
for/since chosen by feelThe two-question tool: is it a LENGTH (for) or a STARTING POINT (since)? The standing sort makes the decision physical before it must be fast.
The follow-up question stays in the perfect («Where have you gone?»)Drill the handover as a chunk: Have you ever…? — Yes. — WHEN DID you…? The moment the story gets specific, the tense changes — say it as a slogan.
Reasons dropped under speed«Say why» is the price of the point in every round today: no reason, no point, smile included.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
