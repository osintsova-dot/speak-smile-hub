🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Why it is built this way (for the teacher): The 'exchange student' frame turns a review of familiar routines into a task with a decision in it: the country facts now have someone to go to. Alex's three conditions (early mornings · sport · free afternoons) are the filter the crews reread their files through — not 'tell us about France'. It also legitimises the 3rd person: we talk about our country as he/they, so the -s is under load. No 'today's topic is the Present simple' out loud — the grammar hides inside the work with facts.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Agents, new file. This is Alex — an exchange student. One term, one school, and WE choose which one. Alex has three conditions: hates early mornings, loves sport, needs free afternoons. Each crew gets ONE country file. At the end of the lesson you brief the class: is YOUR school day right for Alex — yes or no, and why. Sixty seconds, judges are watching. Crews — take your files!”
In simpler words, if the class needs support: An exchange student is coming. Each crew has one country file. At the end of the pair you prove in 60 seconds whether your school day suits Alex. The judges score you.

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, crews can brief an exchange student on four school days (France · Finland · Brazil · Ghana) and defend a recommendation in 60 seconds, using 15 daily-routine collocations accurately in the 3rd person.
  • Subsidiary. The gate exposes their ceiling for writing routines at speed; the WDYT map on p.12 is closed as a race; Video skills (Japan) is watched 'like producers'; telling my day from his day (3rd person).
  • Personal. The Present simple is NOT announced as the topic — only the mission is. I collect their real 3rd-person errors silently, for the error clinic in Pair 3 (the grammar pair).
By the end, students can…
  • write 8+ daily-routine collocations in 60 seconds
  • report another person's day in the 3rd person without -s slips
  • compare two school days with 3+ details
  • recommend a school for an exchange student and give reasons

Concept checks (CCQs) · Assumptions
'He don't like maths' — what's wrong? → after he/she/it it is always doesn't.
'She studys' — is that right? → no: consonant + y → studies.
'You have been saying I get up for four years. Can you write 8 clean routines in one minute?' → gate.

Assumptions: KTP material: SB pp.12–13 + Video skills. Routines and the Present simple are familiar from A1+ — what is new is the speed, the 3rd person on someone else's facts, and a product with a bar.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
'We know all this' from the doorwayI don't argue — I put the gate in front of them: WRITING speed and a clean 3rd person. The result is private, the rematch comes at the end of the pair.
Country facts get mixed up in the briefingEach crew has its own country file; other crews' facts only come through asking them questions (a genuine information gap).
The strong ones take over the airtimeThe briefing splits the roles: everyone speaks their own part, and the judges dock a point for a 'one-man show'.
Classroom management — signals
«Eyes up in 3-2-1» — count down calmly → all eyes on you at «1». No shouting over noise, ever.
Visible timer — timer on screen = we work; timer stops = pens/hands down. The timer is the boss, not you.
Volume dial — arm up/down = louder/quieter; «radio whisper» for rehearsals.
«Boards up!» — mini-whiteboard rounds: everyone commits, all boards up together — nobody hides, nobody shouts first.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, class answers with two and freezes — instant reset mid-game.
English-first — ideas in Russian are welcome, POINTS only for the English version — recast and repeat.
