🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use twelve verbs for work tasks (arrange, calculate, deal with, deliver, develop, handle, install, manage, organise, produce, run, update) to describe what a job involves, and match five people to eight texts by MEANING rather than by repeated words.
What is genuinely hard
Reading Part 2 is designed to punish word-matching. Every one of the five teenagers is described in words the adverts deliberately do NOT repeat: «good local knowledge» meets «if you know the area around the city», «above-average ability in maths» meets «learning about costs from top accountants». A candidate who hunts for a matching word will find one in the wrong advert, because the distractors are built out of the obvious words. The vocabulary has its own trap: half the twelve verbs look like near-synonyms until you see their objects.
Typical error to expect
Matching a word instead of a meaning — the single reason a good reader loses three marks on this task. Each of the five descriptions has THREE requirements, and the right advert meets all three; the wrong one meets the loudest one.
«I made my homework», «I did a cake» — the do-and-make family that these twelve verbs sit next to. Produce, develop and manage all have fixed objects, and the exercise is where they get fixed.
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary arrives through four teenagers describing real placements, so the verbs are attached to jobs from the first minute. The listening is played twice with the Teacher's Book support written on the board between the two plays — the speakers' verbs listed by name — which turns an impossible twelve-gap task into a hard but fair one. The reading half is run as a method lesson, not a comprehension one: underline the THREE requirements in each description, then hunt for meaning, then justify. The productive stage sends the twelve verbs into their own lives — what does the job you want actually involve, hour by hour.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Eight real work placements are on the next page — a cruise ship, a riding school, a television studio, a city museum. Five teenagers want one each, and not one of them uses the same words as the advert that fits them. Today: the twelve verbs that say what a job actually involves, and how to match a person to a text without being tricked.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, worksheets collected, and a jobs brainstorm sorted into three columns nobody has labelled

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what is one thing you are going to do this summer? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 2 minutes — the Grammar Plus worksheets from Units 17–18 come in. Collect at the door, look at each for five seconds, and note who has nothing. Do not spend lesson time on it.
  3. 1 minute — the unit warmer, and it opens the topic in ninety seconds. Small groups, three minutes on the clock but ninety seconds is enough: brainstorm as many jobs as you can. Then draw THREE columns on the board without labelling them, and write their suggestions into the right one as they call them out.
  4. Ask the class to name the three columns. Answer: office jobs, active or outdoor jobs, everything else. Then take a vote on the most popular type — it is a thirty-second poll and it makes the About you question land.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the unit: Page one hundred and eight. Unit nineteen — the world of work. Four teenagers are about to tell you what they actually did all day.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4: the twelve gaps with support, then the verbs applied to five jobs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Before the second listening, do the thing the Teacher's Book asks for and it takes ninety seconds: read the twelve verbs aloud in random order while students tick them on the page. They hear the shape of each word before they have to catch it.
  2. Then write the support on the board — this is the difference between a fair task and a lottery: Connie calculate, arrange, produce · Vera handle, deliver, organise · Ali manage, run, develop · Gordon update, deal with, install. Weaker students use it; stronger students cover it.
  3. ex.3, track 74 again, twelve gaps, four minutes. Warn them the FORM changes — some need a past tense, one is passive. Answers: 1 arranged, 2 produce, 3 calculating, 4 were delivered, 5 handle, 6 organised, 7 runs, 8 managing, 9 develop, 10 dealt with, 11 install, 12 updated.
  4. Check by asking for the whole sentence, and every confirmed verb goes on the wall in one of the two columns, written by the student who answered, with the OBJECT written next to it.
  5. ex.4 as Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade, four minutes, standing: each student takes one job — accountant, engineer, shop assistant, teacher, website designer — and finds three partners. What does this person have to do? Two verbs minimum in every answer.

Reading Part 2 · five people, eight texts
individual → pairs → class
Why: Reading Part 2 · five people, eight texts

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: the exam task run as a method, with three requirements per person

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Name the format and the trap in one breath: Five people, eight texts, three of the texts are not used. And the words in the description are NEVER the words in the advert.
  2. The method goes on the board and it is the lesson: for each person, underline THREE requirements. Do person 1 together — Leila wants the arts including live music and dance, she has IT skills she is happy to use, and she does not mind travelling. Three things, all three have to be met.
  3. Five minutes of underlining, individually, before anyone reads a single advert. Walk the room and check that there are three underlinings per person and not one.
  4. Six minutes on the task itself, silent, clock visible. Answers: 1 D, 2 A, 3 F, 4 H, 5 E.
  5. Pairs compare, two minutes, and must agree before the check.
  6. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together. For every person, the named student says all three requirements and the line of the advert that meets each. That is the whole point of the stage — three proofs, not one.
  7. Then the ninety seconds that transfer to the exam: for person 2, which advert was the tempting wrong answer, and which requirement did it fail? Do it once, aloud, and it changes how they read the next one.
📺 On screen: the three-requirements box and the five answers with their proof lines — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail. Backup: the adverts are recorded as track 75 if a weaker reader wants them read aloud
👦 Students do: underline fifteen requirements before reading, work silently against a clock, then defend three proofs per answer

🎯 Speaking out · the job I actually want
individual → pairs → class
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · the job I actually want

WHAT WE DO: the twelve verbs applied to their own future, then a placement advert written for it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everybody names one job they might actually want. Not a dream job — a possible one. Thirty seconds of thinking, then round the room, one word each.
  2. Five minutes, silent, individually: write SIX things that job involves, hour by hour, each one starting with a verb from the wall. Rule on the board: at least four different verbs, and the object is written with the verb.
  3. 🎯 Kagan Timed Pair Share, two minutes each way: read your six. The listener's job is one question that makes the sixth item more specific — Who do you deal with? What exactly do you update?
  4. Then the round that makes it a lesson, ten minutes in pairs: write a WORK PLACEMENT ADVERT for that job, in the style of the eight on page 109 — about sixty words, a title, what the person would do, and what kind of person you need. The eight models are on the page and should be used.
  5. Four minutes: three pairs read theirs out; the class says which of the five teenagers from the exam task would apply, and why. It closes the loop on the reading skill from the other side.
  6. Delayed correction, three minutes: five phrases you heard with the wrong object, on the board, repaired by the class.

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«She runs a restaurant.» Is she moving fast? → no. Run + a business means to be in charge of it.
Deal with, handle, manage — which one takes «a problem»? → all three, and that is the point: they are close. Which takes «software»? → none of them. That is install.
The description says «reasonably strong». The advert says «fit enough to lift heavy items». Same word? → no. Same meaning? → yes. That is the whole task.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
They match on a repeated word and defend it loudlyDo not argue. Ask for the other two requirements: «She also has IT skills and she doesn't mind travelling — where is that in your advert?» The task defeats itself as soon as all three are checked.
Twelve gaps in one listening is too manyThe Teacher's Book support goes on the board BETWEEN the two plays, not after the failure: the three verbs each speaker uses, listed under their name. It turns a guessing task into a listening one.
Half the verbs feel identicalTeach them by object, not by definition. Every check asks the same question: what does this verb take after it? The two-column wall is built on that question and it answers itself.
Fast finishers in the readingThe Teacher's Book task: find and underline although, whereas and while in the eight adverts and decide what they express. The answer is contrast, and it is tomorrow's lesson — they arrive already knowing it.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 10–18 (the reading section — work and career are among the exam's three most frequent topics) and tasks 32–38 (lexical choice).

Reading Part 2 is a matching task built entirely on paraphrase: the eight adverts never repeat the words used to describe the five teenagers, so a candidate who matches vocabulary instead of meaning gets four out of five wrong. That is exactly the machine behind the Russian heading-matching item. The twelve work verbs that arrive today are exam currency in their own right — deal with, handle, manage, run and organise print in the lexical section every year, and the speaking card about future professions is unanswerable without them.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
