🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use eleven health verbs (ache, beat, bleed, blink, blow your nose, breathe, burn, cough, injure, recover, yawn) to say what is wrong and what to do, and use modals of obligation, necessity and advice: have to, don't have to, must, mustn't, should, shouldn't, ought to.
What is genuinely hard
The modals carry four different meanings that Russian expresses with two, and the pair that costs marks is mustn't against don't have to — a prohibition against a free choice. The verbs are also physical actions students have never named in English: blink, yawn and breathe are known concepts with unknown words.
Typical error to expect
«You mustn't come if you're busy» for «you don't have to come» — mustn't is a prohibition, don't have to is a free choice. Russian uses one negative for both, and this is a marked exam item.
«I must to go», «You don't must run» — must takes a bare infinitive and makes its negative directly. The Student's Book plants both traps in the same exercise.
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary arrives inside a first-aid quiz, which is the only honest way to teach eleven verbs at once: the class has to understand them in order to answer, and the podcast then tells them who was right. The reading is an article about screen time and sleep that every teenager in the room has an opinion about, and it is stuffed with the target modals — so the grammar is extracted from a text they have already argued with. The productive stage is a set of house rules for a healthy night, which cannot be written without all four meanings.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Doctors say you need nine hours. The blue light from your phone tells your brain it's still daytime. Today: the English words for what your body actually does — and the difference between «you mustn't» and «you don't have to», which is bigger than it looks.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a 90-second past-tense rewind that walks into the new topic, and the bridge into Unit 6

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what's the best thing about today so far? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — the storytelling rewind, in one sentence each. On the board: Tell your partner about a time you hurt yourself. What were you doing when it happened?
  3. Kagan Think-Pair-Share, 90 seconds. The question is built to demand yesterday's two tenses, and it walks straight into today's topic.
  4. Take three answers and let the class supply the missing English words — that gap is the lesson's opening argument.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the unit: Page thirty-six. Unit six — Take care of yourself. Eight first-aid questions, and I want to know how many you get right.
📺 On screen: the question on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: tell a real story in pairs, then hear where the vocabulary runs out

Why: Vocabulary · the first-aid quiz

WHAT WE DO: About you + ex.1–4: eleven verbs met inside eight real questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. About you in pairs, 60 seconds: Have you ever had to help someone who was hurt? What did you do? When did you last have a day off school because you were ill?
  2. ex.1: what is wrong with the people in the photos? Two answers.
  3. ex.2 (EP): check the meaning of the highlighted verbs in the quiz — in pairs, with gesture and mime allowed and encouraged. Do not translate; act. Take the four hardest to the whole class (blink, yawn, ache, recover).
  4. ex.3: do the quiz in pairs, six minutes, timed. Every choice has to be argued in English.
  5. ex.4, track 20: the podcast gives the answers. Play once, count the scores, and let the class react — the burn question (cool water, not ice, not butter) is always the surprise.
  6. Drill pronunciation of the four that will be needed later: ache /eɪk/, cough /kɒf/, yawn /jɔːn/, breathe /briːð/.
📺 On screen: track 20 for the answers. Backup: if the audio fails, read the podcast from the audioscript (TB p.289) — the answers and the explanations are the same
👦 Students do: argue in pairs about first aid, then find out who was right. The vocabulary is understood because it had to be used

32–36′
Move break · mime the symptom
standing, teams
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that fixes the eleven verbs to actions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three teams. One student from each comes out, takes a card and mimes it — no words, no drawing.
  2. The team must produce a full sentence to score: He's coughing. Her leg aches. A bare verb scores nothing.
  3. Six rounds, thirty seconds each, rotating the mimer.
  4. Last round is the hard one: recover. Let them struggle for twenty seconds, then let another team steal it.
  5. Freeze and sit: Page thirty-seven. Now — why you're tired in the mornings.
📺 On screen: eleven verb cards prepared in advance — nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone watches and produces full sentences; the sentence rule keeps the language complete

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4: four headings from five, then the highlighted words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 in pairs, four minutes: match four of the five headings to paragraphs A–D. Do paragraph A together first and name the words that decided it (devices, blue light, melatonin, sleep patterns).
  2. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together. For each paragraph, the answer must come with two words from the text that support the heading.
  3. Ask about the extra heading — What would a paragraph called «What you should eat before you go to bed» contain? — because recognising the distractor is the transferable skill.
  4. ex.4: five highlighted words, two minutes. Weaker students get pointed at the evidence rather than told (devices → «like phones or tablets»; mood → «sad or anxious»).
  5. Talking points in pairs, three minutes: What was the last thing you did before you went to sleep last night? Do you have a routine before going to sleep? Should you change it? — pairs first, then the floor

class → pairs
Why: Grammar · four meanings, four modals

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: rules extracted from the article, then the traps done in public

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books at page 38. Warmer from the Teacher's Book, books closed, two minutes: four columns on the board — a good idea · a bad idea · necessary · not necessary. Pairs suggest ways to sleep better and you place each suggestion in a column.
  2. ex.1: the six examples are all sentences from the article. Complete the five rules as a class and write the modal into the matching column on the board.
  3. Now the pair that costs marks. Two boxes on the board: a crossed-out phone for mustn't, a phone with a tick and a shrug for don't have to. Test with three quick sentences the class sorts by pointing.
  4. ex.2, track 22: six short recordings, choose the modal. Play twice; this is a listening task in disguise and it is where mustn't and don't have to are hardest.
  5. ex.3 individually, then checked in RallyCoach pairs. Items 5 and 6 are the form traps (I must ask, not must to ask; don't have to, not don't must) — do those two in public on the board.
  6. Grammar reference is SB p.152.
📺 On screen: the four columns and the two boxes on the board, plus track 22 for ex.2. Backup: if the audio fails, read the six sentences aloud yourself from the audioscript (TB p.290) — the choices are unchanged
👦 Students do: sort suggestions into four meanings, then hear and produce the modals. The two boxes settle the hardest distinction visually

74–86′
🎯 Speaking out · house rules for a healthy night
pairs → class
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · house rules for a healthy night

WHAT WE DO: five rules written and defended, using all four meanings

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. In pairs: write FIVE house rules for a healthy night, for a family with a fifteen-year-old. The requirement is on the board: Use all four — have to, don't have to, mustn't, should or ought to. At least one of each.
  2. Six minutes. Circulate and check the forms as they write — this is the cheap moment to catch must to and don't must.
  3. Pairs join into fours and merge their two lists into ONE list of five. That negotiation is the speaking task, and it forces disagreement in English.
  4. Each four reads its final five. The class votes on the rule they would actually keep and the rule they would break tonight.
  5. Log two or three modal errors silently; they open lesson 4.
📺 On screen: the four required modals listed on the board while they write — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: write five rules in pairs, then negotiate a merged list in fours. Everyone argues and everyone produces modals

Structure
Teenagers have to get at least nine hours' sleep. · You don't have to use the alarm on your phone. · You mustn't worry about your friends. · You ought to reduce your screen time. · You shouldn't use your phone before you go to sleep.
Form
have to = necessary · don't have to = not necessary · mustn't = very important NOT to · should / ought to = a good idea · shouldn't = a bad idea; must and should take a bare infinitive.
Pron. watch
mustn't /ˈmʌsnt/ — the first t disappears completely · ought to runs together as /ˈɔːtə/ · ache /eɪk/ with a hard k, not «atch» · cough /kɒf/ and yawn /jɔːn/ · and the everyone / anyone pair is stressed on the FIRST syllable, always
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«You mustn't take my phone.» Am I allowed to? → no, never. And «you don't have to take my phone»? → you can if you want; it is your choice. Two different worlds.
«You ought to sleep more.» Is that an order or advice? → advice — the same as should.
«I must to go» — what is wrong? → the to. Must never takes to; ought always does.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The quiz turns into ten minutes of arguing about first aidTime it hard (six minutes for reading and answering) and hold the language line: any argument has to be made in English with a verb from the page. The podcast settles the facts, not you.
mustn't and don't have to keep swappingTwo boxes on the board with a picture in each: a crossed-out phone for mustn't, a phone with a tick and a shrug for don't have to. Every disputed sentence gets pointed at a box rather than explained.
Students write «I must to sleep more»The Student's Book plants both traps in the same exercise (items 5 and 6). Do them in public, and leave the corrected forms on the board for the productive stage.
Fast finishers in the readingThe Teacher's Book task: give each other advice about improving sleep. Listen for the modals and note what to fix — it is free diagnostic data for the grammar stage.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

The modals of obligation are the other half: must, mustn't and don't have to are three different meanings that Russian collapses into two, and the exam tests exactly that gap.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
