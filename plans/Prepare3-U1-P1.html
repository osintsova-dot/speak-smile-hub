🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Describe a person's character with ten adjectives and choose between present simple and present continuous while talking about yourself.
What is genuinely hard
The form is not the problem — both tenses came up in Get Involved! A2. The hard part is CHOOSING between them at speaking speed, and keeping the answer longer than one word: at this age the thought runs ahead of the grammar.
Typical error to expect
My brother play football every day instead of plays — the dropped ‑s in the third person singular [high priority, A2/B1]
A one-word reply («Fifteen.», «Pets.») does not count as an answer at all: an answer needs a verb in it [national exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
The two emails do the work twice: first as gist and detail reading, then as the raw material students mine for the grammar table — so the rule is discovered, not announced. Vocabulary comes before reading because those ten adjectives are what the emails are made of. The productive stage sits at 74′, after two rounds of controlled practice, because fluency needs the forms to be at least warm. During that stage you say nothing about errors: correcting kills the very thing you are measuring. You log them and open the next lesson with them.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Ten words and two tenses. By the end of today you can describe anybody in this room — and talk about yourself for forty-five seconds without stopping. One rule: your answer needs a verb. One word is not an answer.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a finger-vote review of three tenses, and the bridge into the unit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. Back to the intro lessons: Three tenses on the board. Present simple, present continuous, past simple. I say a sentence — you show one, two or three fingers. Five sentences: I get up at seven / I'm holding a book / We played a game last week / She lives in Norilsk / They're talking now.
  3. Count the score out loud: Hands up if you got five.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the first unit: Books open, page ten. This unit is about people — what they are like, and what they are doing right now. Today the topic is you.
📺 On screen: three tenses written on the board in columns — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: show fingers together on your command. Expect: simple and continuous are told apart; She lives trips a few (they show 2). Typical problem — copying the neighbour: call boards up so everyone reveals at once

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two columns of markers on the board: usually · often · every week and at the moment · now · today, with an arrow and a question mark between them.
  2. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: ten personality words and a choice between two present tenses. You know both — what is new is picking the right one while you are talking about yourself and have no time to think.
  3. The key idea about answers: Only what is said out loud counts. A one-word reply is not an answer. If you don't understand, ask again (Can you repeat that, please?) — silence is the one thing that fails.
  4. Set the bar: Your answer needs a verb and two facts. One word is not an answer.
📺 On screen: a slide with the two columns of time markers (Backup: the same two columns in chalk on the board — they stay up all lesson and are needed again at 47–62′)
👦 Students do: listen; one student repeats the standard in their own words. Expect: “so we have to talk for longer”. Typical problem — “two facts” is heard as “two sentences”: show that both facts fit inside one utterance

Lead-in · the Award
desks → pairs
Why: Builds the situation and pre-teaches the three words that would otherwise block the reading. Curiosity first, language second.

WHAT WE DO: open p.10: the DofE Award poster plus the About you questions in pairs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Book open, page ten. Look at the poster and the photos. What is this?
  2. Pre-teach three words (TB p.17): volunteering — helping without money, expedition — a long trip with a plan, hiking — a long walk in the countryside.
  3. Kagan Think-Pair-Share on the About you questions: Think for twenty seconds. Then tell your partner. Then I ask two pairs.
  4. Background for you, not to be read out in full: TB p.17: The Duke of Edinburgh's Award is a charity programme founded by Prince Philip in 1956; about 300,000 participants a year in some 145 countries; three levels (Bronze, Silver, Gold); at least two years' work; finish before you turn 25.
📺 On screen: the coursebook page on the screen (Backup: work from the paper book and write the three words volunteering / expedition / hiking on the board)
👦 Students do: study the poster and answer in pairs. Expect: comparisons with school award schemes they know. Typical problem: they retell it in Russian — bring them back to the English-first rule

Vocabulary 1 · personality
pairs → class
Why: Meaning before form: they meet the words in sentences, then drill them in a mingle so the words are said out loud many times, not just read.

WHAT WE DO: ten personality adjectives: ex.3 p.10 (track 02) plus a Quiz-Quiz-Trade mingle

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Meaning first, then form. Concept check from TB p.18: Are friendly people nice or not nice?
  2. Books closed on ex.3 — in pairs they fill the ten gaps (5 minutes).
  3. Play track 02 — check and choral repetition. Key: 1 lazy · 2 active · 3 kind · 4 popular · 5 funny · 6 polite · 7 friendly · 8 quiet · 9 helpful · 10 creative.
  4. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade: everyone holds a word/definition card and mingles: Quiz your partner. Coach if he's wrong. Trade cards. Find a new partner! 4 minutes, at least five trades each.
  5. Round it off: Boards up — write the word: 'She never says a lot.'
📺 On screen: track 02 in the whiteboard app (Backup if the board is down: read the ten sentences aloud yourself and have the class repeat chorally; the Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards are printed in advance and need no technology at all)
👦 Students do: fill the gaps, then mingle with cards. Expect 7–8 out of 10 done unaided. Typical errors: polite confused with quiet; creˈative stressed wrongly — fix it with the clap

28–40′
Reading 1 · two emails
solo → pairs
Why: Gist first, then detail — the CELTA order. The texts are also the source of the grammar they will discover in the next stage, so the reading is doing double duty.

WHAT WE DO: the two emails to Mr Jones on p.11: gist first, then four detail questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the gist task BEFORE they read: Two emails. Two people. Who sounds more interesting to you — Daniel or Grace? Ninety seconds, read fast.
  2. Then ex.2 on p.10 — four questions, answer Daniel or Grace, 4 minutes.
  3. Key (TB p.18): 1 Daniel · 2 Grace · 3 Grace · 4 Daniel.
  4. The evidence rule: No line from the text — no point. Finger on the line. Kagan RallyCoach: partners take turns — one answers and points to the line, the other checks and either praises or corrects.
  5. Fast finishers (TB p.17): write down three of their own skills — I can play the piano · I can cook quite well · I am learning to draw.
📺 On screen: the emails on the screen so a line can be pointed out to everybody at once (Backup: everyone works from the book and you keep a running “who found the line” score on the board)
👦 Students do: read against the clock, then check in pairs with the evidence line. Expect question 3 (who is preparing a surprise) to cause an argument — only the line about mum's birthday settles it

Why: First free use of the new adjectives, protected by anonymity — a shy student writes about a classmate more happily than about themselves.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 p.10: write sentences about your neighbour using the new adjectives and guess who

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write two sentences about your partner. Use the new adjectives. Do NOT write the name — use 'they'. Model on the board: They don't talk very much, but they're very creative.
  2. Collect the slips and read them out — the class guesses.
  3. Micro-correction: if a mistake turns up, write the sentence on the board and let the class repair it themselves (technique from TB p.18).
📺 On screen: two model sentences on the board — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write two sentences each, then guess. Expect laughter and full attention. Typical error: He is a lazy — drop the article chorally

47–62′
Grammar · discovery
pairs → class
Why: Guided discovery, not presentation: they meet these tenses already, so the useful work is noticing the difference themselves. What they extract, they remember.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.11: pull both tenses out of the emails and formulate the rule yourselves

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two lines on the board (TB p.18 technique): Daniel plays the guitar. / Daniel is playing the guitar. Which one is present simple? Which one is now?
  2. Pairs fill the ex.1 table with examples from the emails — at least three in each column, 5 minutes.
  3. Check against: Present simple: I often make people laugh · I work hard · I play hockey and go swimming every week · I don't talk much · She always tells me I'm kind. Present continuous: I'm learning to play the keyboard · I'm also teaching my brother to swim · At the moment, I'm doing a big painting · My mum and I are planning a party for her.
  4. ex.2 — close the rule. When Daniel writes 'I work hard' — does he mean only now? (no). Key: 1 continuous · 2 simple.
  5. Collect the time markers on the board in two columns: usually · often · every week · at the weekends || at the moment · now · right now · this week · today.
📺 On screen: the “Present simple | Present continuous” table on the screen, filled in live (Backup: the same two-column table in chalk, with students coming out to write their own examples)
👦 Students do: copy examples out of the emails and derive the rule. Expect continuous to be found faster. Typical error: I like to be busy filed under continuous because of to be — unpack it aloud

62–74′
Grammar · practice
solo → pairs → class
Why: Controlled practice with immediate peer correction — the error hunt makes them justify the choice, which is what transfers to speaking.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 (choose the form) and ex.4 (six sentences with an error) against the clock, checked in pairs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 solo, 4 minutes. Kagan RallyCoach to check: Partner A explains one to three, partner B four to six. Say WHY, not just the answer.
  2. Key to ex.3: 1 watch · 2 do you usually eat · 3 'm practising · 4 are learning · 5 don't always do · 6 is teaching.
  3. ex.4 — the error hunt, 4 minutes. Kagan Showdown: the team writes the corrected sentence on a mini-whiteboard and on Showdown! everyone reveals at once.
  4. Key to ex.4: It's raining a lot at the moment. · In my free time, I usually go to the cinema. · I'm selling my English book. Would you like to buy it? · Right now, I'm watching basketball. · I usually wear a jacket, even when it's hot. · We swim and sunbathe every day..
  5. Students who need support (Mixed ability, TB p.18): work in a pair on one sentence, but explain it aloud to the class.
📺 On screen: the ex.4 sentences one at a time on the screen so the whole class repairs the same one (Backup: write the six sentences on the board before the lesson and uncover them one by one)
👦 Students do: solve, then check in pairs and reveal the boards. Expect 4–5 out of 6 caught. Typical error: they correct We are swimming every day to We swim every day and forget sunbathe

🎯 Speaking out · about yourself
pairs, rotating
Why: The productive outcome: freer practice where they use the new language for themselves, under time pressure. Errors are logged silently — correcting now would kill fluency.

WHAT WE DO: the first real conversation about themselves: 45 seconds, two facts — “usually” and “now”

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the frame: Partner voices only. Two questions each. Answer with a verb and two facts. Questions on the board: Tell me about your free time. · What are you learning at the moment?
  2. Kagan Timed RoundRobin: in fours, each student speaks for exactly 45 seconds in turn while the rest stay silent — no interrupting.
  3. Listeners hold up two fingers and drop one for each fact that arrives. An open hand at the end means the answer did not reach the bar.
  4. Second round — new fours, same question. The target is simply: longer and without pauses.
  5. Log errors silently on your correction sheet; do not correct aloud — the feedback slot is the start of the next lesson.
📺 On screen: the two questions and a 45-second timer on the screen (Backup: questions on the board and you time it on the wall clock, calling “stop” yourself)
👦 Students do: speak in turn against the clock while listeners count facts on their fingers. Expect the second round to be visibly longer. Typical error: I'm playing football every week — note it, say nothing

WB Unit 1 (p.4–7)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can describe a person's character with 10 adjectives and choose between present simple and present continuous when they talk about themselves.
  • Subsidiary. Reading two emails for gist and for detail (SB p.10 ex.2); guided discovery of the grammar from ex.1–2 on p.11; a written model email, ex.6.
  • Personal. Do not explain the rule until students have pulled the examples out of the emails themselves. During the speaking stage log tense errors silently — delayed feedback opens the next lesson.
By the end, students can…
  • describe a classmate with 3–4 personality adjectives
  • say what they usually do and what they are doing now
  • correct a wrong tense in a sentence and explain why
  • give a 45-second answer about themselves without one-word replies

Form
present simple: I/you/we/they + V, he/she/it + V-s; negative don't / doesn't. present continuous: am/is/are + V-ing.
Pron. watch
adjective stress: ˈactive · ˈfriendly · ˈpopular · creˈative (stress on the second syllable!) · poˈlite (second syllable) · Edinburgh /ˈed.ɪn.bər.ə/ (TB p.17).
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«It rains a lot at the moment» — what's wrong? → at the moment = now, so It's raining a lot at the moment.
«Daniel writes: I work hard.» Does he mean only today? → no, it's always true (TB, question to ex.2).
«I'm usually wearing a jacket» — why not? → usually = regularly → I usually wear a jacket.

Assumptions: SB p.10–11, syllabus lesson 4. Both tenses are familiar after Get Involved! A2 — what is new is choosing between them and stretching the answer. Of the ten adjectives they usually already know five or six (friendly, funny, kind, quiet, lazy).

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
“We know this already” the moment they hear the word grammarDon't argue: give ex.4 on p.11 (six sentences with mistakes) against the clock. They normally catch 3–4 out of 6 — after that they work without commentary.
creative and polite stressed on the first syllableBeat it out with your hand: cre-A-tive, po-LITE. Three choral repetitions with the clap, then individually.
One-word answers in the speaking stageThe rule of the lesson is one word is not an answer: an answer counts if it contains a verb. The partner holds up two fingers — the second fact is compulsory.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
