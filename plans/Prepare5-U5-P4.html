🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use seven fixed phrases with at (at least, at first, at once, at risk, at present, at long last, at its best) and take part in a discussion: give an opinion, ask for your partner's, ask for repetition, refer back, and keep it going for three minutes without dying.
What is genuinely hard
Fixed phrases cannot be worked out — at first and at last look like a pair and are not, and Russian uses a different preposition for most of them. The speaking half is harder than it looks: teenagers can give an opinion but not ask for one, and a discussion in which both candidates only state opinions scores badly in both exams.
Typical error to expect
«In first I didn't like it», «at the end I decided» — the preposition is fixed and there is nothing to reason about; at first is the beginning, at last is the end.
The discussion where nobody asks anything. Two candidates who take turns making statements are not having a discussion, and the criteria say so explicitly.
Why the lesson is built this way
The phrases are done fast and physically because they are memory, not understanding — matched, drilled, then hidden in true/false sentences about the students themselves, so that every phrase gets used in a sentence that is worth listening to. The listening is a video call between two students planning a project, which is exactly the register of the speaking task that follows. Then the discussion skills arrive as a judgement task — seven pieces of advice, five good and two bad — and the class works out why the two are bad, which teaches more than a list of phrases. The final stage is a real three-minute discussion in threes with an observer holding a tally sheet.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two people who take turns saying what they think are not having a discussion. The examiner is listening for something else — the questions. Today: seven phrases with «at», and the five things that keep a conversation alive when you have run out of opinions.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a two-minute conditional chain that everybody contributes to, and the bridge into the at-phrases

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what are you doing this evening? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — yesterday's conditionals, in one chain. Start it yourself: If we don't protect the rainforest, the orangutans will lose their habitat.
  3. Kagan RallyRobin round the room, one sentence each, each one starting from the end of the last. Two minutes, no repeats.
  4. Stop it whenever it breaks and repair the break publicly — the break is always will after if.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page sixty, the right-hand side. Seven phrases, and not one of them can be worked out.
📺 On screen: the chain written on the board as it grows, in short form — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everybody adds one conditional to a live chain, under pressure

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and split the lesson honestly into two halves

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: matched, drilled as chunks, then used

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1, two minutes: the seven phrases matched to their meanings — at least (not less than), at long last (finally), at first (in the beginning), at its best (as good as it can be), at risk (in danger), at once (immediately), at present (now).
  2. Write them on a card strip as WHOLE CHUNKS and drill them chorally four times, fast, as single words: /ətˈfɜːst/, /ətˈwʌns/, /ətˈliːst/.
  3. ex.2 individually, three minutes: seven gaps. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together — at its best, at once, at present, At first, At least, at risk, At long last.
  4. The two that always swap: at first and at long last. Put them at opposite ends of the board and point at the correct one every time they are confused.
  5. Fast finishers: two more gapped sentences for the class.
📺 On screen: the seven-phrase card strip, written and drilled — it is card, so nothing can fail
👦 Students do: match, drill as chunks four times, then produce seven answers alone

pairs → standing
Why: Vocabulary 2 · true or false about me

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: the pairwork on SB p.135, played as a guessing game

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3, the pairwork on SB p.135: everyone writes four sentences about themselves using four different at-phrases. Two true, two false. Four minutes, silent.
  2. Model it yourself first with two of your own — one true and one obviously false — and let the class guess. Thirty seconds, and it sets the tone.
  3. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade, four minutes: everyone stands, reads one sentence to a partner, the partner guesses true or false and then has to ASK ONE QUESTION about it. That question is the bridge into the second half of the lesson.
  4. Three partners each. Sit and take two of the best false sentences to the floor.
📺 On screen: the seven-phrase strip, visible during the mingle — nothing to fail
👦 Students do: write four personal sentences, then read and question three partners standing

Move break · beginning or end
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that fixes the two phrases people swap

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. Two ends of the room: BEGINNING and END. You say a sentence with at first or at long last and they walk to the right end. Eight sentences, fast.
  2. Then the harder round: you say a situation (You waited forty minutes for a bus) and they call the phrase (at long last).
  3. Last thirty seconds in pairs: One thing you didn't like at first and like now.
  4. Freeze and sit: Page sixty-one. Two students are planning a project on a video call — and they disagree.
📺 On screen: the two ends of the room named and pointed at — no technology, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone moves on every sentence and produces one personal at-first sentence

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: Lola and Pete planning a project on endangered species (track 39)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 before any audio, thirty seconds: look at the picture. What have they been asked to do? Possible answer — prepare a project about endangered species.
  2. Read the five questions aloud before playing, and let the class predict two of the answers. Prediction is the habit that makes listening work.
  3. Play track 39 once, whole. Ninety seconds in pairs to compare.
  4. Play it a second time, and this time stop after each answer is reached — the class tells you when to stop, which keeps everyone listening.
  5. Check: humans are destroying its habitat · Lola wants animals with something in common · sea animals live in water most of their lives and Pete wants them included · they will start by explaining the word endangered · a turtle, a whale and a seal.
  6. Then the observation that sets up the whole speaking half, said once: Listen to what Pete does when he disagrees. He asks a question. That's the whole technique.
📺 On screen: the five questions on the board with the class's two predictions next to them. Backup: if the audio fails, read the conversation from the audioscript (TB p.294) with a student taking one of the two parts
👦 Students do: predict, listen twice with a stop-me job, then notice a technique rather than a fact

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: seven pieces of advice judged, and the two bad ones explained

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 in pairs, three minutes: seven pieces of advice about taking part in a discussion. Five are good, two are not. Which two, and WHY?
  2. Answer: 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6 are good; 2 and 7 are wrong — and the reason is the same for both. They stop the other person speaking.
  3. Do not give the answer. Take a vote on each of the seven with hands up, then let two students argue for and against the disputed ones before you confirm.
  4. Write the principle on the board in six words: A discussion is not two monologues.
  5. Then the practical version, also on the board: Opinion → reason → question to your partner. That three-part shape is what the rest of the lesson practises.
📺 On screen: the seven pieces of advice with the class's votes next to them — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: judge seven statements in pairs and argue about two of them in public

WHAT WE DO: ex.2: seven phrases matched to five functions, then drilled in a fishbowl

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.2, three minutes: the seven Prepare to speak phrases matched to what they DO — asking for repetition, asking for explanation, referring back, encouraging someone to continue, changing the subject.
  2. Check: 1 e, 2 a, 3 d, 4 b, 5 a, 6 d, 7 c. Then write the seven on the second card strip and put it on every desk.
  3. Fishbowl, four minutes: two volunteers discuss one question in the middle of the room (Should zoos exist?) while everyone else holds a tally sheet and counts the phrases they hear. Two minutes only.
  4. Debrief the tally in ninety seconds: how many questions did they ask each other? How many phrases from the strip? No criticism of the volunteers — the numbers do the teaching.
  5. Then the drill in pairs, three minutes: A gives an opinion, B must respond using a phrase from the strip before giving their own. Swap after each exchange.
📺 On screen: the seven-phrase discussion strip on every desk, and the tally on the board after the fishbowl — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: match seven functions, count phrases during a live fishbowl, then drill the responses in pairs

🎯 Speaking out · three minutes, in threes
groups of three
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · three minutes, in threes

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 = B1 Speaking Part 4: a real discussion, with an observer counting

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of three. Roles: two speakers, one observer with a tally sheet (columns: opinions given · questions asked · phrases used).
  2. The questions come from ex.3 and they are about animals, the environment and what people should do — the content the whole unit has built, which is why this stage works at all.
  3. Round 1: three minutes of discussion, then ninety seconds of feedback from the observer, using the numbers only. You gave five opinions and asked one question. He asked four.
  4. Rotate roles. Round 2, three minutes, new question. Rotate again. Round 3, three minutes. Everybody speaks twice and observes once.
  5. The rule that is enforced in every round, written on the board: At least three questions each. Silence longer than five seconds and the observer points at the strip.
  6. Take the tally sheets in at the end — they are your speaking record for the term, and they are more precise than any impression you would form standing at the front.
  7. Close with the count: Nine minutes of discussion in this room. In the exam it is three.
📺 On screen: the tally sheet design drawn on the board once, before round 1 — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: discuss for six minutes in total, observe for three, and receive feedback as numbers rather than as opinions
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE tasks 39–40 (the spoken part). Giving an opinion, asking your partner and referring back is the behaviour the oral criteria reward, and the tally sheet measures it directly.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: record one group of three on a phone during round 2 and play thirty seconds back with the tally next to it. Two minutes, taken from round 3, and worth more than any feedback given live.

Structure
At first I thought it was boring. · We should do something at once. · At least three species are at risk. · Going back to what you said … · Do you mean …? · Sorry, what were you going to say?
Form
fixed prepositional phrases with at (no article changes, no variation) · discussion functions: asking for repetition, asking for explanation, referring back, encouraging, changing the subject.
Pron. watch
the at-phrases are said as single words — /ətˈfɜːst/, /ətˈwʌns/, /ətˈliːst/ — and a student who says them as two separate words sounds like a beginner · Sorry, what were you going to say? runs together as /wɒtwəjəɡənəseɪ/
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«at first» — is that the beginning or the end? → the beginning. And «at last»? → the end, after waiting.
«at once» — in a minute, or now? → now, immediately.
In a discussion, is it enough to say what I think? → no. I have to ask what YOU think, or the mark drops. Both exams.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The at-phrases are half-remembered and half-inventedThey go on a strip in fixed form and are drilled as whole chunks, never as at + word. Any invented version (in first, at the last) is answered with the correct chunk said twice, not with an explanation.
One student dominates the discussionThrees, not pairs, and the third student holds a tally sheet counting who spoke and who ASKED. Roles rotate, so everybody observes once — and observing is where the learning happens.
The discussion dies after ninety secondsThe phrase strip is on the desk, face up, during the whole task, and the observer's job includes pointing at it silently when there is a gap. Nobody rescues in Russian.
Fast finishers in the vocabulary stageThe Teacher's Book task: write two more gapped sentences on the at-phrases for the class. They get used in the check.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 39–40 (the spoken part) and tasks 1–9 (listening).

Speaking Part 4 is a discussion: give an opinion, ask for your partner's, come back to something that was said, and keep it going. The Russian oral rewards precisely that behaviour in the dialogue task, and the phrases learned today (Going back to…, Do you mean…?, Like I said before…) are the ones examiners hear as fluency rather than as filler.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
