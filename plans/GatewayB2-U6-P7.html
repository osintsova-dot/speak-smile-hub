🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Work through four exam tasks in one lesson — a four-option multiple-choice reading on a long narrative, a two-part collaborative speaking task, a five-speaker matching listening, and a 140–190-word reply email — and name the strategy that each of them actually rewards.
What is genuinely hard
Each of the four tasks fails for its own reason, and the reasons are mechanical rather than linguistic. Multiple choice fails when the student picks the option containing a word they recognise from the text. Matching fails when they stop listening after the first plausible letter. The speaking task fails when the pair agrees in forty seconds. And the email fails when one of the three questions in the prompt goes unanswered — which is invisible to the writer and fatal to the mark.
Typical error to expect
Answering two of the three questions in the email prompt. The writer feels finished because the text is long enough and the register is right; the examiner sees a task not completed and the content mark drops however good the English is. The fix is mechanical: number the questions in the prompt, then number your own paragraphs against them before you write a word.
Why the lesson is built this way
This is the closest the course comes to an exam morning, so the design is honest about time rather than comprehensive. Each task is preceded by its own Exam tip read WITH the class, run under a real clock, and followed by a check that asks for the evidence and not the letter. The topic is continuous — the flood story, sports courses, keeping fit — so the vocabulary of Units 5 and 6 keeps working. The Collaborative project on p.83 is not part of the exam section and is left as an option, not squeezed in.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“A boy watches a river rise past his window, gets his family onto the roof, and builds a raft out of a door floating past. Six questions. Then you negotiate, then you listen to five people who did a sports course, then you answer an email from a friend who is bored of the gym. Four exam tasks in ninety minutes — and for each one I am going to tell you the single thing that decides it.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, ninety seconds of cross-unit recycling, and the premise of an exam-strategy lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one full sentence back: One sentence — how did you sleep last night, and how do you feel about it? Any answer counts as long as it is in English and it is a sentence.
  2. 2 minutes — two units in six sentences. Kagan RallyRobin in pairs: take turns, six sentences in ninety seconds, three of them using money vocabulary from Unit 5 and three using health vocabulary from Unit 6 — and every sentence about the same person.
  3. 1 minute — the strategy question. Ask one thing and take three answers: In the test you did last lesson, what lost you marks — the English, or the way you read the question? Almost everybody says the second, and that is the lesson's premise.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): point at their own answer: Today is not about new English. It is four exam tasks and four decisions about how to read them. Page eighty-two.
📺 On screen: nothing to project in the first five minutes — paper and voices only
👦 Students do: produce six sentences each spanning both units, then name what actually cost them marks

WHAT WE DO: the Reading exam tip read together, then ex.1 to activate the topic

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the Reading exam tip on p.82 WITH them, not to them: read the text first to get the topic and the general meaning, then identify which SECTION of the text each question relates to.
  2. Turn it into one instruction they can actually follow: Never answer a question from memory. Go back, find the lines, compare all four options against them.
  3. Add the trap they met in the listening in lesson 36 — it works identically in reading: The option that repeats the text's own words is usually the wrong one.

  5. Set the reading up in one sentence and then stop talking: A boy, a river and one night. Read it once, fast, before you look at a single question.

Why: Reading · six questions, four options each

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 under a real clock: the narrative read for gist, then question by question with evidence

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three minutes, silent, questions covered: read the whole story once. Nothing else. Call time.
  2. Then ten minutes for the six questions, individually, clock visible. The rule of the day, written on the board: Next to every answer, the line number that proves it.
  3. Pairs compare for two minutes — line numbers first, letters second, exactly as in lesson 1.
  4. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: 1 B · 2 B · 3 A · 4 B · 5 D · 6 A. The nominated student reads the proving line aloud and only then says the letter.
  5. Spend the last ninety seconds on the two items the class split on — usually 4 and 6 — and ask a different question about them: What would have to be in the text for option C to be right? That question teaches more than the answer does.
  6. Name the strategy out loud before moving on, in one sentence they can repeat: compare every option against the lines, never against your memory.
📺 On screen: the clock and the six answers as they are confirmed. Backup: the whole task is printed in the book, so if nothing can be projected the board carries the finishing time and the stage runs unchanged
👦 Students do: read a long narrative once for gist, answer six four-option questions with line references, and defend two disputed items

Move break · four corners, four risks
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Seventeen-year-olds who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies quietly.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that changes the room and the skill at the same time

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Four corners of the room: A RAFT · A BOAT · THE ROOF · RUN FOR THE BRIDGE. You ask What would you have done that night? and everybody walks.
  2. One person per corner justifies it in a third conditional — the grammar of Unit 6 doing real work: If I had been there, I would have … Four sentences, thirty seconds each.
  3. Then one mix: Kagan Mix-Pair-Share, clap, pair, and answer the question that opens the next task — If your school suddenly had money, what would you spend it on? Fifteen seconds each, two partners.
  4. That question is literally the speaking task on p.146, so the stirrer doubles as the lead-in and nothing is wasted.
  5. Freeze and sit, books open at page one hundred and forty-six.
📺 On screen: nothing to project — the corners are named aloud and the room does the work
👦 Students do: commit to a decision physically, justify it in a third conditional and pre-load the speaking topic with two partners

WHAT WE DO: ex.5: the pair judges itself on the four criteria the book prints, then fixes one thing

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

Why: Listening · five speakers, eight options

WHAT WE DO: ex.6 on track ES3: matching where the answer is always a paraphrase

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the Listening exam tip on p.146 with them: read each statement quickly BEFORE you listen and think about what you expect to hear.
  2. Ninety seconds of silence to read options A–H and underline the key idea in each. Remind them of the arithmetic: eight options, five speakers, THREE letters left over — so a plausible letter is not a used letter.
  3. Play track ES3 once. Students answer alone.
  4. Thirty seconds to compare in pairs — but only the letters they are SURE of. Then play track ES3 a second time for the rest.
  5. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: 1 E, 2 A, 3 G, 4 C, 5 H. For each one the nominated student says the paraphrase that proved it — speaker 1 said the course was «too much», speaker 5 said they «went right back to the basics».
  6. ex.7 to close, ninety seconds in pairs: if you could do a sports course, which sport would you choose and why? It puts the vocabulary back into their own mouths before the writing.
📺 On screen: track ES3 in the board app, played exactly twice. Backup: if the audio fails, the class audio script is in the Teacher's Book — read it aloud yourself twice, changing your voice for the five speakers; the task is identical
👦 Students do: read eight options before listening, answer alone across two plays, and justify each answer with the paraphrase
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE tasks 3–9 and the matching task that opens the Russian listening paper. Eight options for five speakers is the same arithmetic as the Russian task, and the same discipline saves it: read every option first, underline the idea rather than the words, and accept that the right answer will be a paraphrase. The extra letters exist precisely to catch the candidate who stops listening early.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: after the check, take the three UNUSED letters and ask what a speaker would have to say for each of them to be right. Three minutes, taken from ex.7, which works as a thirty-second question instead of ninety.

Why: 🎯 The reply email

WHAT WE DO: ex.8–9 on p.146: Cory's email answered in 140–190 words, every question covered

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.8 first, two minutes in open class, because it generates the content: How important is it to take exercise to stay healthy? Give reasons. Take four answers and leave the best three on the board.
  2. Then read Cory's email together and NUMBER the three questions in the book: what do you do to keep fit, why do you like doing it, do you think it is important for us to keep fit. Ninety seconds, and it is the stage that saves the content mark.
  3. Put the plan on the board from the Teacher's Book questions and make them answer it in pairs, ninety seconds: what kind of text, who to, what register, what three questions, how to open, how to close, how many words. Answers: an email · to a pen friend called Cory · informal · the three numbered questions · open by thanking him and sympathising that getting fit has been boring · close by hoping the answers helped or wishing him luck · 140–190 words.
  4. 🎯 Ten minutes, silent, palm down: write the email. Circulate and ask one question only, in a whisper: Which of Cory's questions is this paragraph answering?
  5. Checkpoint at eight minutes: stop, count the words, write the number in the margin, and tick off the three numbered questions against your own paragraphs.
  6. Note the register difference out loud before they start, because it reverses yesterday's rule: This one is informal. Contractions are not a mistake here — they are expected.
📺 On screen: the three best reasons from ex.8 and the seven-point plan stay on the board. Backup: everything needed is printed on p.146, so the stage runs unchanged with a blank screen
👦 Students do: generate content aloud, number the prompt's questions, plan against them and write a 140–190-word informal email

WHAT WE DO: ex.10: partner checking against the Writing checklist on p.141, with the numbered questions first

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap emails. First job, sixty seconds, and it is the one that matters: find question 1, question 2 and question 3 in your partner's email and write the numbers in the margin. A missing number is a missing mark.

Structure
The answer is B because line 4 says … · Shall we go for …? · My choice would be … · Speaker 3 said …, which is a paraphrase of G · Thanks for your email. · As for your question about … · Write back soon.
Form
the reply email: informal register, contractions ALLOWED and expected, an opening that reacts to the friend's news, one paragraph per question, three questions of your own if the prompt asks for them, a closing line · 140–190 words in the Cambridge format on p.146.
Pron. watch
in the listening the answers are carried by weak forms and linking, which is why a repeated content word is almost never the answer · in the speaking task the invitation questions must RISE (What do you think? Shall we choose that one?) or they sound like an order rather than an invitation.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Option A uses three words that are in the text. Is that a good sign?» → no, it is usually the trap. What does the right option usually do? → say the same thing in different words.
«You have finished the discussion in forty seconds. What went wrong?» → we agreed instead of exploring. What could you have said after «I agree»? → «… but what about …?»
«Cory asks three questions. How many paragraphs does your email need?» → at least three plus an opening and a closing. Count the questions before you count the words.

Likely problemWhat you do
Time collapses and the writing gets four minutesThe clock is the plan. Write the four finishing times on the board at minute five — reading ends at 33, speaking at 55, listening at 67, writing at 82 — and hold them even if a task is unfinished. An unfinished task is data; a missing task is not.

The pair reaches agreement in forty seconds againThe Exam success tip on p.146 says it plainly and so should you: they must speak about ALL the ideas for two minutes and neither of them should decide anything until the second part. Two minutes is the instruction, not a suggestion.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 12–18 (four-option multiple choice on a long text), tasks 3–9 (matching what five speakers say) and task 37 (the reply email, 100–140 words in the Russian format).

This is the closest the course comes to a full exam morning, and the three tasks fail for three different reasons. Multiple choice fails when the student picks the option containing a word they recognise from the text. Matching fails when the student stops listening after the first plausible letter. The email fails when a question in the prompt goes unanswered — the single most common way Russian candidates lose К1. Say all three out loud before the paper starts, not after.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
