🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Negotiate to a decision with a partner — ask for an opinion, agree partially, disagree politely, and finish with a conclusion — and write a story that uses a variety of past tenses, participle clauses and sequence linkers.
What is genuinely hard
Two opposite failures in the speaking task: agreeing immediately (the conversation dies in forty seconds) and disagreeing flatly (which sounds aggressive in English and costs the interactive mark). The English solution is partial agreement, and Russian has no everyday equivalent of «I agree up to a point, but …». In the writing, the difficulty is tense variety: students tell the whole story in the past simple and it reads like a list.
Typical error to expect
«I completely disagree» as an opening move — grammatically perfect, pragmatically wrong; and «Yes, I agree» repeated four times, which ends the task early. In the story: eight consecutive past simple verbs with no past perfect, no continuous and no linker except «and then».
Why the lesson is built this way
The speaking half starts from a recording of two students doing exactly the task the class will do, and the second listening asks five yes/no questions about HOW they did it — did they listen, did they respond, did they speak the same amount. That converts a listening into a set of criteria the students then apply to themselves. The writing half reuses the tenses of the last two lessons: Sam's story is analysed for the five things the Writing bank names, and the students hunt for each in the model before they plan their own. The first two paragraphs are written in class, so the teacher sees the tense variety before it goes home.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two students, one diagram, five minutes, one decision. If you agree with everything your partner says, you both lose. And then a story: a young doctor, a concert, and an accident on stage.”

desks
Why: Turning the model into a checklist means the students evaluate before they perform, which is the only reliable way to change how a pair task sounds.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.26 ex.2–3 (track 09): two students doing the exact task

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Look at the diagram together — five reasons for a weekend job around a central question — so the listening has a shape.
  2. First play, ex.2: what does the examiner ask first? what is the second question? what do they choose? Key: how important the reasons are · which is most important · «learn new skills».
  3. Before the second play, hand over the criteria: Now you are the examiner. Five yes/no questions about HOW they did it.
  4. Second play, ex.3. Key: all five are yes — they asked each other's opinion, talked about all the reasons, reached a decision, responded to each other and spoke about the same amount.
  5. Land it: Those five sentences are the mark scheme. Not the ideas — the behaviour.
📺 On screen: track 09, played twice, and the diagram from p.26. Backup: read the dialogue from the Teacher's Book script, p.125, with a strong student taking the second voice.
👦 Students do: answer three content questions, then judge the speakers against five criteria

25–32′
The Speaking bank · asking, agreeing, disagreeing
desks → board
Why: Functional phrases are stored by function; intonation is what makes the polite disagreement actually polite, and it has to be heard and copied.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.26 ex.4: three categories, ten expressions, and the intonation that makes them polite

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ex.4: give the three sections their titles. Key: 1 asking for an opinion · 2 agreeing · 3 disagreeing.
  2. Point out what the Teacher's Book points out: every disagreement in the list is soft. There is no «I completely disagree» anywhere on the page, and that is deliberate.
  3. Drill the intonation, chorally, twice each: Do you agree? ↗ · Don't you think so? ↗ · What do you think? ↘ · What about you? ↘
  4. Then a 60-second drill: you say a category, they produce an expression. Eight rounds, whole class, fast.
  5. Elicit the grammar point: after «What about …?» and «What do you think about …?» comes a noun or an -ing form.
📺 On screen: the three category headings on the board with two examples each. Backup: the bank is printed on p.26 — run the drill from the page.
👦 Students do: title three categories, repeat four intonation patterns, produce expressions on demand

🎯 Negotiate to a decision
desks in pairs
Why: Two rounds with a debrief between them is what makes the second one measurably better — and the self-assessment is the transfer.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.26 ex.5–6: the same task, then a harder one with a new partner

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ex.5 first, three minutes: the same diagram, their own opinions, then a decision about the most important reason.
  2. Debrief against the five criteria — the students score themselves, not you: did you discuss all of them, did you both speak, did you disagree politely, did you decide?
  3. Ex.6 with a NEW partner and the harder diagram (salary, travel opportunities, job security, working hours, chances of promotion). Three minutes minimum, then one minute to decide.
  4. Monitor with a tally sheet: count who uses a Speaking bank phrase. Read out the count at the end — not the names, the count.
  5. Support: the quiet partner gets a slip with two questions on it and must use both.
📺 On screen: a visible timer for each three-minute round. Backup: a phone stopwatch on a desk.
👦 Students do: negotiate twice with two different partners and self-assess against five criteria

WHAT WE DO: SB p.27 ex.1–3: predict, then read the model

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

The order of events
desks
Why: Sequencing a model is how the structure gets into the students' hands before they have to invent one.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.27 ex.4a–4b: eight events to sequence, three parts to name

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ex.4a alone, four minutes: put eight events in order. Warn them, as the Teacher's Book does, that the wording of the events is NOT the wording of the story. Key: 1 h · 2 d · 3 g · 4 e · 5 b · 6 f · 7 a · 8 c.

61–70′
Hunting the Writing bank
desks
Why: A model is only useful when the students find the features themselves; the participle clause needs an explicit drill because it is the one genuinely new structure.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.27 ex.5: find each of the five techniques in the model

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the Writing bank together, then hunt one point at a time — the Teacher's Book method and it works.
  2. Past tenses: had always wanted, had been studying, used to imagine, applied, were playing. Participle clauses: After finishing his degree, Having seen the accident. Time expressions: That weekend, At 8.30 pm. Sequence linkers: After just five minutes, Ten minutes later. Adjectives and adverbs: amazed, awful, incredibly, Unbelievably.
  3. Teach the participle clause explicitly, with the trap: After coming home, Theo had a sandwich ✔ — same subject. After coming home, there was nobody there ✘.
  4. Ninety-second drill: rewrite three sentences with a participle clause. He finished his degree and then applied. → After finishing his degree, he applied.
📺 On screen: the five Writing-bank headings on the board with one example found by the students under each. Backup: the bank is printed on p.27.
👦 Students do: find five techniques in the model, then rewrite three sentences with participle clauses

Why: Writing begun in class can still be influenced; the underlining requirement converts «use a variety of tenses» from advice into a checkable instruction.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.27 ex.6a–6b: their own story, started where you can see it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ex.6a, four minutes: the task is the same one — a story beginning with the given sentence, including a trip and an accident. Plan four paragraphs in note form on the board skeleton.
  2. RallyCoach, two minutes: swap plans, and the partner checks ONE thing — are there clearly a beginning, a middle and an end?
  3. Eight minutes of silent writing: paragraphs 1 and 2 only. Silent mode. No questions during the timer.
  4. The visible requirement, on the board: UNDERLINE one past perfect, one past continuous and one participle clause in what you write.
  5. Walk the room and put a pencil dot where a story goes flat (three past simples in a row). No commentary.
  6. Announce the finish: The rest is homework — Workbook page nineteen, exercise seven, 140 to 180 words, and the first sentence is given to you.
📺 On screen: a visible timer for the eight silent minutes, and the three-underline requirement on the board. Backup: a phone stopwatch.
👦 Students do: plan four paragraphs, check a partner's plan, then write two paragraphs in silence with three underlined structures

Structure
What do you think about earning money? · I agree up to a point, but … · I see what you mean, but … · So shall we say that learning new skills is the most important? · Having seen the accident, Sam ran to the stage.
Form
negotiating: question → opinion → partial agreement → counter-idea → joint conclusion · participle clauses: After finishing his degree, he applied … / Having seen the accident, he ran … (only when the subject of both clauses is the same) · sequence linkers: At first, Next, Then, After that, Finally, In the end.
Pron. watch
yes/no questions rise: Do you agree? ↗ Don't you think so? ↗ · wh- questions fall: What do you think? ↘ What about you? ↘ · linking: «I agree_up to_a point»
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I agree up to a point.» Do I agree completely? → no, partly — and that is what keeps the conversation alive.
«Having seen the accident, Sam ran to the stage.» Who saw and who ran? → the same person. Is that necessary? → yes, always.
ICQ before the negotiation: do you have to agree in the end? → you have to reach a decision. Do you have to agree quickly? → no, the opposite.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The decision is reached in forty secondsName it before they start and set a floor, not a ceiling: Three minutes minimum. You may not agree in the first minute. The Exam tip says the same thing.
One student does eighty per cent of the talkingGive the quiet partner the two questions from the Speaking bank on a slip: «What do you think?» and «Don't you think so?». Their job is to use both. It changes the balance mechanically.
The story is written entirely in the past simpleThe requirement is made visible before writing: at least one past perfect, one past continuous and one participle clause, underlined by the writer. Underlining is what makes it checkable.
Participle clauses with two different subjectsThe Teacher's Book warning, boarded: After coming home, Theo had a sandwich ✔ / After coming home, there was nobody in the living room ✘.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 39–40 (the oral part — the joint task in particular) and task 38 (the written project, where a position must be reached).

Negotiating with a partner is a Cambridge format rather than a Russian one, but the two skills it builds are exactly what the Russian oral rewards: agreeing partially instead of totally («I agree up to a point, but …»), and reaching a conclusion instead of stopping when the time is up. The story writing has no Russian twin — but the past tenses it drills are the backbone of every narrative gap in tasks 19–25.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
