🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Place too and enough correctly (too busy · long enough · enough exercise) and write a ~100-word informal email that answers all four of a friend's points — with an informal opening and ending, short forms and informal words.
What is genuinely hard
too/enough is a POSITION problem: Russian «достаточно высокий» puts enough first, English says tall enough — but enough money. And in the writing the hard part is task discipline: Jamal himself missed one of Mark's questions, and so will half the class unless the plan stage catches it.
Typical error to expect
enough tall · money enough — Russian «достаточно высокий» puts enough BEFORE the adjective, English puts it after (tall enough) but before nouns (enough money) [A2/B1]
an exam email opened like an essay (Dear Sir… I want to tell you…) or closed with nothing at all — register and the opening/closing phrases are marked separately [national exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
The grammar half is deliberately loud (whiteboards, gestures) because the writing half needs quiet — the lesson spends its energy budget early. The model email is read as MAIL first (did Jamal answer his friend?) — and the discovery that he missed the room question teaches more about exam task-reading than any warning. Then the model is mined: opening and closing phrases, six short forms, six informal words — every tool touched in the last half hour before students write. The four-note plan is compulsory: no plan, no pen. Peer review against the same checklist closes the loop, and the TB's requirement (one comparative + one each of too / not enough / not as… as) recycles lesson 1 inside the letter.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Your best friend moved away and just wrote to you. Today you write back — a real letter, a hundred words, that answers everything your friend asked. The trap? The boy in the book forgot ONE question. Find it, and never make his mistake.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts. The rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a three-sentence repair clinic from last lesson's speaking stage, and the bridge into too/enough

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — error clinic from the best-age stage. Three anonymous sentences from your lesson-1 log on the board (a double marker, a backwards not as… as, an opinion without a because). Kagan RallyRobin in pairs: find and fix all three, then hands up.
  3. Repair together and be generous: These are OUR mistakes — and they're already better than last week.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page twenty-two, bottom half. Three small words — too and enough — and then you write a letter a friend would actually read.
📺 On screen: three faulty sentences in chalk — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: hunt the errors in pairs and fix them. Expect the backwards not as… as to be found last — it hides best

Vocabulary · too and enough
pairs → class
Why: Three example sentences carry the whole system: the only genuinely hard thing is WHERE enough stands, and that is a position problem Russian gets backwards — so position is drilled, not translated.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: three sentences → three position rules; ex.2: controlled choice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open — the three example sentences from Emily's world: The days aren't long enough! · They don't do enough exercise. · Teenage life is too busy. Pairs extract the rules a–c, 2 minutes. Key: rules from the three examples: a too — BEFORE the adjective (sentence 3, too busy) · b enough — AFTER adj/adv (sentence 1, long enough) · c enough — BEFORE nouns (sentence 2, enough exercise).
  2. Rule on the board with arrows: too goes BEFORE the adjective (too busy) · enough goes AFTER an adjective or adverb (long enough) · but BEFORE a noun (enough exercise).
  3. Concept checks from the plan header — the shoes one and the time one, cold-called.
  4. ex.2 solo, 3 minutes, pair check. Key: 1 weren't big enough, felt too tight · 2 didn't have enough time, was too tired · 3 too slow, hasn't got enough memory. Watch item 3: too slow AND enough memory in one reply — both positions in one breath.
📺 On screen: the position rule with arrows in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: extract the rules, then choose. Expect the adjective/noun split to need one extra example (old enough vs enough money — say both, point at the arrows). Typical error: enough big
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare4_Unit3_Vocabulary2_Too_Enough_A5
A5 grammar-vocabulary card — HOW MUCH IS ENOUGH?: four structures on one sheet — TOO + adjective (more than we need, a problem: He's too young to vote.) · adjective + ENOUGH (as much as we need: She's old enough to vote.) · NOT + adjective + ENOUGH (less than we need: He isn't old enough to drive.) · ENOUGH / NOT ENOUGH + noun (We have enough time. · There aren't enough buses.) — with the WORD ORDER box too + adjective · adjective + enough · enough + noun
  • Glue the card in — the word order box is the whole rule, mark it.
  • Colour the three positions of enough: after an adjective, before a noun.
  • Write four sentences about yourself, one per structure.
  • Check each one: is it too much, just enough, or not enough?
When: ~5 min at the start of the “Vocabulary · too and enough” stagePrep: 1 × A5 per pupil. Scissors, glue sticks, pencils.

Too/enough pairwork
pairs → teams
Why: Retrieval straight after input, in speech and on whiteboards: the position rule must survive real sentences before it goes into a letter.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 (SB p.120) + a whiteboard round of real-life complaints

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 pairwork from p.120 as the book sets it — 4 minutes. Teacher's note: ex.3 pairwork on SB p.120: the positive sentence is item 4; students answer for themselves — enough works in positives too, often with an infinitive (I have enough money to buy…).
  2. Kagan Showdown on mini-whiteboards — you call real situations, teams write the complaint: The maths test had forty questions, you finished twenty… (We didn't have enough time!) · The jeans are size S, you take M… (They aren't big enough! / They're too small!) · Your phone is from 2015… (It's too slow!) Six rounds, show on three.
  3. Score on the board; a team names the rule that earned each point.
📺 On screen: situations called aloud, answers on mini-whiteboards — voice and boards are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: complain correctly at speed. Expect the two possible answers (too small / not big enough) to surface — celebrate it: same fact, two frames. Typical error: time enough

Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: 90 seconds of too/enough gestures + a breath before the quiet half

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. Hands ABOVE your head = too. Hand flat at your chest = enough. Hands at your knees = not enough. Call fast: The bag weighs twenty kilos! (too) · You slept nine hours. (enough) · One laptop for ten pupils! (not enough)
  2. Sit them down with the volume dial: The loud half of the lesson is over. From here — writers' room rules.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: vote with their bodies, laugh, then land in the quiet half. Expect the not-enough-laptops item to get a knowing groan — it returns in Jamal's letter in five minutes

Model email
solo → pairs
Why: The model is read as mail first (did Jamal answer his friend?), then mined as a writer's model. Spotting the question Jamal MISSED teaches task-reading better than any warning.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: read the exchange as mail — and find the question Jamal missed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the frame: Mark wrote to Jamal. Read Mark's email — what happened to Jamal recently? Key: What did Jamal do recently? — he moved to a new area and started a new school. Point at the four margin notes: say congratulations · describe · explain · tell about Simon.
  2. The detective task, ex.2 — Kagan Think-Pair-Share: Read Jamal's reply. Did he answer EVERYTHING Mark asked? Prove it. 3 minutes. Key: Did Jamal answer everything? — NO: he never answered «What's your room like?». The comparisons in his letter: brighter, bigger, not as big as, the largest, not as modern as, my best.
  3. Land the moral: Jamal is friendly, funny — and he lost a point. The reader had four questions; he answered three. Your plan today has four lines so this never happens to you.
  4. Harvest the comparisons from the letter onto the board — brighter, bigger, not as big as, the largest, not as modern as: Yesterday's grammar, working in a real letter.
📺 On screen: the exchange is in every book on p.23 — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: read as a friend, hunt the missing answer, harvest the comparisons. Expect the miss (the room question) to be found with real satisfaction — let a student announce it. Typical problem: «he answered it!» — make them point at where

Email anatomy
pairs
Why: Openings, short forms, six informal words — the Prepare to write box turns «sound friendly» into a checklist a 13-year-old can actually follow.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–6: the Prepare to write box, short forms, informal words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 — how does the letter begin and end? Key: begins: Hi Mark + It's great to hear from you · ends: Speak soon. Write the menu on the board: BEGIN: Hi… · Hello… · It's great to hear from you || END: Love · Write soon · Speak soon · See you soon.
  2. ex.4 as a race: find the six short forms, 90 seconds. Key: six short forms: it's · there's · isn't · they're · aren't · he's.
  3. ex.5 rewrite with short forms, solo, pair check. Key: 1 He's… we're · 2 How's… you're · 3 I'm… they're · 4 We've… we're.
  4. ex.6: the six informal words to meanings. Key: 1 I guess = I think · 2 really = very · 3 's into = likes · 4 awesome = very good · 5 mate = friend · 6 loads = a lot. Drill them as reactions: I got two tickets for the match! — Awesome! · He knows everything about bikes. — He's really into bikes, I guess!
📺 On screen: the begin/end menu in chalk — the board is the medium and the menu stays up through the writing stage, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: harvest, race, rewrite, react. Expect loads of and 's into to be the fresh ones — both go into the checklist's «informal words» line. Typical error: Yours faithfully surfacing from school memory — point at the END menu
SB p.23 ex.3–6 · board
50–58′
Planning · notes
solo
Why: Four notes stop the blank-page freeze: nobody starts writing without an answer for each of Mark's four points. Planning is where weak writers are made average.

WHAT WE DO: ex.7: four notes for Mark's four points — the plan before the pen

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Give the task exactly as the book does: Imagine you moved to a new town. Mark's email is for YOU now. Answer all four points.
  2. Four planning lines on the board: 1 congratulate Mark · 2 my new home + room · 3 free time here · 4 school + a new friend. One line of notes each, 5 minutes, silence.
  3. Show your plan to your partner for 60 seconds — the partner checks ONE thing only: are there four lines?
  4. Walk and unstick: anyone with an empty line gets a menu, not an answer — a bigger room? a garden? a football team? a boy called…?
📺 On screen: the four planning lines in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: plan in four lines, verify the count. Expect line 3 (free time) to stall — the menu trick unsticks it without writing their letter for them

58–76′
🎯 Writing out · my email
solo, silent
Why: The productive outcome in writing: a real ~100-word informal email answering four points, built on a model, a plan and a checklist. Started AND finished in class.

WHAT WE DO: ex.8: write the email — about 100 words, checklist on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Eighteen minutes. About 100 words — 80 is the floor. Four answers. The checklist is on the board. Go.
  2. The TB's secret ingredient, said out loud once: Somewhere in the letter I want one comparison — and one too, one not enough or one not as… as. Jamal managed all three.
  3. Visible timer on. First five minutes: questions allowed in a whisper. After that — silent mode, palm down.
  4. Walk the room with a pencil: underline (don't fix) one spelling and one position slip per draft as you pass.
  5. Fast finishers: write Mark's two-sentence reply to YOUR letter — register held.
  6. Your own model in reserve if anyone needs to SEE the bar: TB model answer (Ben): «Hello Mark, Congratulations… The new house is bigger than the old one… my bedroom's the smallest… there's not enough space… the town isn't as big as my old town… too busy… I've got some new mates… Write soon, Ben» — the TB asks for one comparative/superlative + one each of too, not enough and not as… as in students' letters.
📺 On screen: visible timer counting down 18 minutes (Backup: write the end time in the corner of the board and update every five minutes by hand)
👦 Students do: write, hit the floor of 80 words, answer all four points. Expect the congratulations line to be forgotten first — it is note 1 for a reason. Typical error: enough big under pressure — the underline pencil catches it
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare4_Unit3_Writing_Informal_Email_A5
A5 writing card — WRITE AN INFORMAL EMAIL: the five-point PLAN (who you are writing to · why · what is different · what is better or worse · what question you will ask), Alex's model email annotated in the margin (greeting · reason · details + comparison · question · ending), a USEFUL LANGUAGE strip (Thanks for your email. · I'm writing to tell you… · What about you? · Write soon,) and a CHECK list (greeting · reason + details · comparative / not as … as · question · friendly ending · spelling and punctuation)
  • Glue the card in on the page where the email will be written.
  • Mark the five parts of the model before writing a single sentence.
  • Write your own email: one comparative and one not as … as compulsory.
  • Finish with a question — an informal email never ends on you.

76–84′
Peer review + polish
pairs
Why: A checklist swap turns every student into an editor: they catch each other's missing answers and misspellings faster than they catch their own — both sides learn.

WHAT WE DO: checklist swap: every letter gets a real reader

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap notebooks. Kagan RallyCoach against the board checklist: tick the six points, circle ONE spelling doubt, and answer the only question that matters: Did your friend answer all four points? Which one is missing?
  2. Two minutes back with your own draft: fix what your reader found — a missing answer is two sentences, not a rewrite.
  3. Ask for volunteers: Whose partner opened the letter beautifully? Read two or three openings aloud.
📺 On screen: the checklist is already on the board from minute 5 — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: review as the reader, then patch the gaps. Expect at least a third of letters to miss one point on the first pass — exactly like Jamal; the patch takes a minute and the lesson lands

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«The shoes weren't big enough» — were they too big or too small? → too small. And where does enough stand? → AFTER the adjective.
«I didn't have enough time» — enough before or after the noun? → BEFORE nouns, after adjectives.
«Congratulations on becoming captain! Awesome news!» — to a friend or to a head teacher? → a friend: informal — short forms and Awesome! belong in this letter.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«enough tall», «money enough» — position guessed from RussianAnchor position with gesture, not translation: enough AFTER the adjective = hand pats the shoulder from behind; enough BEFORE the noun = hand points ahead. Drill the worst three as chunks: old enough, big enough, enough time.
Letters that answer three of Mark's four pointsThe plan is compulsory and checked BEFORE writing: four notes, four lines. No plan — no pen. Jamal's own miss is the cautionary tale.
Formal-essay register creeping in («Dear Sir», «I want to tell you»)The Prepare to write box is the law of the room: opening and closing phrases from the box only. Circle any Dear Sir with a smile and point at the box.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
