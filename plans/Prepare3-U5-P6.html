🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Pull back and use everything the first half of the year owns — the vocabulary of Units 1–10 and the choice between four tenses, modals and verb patterns — and then produce it once, alone, on paper, to a known scale.
What is genuinely hard
Not the individual items. What is hard about a half-year paper is the distance: Unit 1 was in September and has not been touched since October. The second hard thing is the arithmetic of it — a teenager who does not know what the paper is worth spends the first ten minutes managing fear instead of answering questions.
Typical error to expect
The four that come back on every paper of the half-year: the present continuous used for a routine (every day we are eating), we was, the double past marker (did you went), and the comparative adverb (more fast). All four are on the paper and all four are the clinic at the end.
Why the lesson is built this way
Three decisions, and all three are arguable, so here is the reasoning. First: the scale goes on the board before the papers are handed out, and the bottom of it is a retake rather than a two. When the rules are known in advance a paper stops being a threat and becomes a task, and a threshold you can sit again is a threshold people read. Second: the paper is thirty-six minutes and not forty-five, and the difference is spent on marking it in this room. The syllabus says 45 + 45; in practice the revision runs thirty-five minutes and the second half is thirty-six minutes of paper plus seventeen of repair, because a paper that goes home in a bag unmarked was not worth the hour. Third: the revision runs across two review spreads at once — Review 1 for Units 1–4 and Review 2 for Units 5–8 — with a third station built by hand for Units 9 and 10, which have no review page of their own. And one thing is said out loud rather than hidden: the Cambridge Term 1 Test does not reach Units 8–10, so Part 10 of the paper was written by us and is labelled as ours.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Half a year. Ten units. Today we close it. First thirty-five minutes: everything comes back — pages thirty and fifty-two, plus the two units the book forgot to give a review page to. Then a break, then seventy-eight marks in thirty-six minutes. The scale is on the board now, before you start, and it does not move: seventy is a five, forty-three is a three, and under that is not a two — it is a retake in January on your own sections. Then we mark it here, together, and you leave with three lines instead of a number.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, the error lists collected at the door, and the bridge into the half-year

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — collect the revision plans at the door. Everybody was told at the end of lesson 32 that their error list from the block 5 test IS today's revision plan. Ask for it as they come in, by name, and look at it — thirty seconds each is enough to know what station 2 has to fix.
  3. Then one round on the board, fast: One thing you must not do in a museum. One thing you don't have to do on a Saturday. One thing you enjoy doing. Three students, three sentences, no comment.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the lesson: Half a year. Ten units. Today we close it — and the first thing you get is the scale.
📺 On screen: the three prompts in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: hand over their error lists and produce one sentence each. Expect about half the group to have the list and half to have lost it — do not spend a minute on that today, note it and move on. Typical error: I don't must for I don't have to, which goes straight onto the briefing board

Lesson frame · and the scale
board
Why: The one stage that decides how the other eighty-two minutes feel. The scale goes up before anything else happens, so that every student spends the revision knowing exactly what the paper is worth and exactly what happens if it goes badly.

WHAT WE DO: name the aim, then put the scale up before anything else happens

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Put the scale on the board without a word, before anybody asks:
    Marks out of 78Grade
    70–785 — the half-year is closed
    59–694 — solid, and the error log names the three lines that would have made it a 5
    43–583 — half of it holds; the error log is compulsory before Unit 11
    42 and below2 — not entered in the register on the spot: a retake in the first fortnight of January, and only on your own sections, not the whole paper
  2. The scale goes on the board BEFORE the papers are handed out, and it does not move. Say it in these words: Seventy-eight marks. Seventy and up is a five. Fifty-nine is a four. Forty-three is a three. Under forty-three is not a two in the register — it is a retake in January on your own sections. You know all of that before you start, which means nothing today is a surprise. Three things take the fear out of a paper and all three happen before it starts: the scale is visible, the format is familiar, and the threshold is a second chance rather than a mark.
  3. Then the shape of the lesson, in twenty seconds: Thirty-five minutes of revision. A break. Thirty-six minutes of paper. Then we mark it here, in this room, together.
  4. Set the bar for the revision half: Every answer in the next thirty-five minutes comes with the reason. «Present simple» is half an answer. «Present simple because it's every day» is an answer.
📺 On screen: the scale written in marker on the board, deliberately in chalk and marker and not in anything that can be switched off — it has to stay visible for the next eighty-two minutes, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: sit down and read the scale before any explanation. Expect the first question to be about the bottom line, and that is good: the answer removes half the anxiety in the room in one sentence. Typical problem: somebody works out that they need exactly fifty-nine and announces it — let them, that is the habit you want

WHAT WE DO: Review 1 and Review 2 vocabulary in one run: Units 1–8, orally and then in writing

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The Teacher's Book warmer for this spread is four true-or-false sentences and it takes ninety seconds: The past tense of buy is bought (true) · Your surname is your family name (true) · We can use the verb like in the continuous (false) · A hill is bigger than a mountain (false). Ninety seconds, whole class, hands up.
  2. Books open at p.30. Review 1 Vocabulary ex.1 and ex.3, oral, three minutes, teams on the buzzer. Keys: ex.1 — 1 holidays · 2 Earth · 3 transport · 4 homes / house · 5 information; ex.3 — 1 desert · 2 forest · 3 river · 4 valley · 5 volcano
  3. Now p.52. Review 2 Vocabulary ex.2 (the three columns) in pairs, three minutes — it is the fastest eighteen-word check in the book. Key: Materials — cotton, gold, leather, metal, plastic, silver · Home — air conditioning, bookcase, fridge, heating, roof, stairs · Adventure — compass, first-aid kit, map, sleeping bag, tent, torch
  4. Written round, three minutes: Kagan Showdown — you say the word, the team writes it and reveals on command. Spelling counts. Put the traps in deliberately: receptionist · guidebook · ceiling · chemistry · luggage · foreign languages.
  5. Every misspelling goes into a «watch out» column on the board and stays there until the papers are handed out.
📺 On screen: SB p.30 and p.52 on the screen so the whole class works off the same exercise (Backup: everybody works from their own book — the two pages are printed on p.30 and p.52 — and you dictate the Showdown words from the list above; the «watch out» column goes on the board either way)
👦 Students do: answer orally in teams, then write on mini-whiteboards. Expect the oral rounds to be nearly perfect and the written round to drop by a third — that gap is the whole reason for the station. Typical errors: cieling, recepcionist and chemistry with two m's

Revision station 2 · the grammar of the half-year
pairs → class
Why: Same logic for grammar — and thirty minutes before the paper it shows you, and them, exactly which of the four tenses still breaks. Everything found here goes straight onto the briefing board.

WHAT WE DO: Review 1 and Review 2 grammar: four tenses, adverbs, modals, and the reason each time

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. SB p.30 Review 1 Grammar ex.1 (four to choose, four to correct), four minutes in pairs. Key: 1 I swim · 2 wants · 3 had · 4 there are · 5 I visited Thao Cam Vien zoo, but I didn't like it. · 6 Every day we do different tests or exams at school. · 7 Do you like the competition? / Did you like the competition? · 8 I can't go shopping today because I'm working.
  2. Then the Gabby text, Review 1 Grammar ex.3 — past simple against past continuous, three minutes. This is the heart of the station. Key: 1 could · 2 was watching · 3 was also practising · 4 came · 5 said · 6 answered
  3. Straight to SB p.52 Review 2 Grammar ex.3 (adverb forms), two minutes — it is the single most-missed item on today's paper. Key: 1 clearly · 2 the worst · 3 louder / more loudly · 4 the fastest · 5 well · 6 more carefully · 7 the most happily · 8 earlier
  4. Kagan RallyCoach throughout: one pen between two, one writes and the other asks for the REASON, not the answer. Then collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together.
  5. ⚠️ For a table that is far ahead of the rest: What to do with Term Test 1 PLUS. It is the same coverage as the Standard paper but with production instead of choice, and that makes it excellent revision material and a poor second paper. Do not give two different papers to one class and then mark them on one scale. Instead, lift PLUS exercises 5, 6 and 7 into station 2 for the strongest table — they are the same grammar, produced rather than chosen.
📺 On screen: SB p.30 and p.52 on the screen, the grammar half of each spread (Backup: the same exercises are printed in their own books on p.30 and p.52 — work from the page and write the three keys on the board one item at a time as the class agrees them)
👦 Students do: solve in pairs with a spoken reason, then answer by number. Expect the Gabby text to be right and the adverb exercise to be half wrong — which is exactly what the briefing four minutes later is for. Typical errors: more fast, more good, and we was reappearing in ex.3

Revision station 3 · Units 9 and 10
teams → solo
Why: The two units of the block that has just finished, and the only two with no review page in the book. Six minutes, entirely oral, entirely on the six sentences that are about to appear on the paper as Part 10.

WHAT WE DO: the two units with no review page, done orally in five minutes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ⚠️ Say out loud why this station exists, because the class will notice that it is the only one without a book page: Units nine and ten have no review page — the book puts them together with eleven and twelve, and we have not done those yet. So this station is six minutes and it is all mine. A class that is told the reason stops looking for the page.
  2. Two rounds and nothing else. Round 1, three minutes, Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade on the Unit 9–10 word cards made in lesson 32 — they exist, they are in the drawer, use them. Round 2, three minutes, Showdown on six sentences you dictate: three modal, three verb pattern.
  3. The six dictation sentences, and every one is about to appear on the paper: The six dictation sentences, and every one of them is a Part 10 item on the paper in thirty minutes' time: You ___ take photos here — look at the sign. (mustn't) · It's Saturday tomorrow, so we ___ get up early. (don't have to) · You ___ wear a white suit to do karate. (have to) · I enjoy ___ films in English. (watching) · We decided ___ by train. (to go) · Do you mind ___ five minutes? (waiting)
  4. Kagan Showdown for the six: you dictate, they write, boards up on command, all at once.
  5. For your own reference, what these two units own: Everything Units 9 and 10 own, in six minutes, off their own pages: SB p.54 sports and activities · SB p.55 must, mustn't, have to, don't have to · SB p.58 relationships · SB p.59 verb patterns, gerunds and infinitives · SB p.60 internet nouns and verbs.

Test briefing
board
Why: The errors from the three stations get named while the paper is still ahead — feedback is only useful when there is still time to use it. Four errors, no more, and all four are on the paper.

WHAT WE DO: four errors named while there is still time to use them, and the rules of the paper

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Four lines on the board, taken from what you have just heard in the three stations, no names attached. In a normal group they will be: every day we are eating · we was watching · did you went · more fast.
  2. Thirty seconds each. Ask for the repair from the room, write it underneath, move on. Do not explain — they have all four of these already, they simply have not used them since November.
  3. Then the rules of the paper, in this order: Seventy-eight marks. Thirty-six minutes. Ten parts. The listening is played twice and it is part eight — I will stop everybody for it. Part ten is three questions on Units eight, nine and ten. If you cannot do something, leave it and come back.
  4. Point at the scale, which has been on the board for twenty-seven minutes: Nothing there has changed and nothing will.
  5. Last line before the break: Everything on this paper has been done in this room since the third of September. There is nothing on it you have not seen.
📺 On screen: the four error lines and the paper rules in chalk under the scale — the board is the medium here and all of it stays up during the paper except the four errors, which are wiped, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair four sentences aloud, then listen to the rules. Expect the room to go quiet at «seventy-eight marks» and to relax again at «nothing you have not seen». Typical problem: somebody asks whether the scale could move if everyone does badly — the answer is no, and saying it now is worth more than saying it later

37–73′
⚡ The half-year paper
solo · silence
Why: Exam conditions, familiar formats, nothing unseen, and a scale that has been on the board for half an hour. Everything on the paper has been done in this room since September.

WHAT WE DO: seventy-eight marks in thirty-six minutes: Cambridge Term 1 Test parts 1–9 plus our Part 10

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Silent mode, palm down. Names on. Turn them over — go. Timer started and visible.
  2. Answer procedure questions only. Answer no content question at all, however small and however politely asked.
  3. At minute 12, stop everybody for the listening (Part 8). Five speakers, played twice with thirty seconds between the playings, exactly as the file runs. Then straight back to silence. Doing it at minute 12 rather than at the end means the slow writers are not cut off in the middle of it.
  4. Call the time at 18 and at 30: Eighteen minutes — you should be in part six. · Six minutes left — go back to anything you left.
  5. Pens down on the timer. Papers stay on the desk, face up. Nobody moves and nobody compares anything.
📺 On screen: the listening file Term Tests Audios_Standard_PRE-TT-1S_ex.10.mp3 from the course folder, played twice at minute 12 (Backup: the full tapescript of all five speakers is printed inside PRE-TT-1S_AnswerKey.pdf — read it aloud twice at natural speed with a pause between speakers; the rest of the paper is on paper and cannot fail)
👦 Students do: work alone in silence for thirty-six minutes. Expect parts 1–4 to be fast, part 7 to be the slow one and part 10 to be quicker than they fear because it was dictated to them nine minutes earlier. Typical problem: somebody spends five minutes on part 6 — the eighteen-minute call exists for exactly that

73–82′
Self-check + the three-line error log
solo → board
Why: They mark their own paper against the key and convert their own number on the scale. What leaves the room is not a mark but three written lines — and the homework is built out of those three lines rather than out of a common list.

WHAT WE DO: they mark their own paper, convert it on the scale, and write three lines

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Keys out, one per desk, and read them aloud at the same time, part by part, so nobody gets lost. Ticks and crosses in their own hand, in a different colour if you have one.
  2. Totals added up by the student, then converted against the scale that has been on the board since minute five. Your number, your grade, your arithmetic. Nobody reads it out.
  3. Three lines. Not five, not twenty. The instruction is exactly this: Look at your crosses. Find the three that came back more than once. Write three lines — what I get wrong, and next to it the correct version in my own words. Twenty crosses on a paper is a wall; three lines is a job. The log is copied into the exercise book BEFORE the papers are collected, and the homework is built out of it — five correct sentences of their own per line.
  4. Walk the room while they write the three lines and read them over shoulders. That is your record of the half-year and it is worth more than the marks column.
  5. ⚠️ Collect the papers only AFTER the three lines are in the exercise book. The log is the homework and the paper is going in your folder.
📺 On screen: the printed keys, one per desk, read aloud part by part (Backup: if the keys have not been photocopied, read all seventy-eight answers from the teacher's copy slowly, part by part, and have them tick in pencil; the scale is already on the board)
👦 Students do: mark their own paper, convert it, write three lines. Expect relief at the conversion — a raw number is frightening and a scale is not. Typical problem: two students comparing totals out loud; cut it once, lightly, and remind them that the number is private

82–87′
Error clinic · four errors
board
Why: Four errors, ninety seconds each, nobody named. This is the difference between a paper that produced a mark and a paper that produced a repair.

WHAT WE DO: four errors, ninety seconds each, and nobody in this room is named

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Four errors, ninety seconds each, and no student is ever named. Write the wrong version on the board, take the repair from the room, write the right version underneath, move on. Everything else that went wrong today lives only in each student's own three lines. The temptation is to do nine errors because you have just marked twelve papers' worth of them; nine errors is a lecture and the class stops listening at the fourth.
  2. The four, and all four are on the paper they have just marked:
    • Every day we are eating soup. — The present continuous used for a routine. Ninety seconds: put both sentences up, we eat and we are eating, and ask one question — which one is happening right now? Then the giveaway: every day and usually can never sit next to am / is / are ‑ing.
    • We was watching a film at nine o'clock. — We were. This is a hearing problem before it is a grammar problem: in a normal sentence was and were are both weak, /wəz/ and /wə/, and they sound almost alike. So the correction has to be written, not spoken: I / he / she / it was · you / we / they were, in a box, in the exercise book.
    • Did you went to the party? — Two past markers in one question. One rule, said once and pointed at all year: Once did is there, the verb goes back to its plain form. Then three fast oral repairs from the room, and stop — this one does not need five minutes, it needs five reminders across five lessons.
    • He runs more fast than me. — The comparative adverb, which is Part 7 of today's paper and the single most-missed item on it. Short adverbs behave like adjectives (fast → faster, early → earlier), ‑ly adverbs take more (carefully → more carefully), and two are irregular (well → better, badly → worse). Three examples on the board and no more — the rest belongs to Unit 5 revision in April.
  3. After each one, one repair from the room out loud and one written into the exercise book. Two of the four should already be in most people's three lines — point that out, because it makes the log feel accurate rather than random.
  4. Close the clinic with the sentence that matters more than the four errors: Everything else that went wrong today is in your three lines and nowhere else. I am not going to say it in front of anybody.
📺 On screen: the four wrong sentences written on the board one at a time, with the repair underneath — chalk is the medium here on purpose, because each one is wiped before the next goes up, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair four errors aloud and copy the four corrections. Expect the adverb one to produce the most «oh» in the room. Typical problem: a student announces that error three was theirs; thank them and do not build on it — the rule is that nobody is named, including by themselves

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can use the vocabulary and grammar of Units 1–10 under exam conditions and convert their own raw score into a grade on a published scale.
  • Subsidiary. Revision through stations built on Review 1 (SB p.30–31) and Review 2 (SB p.52–53); a hand-built station for Units 9–10; marking one's own paper against a key; writing a three-line error log and turning it into homework.
  • Personal. Put the scale up before you hand out the papers and do not change it afterwards, in either direction. During the clinic name no student — not even to praise. And do not read scores out: today's number belongs to the person who wrote it.
By the end, students can…

  • choose between four tenses, modals and verb patterns in context
  • add up their own paper and convert it on a published scale
  • name in three written lines what they will fix before Unit 11

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Does this one count for a grade?» → yes, and the scale is on the board before you start. Under forty-three is a retake in January on your own sections, not a two in the register. The point of the paper is to fix things, not to record them.
«Every day we are eating soup.» Is that a routine or right now? → a routine. So which tense? → present simple. Every day and am / is / are ‑ing cannot share a sentence.
«You mustn't come» and «you don't have to come» — same or different? → completely different. The first forbids, the second frees. This is the single most useful distinction of Unit 9.
«I enjoy to watch films.» What is wrong? → enjoy takes ‑ing. There is no rule behind it, only a list, and the list is on SB p.59.
ICQ before the paper: How many marks? (seventy-eight) How long? (thirty-six minutes) How many times do you hear the listening? (twice) What do you do if you cannot do a question? (leave it, go on, come back)

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«Is this for a grade?» asked before you have finished saying helloIt is the first question of the lesson every single time, so answer it as the second thing you do rather than the tenth. The scale goes up in stage two, before the revision, precisely to take this question off the table for the rest of the lesson.
The revision half runs over and eats the paperThe three stations are ten, nine and five minutes and the timings are tight on purpose. Run them on a visible timer and stop each one mid-sentence if you have to — an unfinished station costs nothing, an unmarked paper costs the lesson.
A student finishes the paper in twenty minutesTwo possibilities and you can tell them apart in ten seconds: check whether Part 10 and the reading are actually filled in. If they are, hand over the Term 1 Test Plus exercise 7 (adverb forms, produced rather than chosen) as extension — it does not count towards the seventy-eight and they should be told that.
Somebody is below the threshold and knows it during the markingThis is exactly why the bottom line of the scale is a retake. Go to them, quietly, while the room is still marking: Three lines. That is all I want from you today. January, your sections only. Never let a student sit with the number alone for eight minutes.
The listening file will not playThe full tapescript of all five speakers is printed inside PRE-TT-1S_AnswerKey.pdf. Read it aloud twice at natural speed, with a pause between speakers, exactly as the recording does. Five marks out of seventy-eight are not worth losing the paper for.
«Why is the last part different from the rest?»Because it is, and they will see it. Tell them the truth in one sentence: The Cambridge paper stops at Unit seven. Units eight, nine and ten are mine. A class that is told this trusts the other seventy-eight marks more, not less.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the OGE — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 20–34 (fifteen marks of grammar and vocabulary) — and, more importantly, the mechanics of the whole written paper: a scale that is known in advance, a fixed time, and a mark that is arithmetic rather than opinion.

There is a second thing today that is worth more than the grammar. The OGE is scored on a published table, and the single commonest way a strong student loses grade 5 is by not knowing, while they are writing, what the table wants. Today they sit a paper whose scale was on the board before the papers were handed out, they add up their own marks and they convert their own number. That habit — I know what this is worth while I am doing it — is worth more over two years than any single exercise. It is also why the threshold is a retake and not a two: a paper you can sit again is a paper you read the scale for.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the OGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
