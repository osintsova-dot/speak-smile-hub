🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Follow a process text from beginning to end and name each stage of it with the right verb — add, prepare, dry, mix, bake, boil, cover, fill — and fill one-word gaps by looking at the grammar around them.
What is genuinely hard
A process text has almost no people in it. Every sentence is about what HAPPENS to the grain, and Russian marks that on the verb ending while English marks it with an extra word. Today the class only has to understand it; the form is next lesson.
Typical error to expect
bread read as /briːd/ and meal as /mel/ — the two letters ea have three common sounds and no rule to choose between them. It matters because reading a text aloud is speaking task 1 of the exam [A2/B1]
Students mark False where the text simply says nothing: they confuse «contradicts» and «not mentioned». In the exam that third option is Not stated [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The unit opens with an object, not a page: a box of cereal on the desk buys three minutes of real attention and pre-teaches half the vocabulary. The reading comes before the vocabulary because the eight verbs are easier to learn out of a text that has already been understood than out of a list. The pronunciation slot is not decoration — ea has three sounds and no rule, and reading aloud is a marked skill in the exam these children will meet in two years. The second half of the lesson moves to Caitlin's blog because a fourteen-year-old writing about cooking makes the topic theirs, and the six-gap task there is the first time the class fills a gap by naming the missing word class rather than by guessing the meaning. The passive is everywhere in both texts today and is deliberately not named — that is Thursday.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“There are eleven things in this box before it becomes breakfast. Today you are going to read what happens to a grain of wheat between a field and this table — and then read a fourteen-year-old girl who thinks cooking your own food is better than any of it. By the end you will be able to describe how something is made.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a three-round rewind of block 9, and the bridge into Unit 19

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last block rewind. Three fast rounds on the board: One — a sentence about a book with «if» in it. Two — one thing you must do before an exam. Three — a word about books that is not «book».
  3. Take one answer per round from three different students, then stop. Block 9 was three weeks ago and the first conditional is the part that fades first.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the new unit: Books open, page one hundred and eight. Last unit but one of the year — and today it is about food.
📺 On screen: the three prompts in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: produce one sentence per round. Expect the conditional to come back slowly and the book words to come back fast. Typical problem — If it will rain; put the correction up in coloured chalk and move on

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Put a real box of cereal on the desk, or draw one, and write one sentence next to it: The cereal is packed into boxes.
  2. Who packs it? The sentence does not say. Why not? Take answers for 30 seconds — do not confirm anything yet.
  3. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: today we read two texts about food and learn eight verbs for what happens to it. The strange grammar in that sentence has a name, and it is Thursday's whole lesson.

WHAT WE DO: the About you box p.108: four questions about breakfast, then the guess

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Warmer from TB p.213, 90 seconds: say the letters of a food word out of order and let them call out the word — z-i-a-p-z (pizza) · m-e-o-n-l (lemon) · t-r-a-w-e (water) · f-u-i-t-r (fruit). Give the first letter first if it stalls.

  3. Kagan Think-Pair-Share on the four About you questions, one round, then two pairs report.
  4. Elicit three grains before the text: What are cereals made from? (corn, wheat, rice).
📺 On screen: the anagram letters written up one at a time in chalk — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: guess the anagrams, then answer in pairs. Expect a loud start and at least one student who eats no breakfast at all — that is a fine answer. Typical problem: they give the Russian names of cereals, which is useful, because it shows you which English words are missing

14–26′
Reading · how cereal is made
solo → pairs
Why: The grammar of the whole unit is sitting inside this text, and the class meets it today as meaning only. Ordering three photos forces them to read the process rather than to hunt for words.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.108 (track 137): the six stages of the process, and the three photos put in order

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the task before the book is open: Three photos, three stages. Read once and put them in order. Two minutes.
  2. Key: the correct order of the photos is B · C · A. Take the order from three different students and make each of them say which sentence proves it.
  3. Play track 137 so they hear the whole process read at speed while they follow with a finger.
  4. Then the content questions, and they are the reason the text is worth reading: TB p.213, the questions that turn the text into a conversation: What do you think is the most difficult stage? (probably cleaning and checking the grain, if people rather than machines do it) · How much of some cereals is sugar? (50%) · Where is the grain checked? (in the factory) · What different shapes is cereal made into? (stars, balls, circles, letters) · Why do you think cereal is made into these shapes? (to make it more interesting for children).
  5. For your own reference, the full process: SB p.108, «Breakfast cereals»: cereals are made from grains — corn, wheat and rice — and usually eaten with milk, or with yogurt and fruit. They began as health foods; today some children's cereals are 50% sugar, and doctors say we should not eat them too often. The process, in six steps: the grain is taken to the factory, where it is cleaned and checked · it is prepared for cooking, made into flour, mixed with other ingredients, water is added and the mixture is boiled · when it is soft it is put into machines that shape it into stars, circles or letters · the shapes are baked in an oven to dry them and then puffed with air · they are covered with sugar or honey, some are filled with chocolate, and then dried in hot air · finally the cereal is packed into boxes for the shops.
📺 On screen: track 137 in the board app with the three photos on the screen (Backup if the board is down: everything is printed on SB p.108 — read the six stages aloud yourself at natural speed while the class follows in the paper book, then read them again with the class saying «stop» at the start of each new stage)
👦 Students do: order the photos, then answer from the text. Expect the order to be right and the 50% sugar figure to produce genuine noise. Typical error: they answer the «most difficult stage» question with an opinion and no reason — push for the reason every time

Vocabulary · words to describe cooking
pairs → class
Why: Eight verbs pulled out of a text they have already understood, so the meaning is done and only the form is left. Writing them in the infinitive is what makes them usable tomorrow.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.108: eight definitions matched to eight verbs from the text, written in the infinitive

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open at the text, ex.2 in pairs, 5 minutes: find the verb in the text and write it in the infinitive. The instruction to enforce: The text says «is mixed». You write «mix».
  2. Key: 1 add · 2 prepare · 3 dry · 4 mix · 5 bake · 6 boil · 7 cover · 8 fill (all written in the infinitive).
  3. Drill all eight chorally, then Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with the definitions on cards, 3 minutes, five trades each.
  4. One sorting question to finish, which costs nothing and fixes the meanings: Which of these eight can you do to a cake? Which to a floor?
  5. Fast finishers: TB p.213: cereal and serial are homophones — /ˈsɪəriəl/ for both. Fast finishers list as many other pairs as they can (ate/eight, hear/here, our/hour, sea/see, wood/would, write/right) and, if there is time, put a pair into one sentence: I ate breakfast at eight o'clock. TB p.217: fast finishers underline Caitlin's rule «if you do the cooking, you don't have to do the washing-up» and write three house rules of their own in the first conditional.
📺 On screen: the eight definitions on the screen, revealed one at a time (Backup: they are printed on SB p.108 — work from the page and write the eight infinitives on the board as the class produces them; the Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards are printed in advance and need no technology)
👦 Students do: match, write in the infinitive, then mingle. Expect 6–7 out of 8 unaided. Typical errors: dried copied straight out of the text instead of dry, and cover confused with fill

36–44′
Pronunciation · the three sounds of ea
class → pairs
Why: Two letters, three sounds and no rule to work it out from — which is exactly why it needs a sorting table and eight minutes rather than a footnote. Reading aloud is a marked skill.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–5 p.108 (tracks 138–139): three sounds, eleven words, one sorting table

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write one sentence on the board and ask the question that opens the whole point: Eat bread for every meal. How many words have «ea» in them? (three) How many different sounds? (three)
  2. Play track 138 for the three model words — ear /ɪə/ · head /e/ · seat /iː/ — and drill them.
  3. ex.5 in pairs, 4 minutes: eleven words into three columns. Then play track 139 to check and repeat. Key: /ɪə/ ear — near · /e/ head — bread, breakfast, healthy, ready · /iː/ seat — beach, clean, eat, meal, teacher, wheat.
  4. ⚠️ Before you accept the printed key: ⚠️ A misprint in the Teacher's Book, TB p.214. The printed key puts meal in the /ɪə/ ear column. It is not: meal is /miːl/, the same vowel as seat. Only near belongs in the /ɪə/ column. Mark /iː/ as correct and tell the class why — a printed key that is wrong is a very good ten-second lesson about checking things yourself.
  5. Say the honest thing about the rule: TB p.214, and this is the honest version: there is no rule. clean is /iː/ but cleanse is /e/; and beyond these three sounds there are more — great is /eɪ/ and heart is /ɑː/. The pair is learnt word by word, which is why the sorting table matters more than any explanation. Watch the /iː/ against /ɪ/ contrast too: seat and sit are different words, and the /iː/ is longer with the lips spread.
  6. Close it with one sentence each: Make a sentence with one word from every column. (Clean your ears before breakfast.)
📺 On screen: tracks 138 and 139 in the board app with the three-column table on the screen (Backup: draw the three columns in chalk and say all eleven words yourself, twice each, in a scrambled order; the class writes them into the columns and then repeats after you)
👦 Students do: sort eleven words by ear, then repeat. Expect the /e/ column to be the easy one and meal to split the room. Typical error: healthy put with eat, because the class knows the word from its spelling and not from its sound

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.110 (track 140): who is writing, what about, and what is on the site

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ask before anything is read: Do you read any blogs? Why do people write them? If you started one, what would it be about? 90 seconds, no more.
  2. Give a time limit and the three questions: Two minutes. Whose blog is this? What is it about? What can you find on it? Key: The blog is written by Caitlin, a teenage chef. It is about cooking. You can find recipes and photos on it..
  3. Play track 140 so they hear the blog read while they follow.
  4. The four questions that make the text worth having read (TB p.217): TB p.217: this is a gap you fill by looking at the grammar around it, not at the meaning. Gap 5 comes after better, which is a comparative, so the construction is comparative + than and nothing else will do. Teach the move: read the whole sentence, name what kind of word is missing, then write it. Comprehension questions that make the text worth reading: Why did Caitlin start this blog? (to show that cooking is fun) · What does she say is the advantage of cooking your own food? (it tastes better) · Does she wash up after she cooks? (no) · If you like what she writes, what should you do? (send her a message).
  5. For your own reference: SB p.110, Caitlin's blog «The Taste Test». She is 14, she has always loved cooking, and she told her parents she wanted to be a chef when she was nine — that is still the plan. She writes the blog to show people how much fun cooking is, that a meal can be quick and easy, and that it tastes better than food made in a factory. Her house rule: if you do the cooking, you don't have to do the washing-up. Two recipes are printed — a grilled steak with garlic butter, fried potatoes and green beans or carrots, and pancakes made from two eggs, 300 ml of milk and 100 g of flour.
📺 On screen: track 140 in the board app with the blog on the screen (Backup: it is printed on SB p.110 — read it aloud yourself at natural speed while the class follows in the book, and put the three questions on the board)
👦 Students do: read fast for the gist, then answer in detail. Expect the washing-up rule to be the line they remember. Typical error: they answer «what is it about» with «cooking» and stop — push for the second half of the answer, which is «and there are recipes and photos»
SB p.110 ex.1 · track 140
58–68′
Reading · one word in each gap
solo → pairs
Why: The gap-fill is not a vocabulary task, it is a grammar task in disguise: you look at what is around the gap and name the missing word class before you write anything.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.110: the About me text with six one-word gaps

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Name the move before they start, and it is the whole strategy: Do not think about the meaning first. Read the whole sentence and say what KIND of word is missing.
  2. Do gap 5 together as the demonstration: The word before the gap is «better». What always comes after a comparative? (than) — and note that the sentence did not even need to be understood.
  3. Solo, 5 minutes, silent mode. Then pairs compare and, for every gap, say what kind of word it is. Key: 1 When · 2 a · 3 how · 4 be · 5 than · 6 to.
  4. Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Gap three — number twos, boards up!
  5. Name the trap once: One word means one word. «A lot» is two.
📺 On screen: the gapped text on the screen so the whole class repairs the same gap at the same time (Backup: it is printed on SB p.110 — work from the page and write the six answers on the board one at a time as the class agrees them)
👦 Students do: fill six gaps alone, then justify each one. Expect 4 out of 6. Typical error: gap 1 answered with Since instead of When, and gap 6 left empty because forget to is a pattern rather than a meaning

Why: Recipes are the one text type where the reader has to act on what is written, so accuracy matters visibly. Matching them to the photos is the fastest possible comprehension check.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.110: two recipes matched to two of the seven photos, then your own

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ask what a recipe contains before they look: Two things. What are they? (a list of food and a set of instructions).
  2. Pairs, 3 minutes: read both recipes and match them to the photos. Key: The best steak! — photo G · Pancakes — photo A.
  3. Kagan RallyCoach: for each of the other five photos the partner names how the food was cooked, using today's verbs.
  4. Then the useful twist (TB p.217): Write your recipe with the steps in the wrong order and give it to another pair to sort out. 4 minutes.
  5. Two pairs read a scrambled recipe to the class and the class puts it in order out loud.
📺 On screen: the two recipes and the seven photos on the screen (Backup: all of it is printed on SB p.110 — the pairs work from the book and the scrambled recipes are written on paper, which is what they were going to be anyway)
👦 Students do: match, then scramble and re-sort. Expect the steak and the pancakes to be matched instantly and the other five photos to produce the real language. Typical error: fried used for the grilled steak — worth noting, because those five words come back properly in lesson 3

WHAT WE DO: the Talking points box p.110 and one process described from a real kitchen

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The book's three questions first, in threes, 3 minutes: What's your favourite food? Do you like to cook? Tell me about your favourite breakfast.
  2. Then the productive task, and it is the one that uses the lesson: Choose ONE thing you can actually make. Describe how it is made in four steps. Use four different verbs from today. 4 minutes.
  3. Kagan Showdown to close: each group says its four steps and the class calls out the four verbs it heard.
  4. The standard from the lesson frame: Four steps, four different verbs. Repeating «mix» three times does not count.
  5. Log errors silently on your correction sheet; do not correct aloud — the feedback slot is the start of the next lesson.
📺 On screen: the four required verbs written on the board as a checklist — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: describe a real process in four steps. Expect tea, pasta and pancakes, and expect the passive to appear by accident in at least one group. Typical error: all four steps built with and then — which is fine today, the linking comes with the grammar

Structure
The grain is taken to the factory. · Water is added and the mixture is boiled. · The shapes are baked in an oven. · Some are filled with chocolate. · It tastes better than food that is made in a factory.
Form
understood, not taught today: is / are + past participle. The eight verbs are written and learnt in the infinitive (SB p.108 ex.2).
Pron. watch
the three sounds of ea — /ɪə/ near · /e/ bread, breakfast, healthy, ready · /iː/ beach, clean, eat, meal, teacher, wheat. Watch /iː/ against /ɪ/: seat is not sit.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«The grain is taken to the factory.» Does the grain walk there? → no. Somebody takes it, and the sentence does not say who.
«Some are filled with chocolate.» Filled by whom? → we are not told. It does not matter — that is the point of writing it this way.
«Write one word for each gap.» Is a lot one word? → no, it is two. One word means one word.
In gap 5, the word before it is better. What always follows a comparative? → than. You can answer without reading the sentence at all.
bread and beach — same sound or different? → different. /e/ and /iː/, and the spelling gives you no help whatsoever.

Assumptions: SB p.108 and p.110, syllabus lesson 62. The class has met the present perfect and both past tenses; the passive has been seen in reading before but never named. Of the eight verbs they usually know mix, bake and boil; prepare, cover and fill are the ones that need teaching. ⚠️ The syllabus calls this lesson «SB p.108–109», but p.109 is the grammar page and belongs to lesson 63 — the unit's second reading is on p.110.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
“Why does it say is taken and not takes?”Somebody always asks, and the answer is one sentence, not a grammar lesson: Because the sentence is about the grain, not about the person. On Thursday we do nothing else for ninety minutes. Write the question on the board and leave it there until Thursday.
The ea table turns into a hunt for a ruleThere is no rule and saying so is better than inventing one. clean is /iː/ and cleanse is /e/; great is /eɪ/ and heart is /ɑː/. Say it, and spend the time on sorting and repeating instead.
The Teacher's Book key for the pronunciation task is wrongIt is. ⚠️ A misprint in the Teacher's Book, TB p.214. The printed key puts meal in the /ɪə/ ear column. It is not: meal is /miːl/, the same vowel as seat. Only near belongs in the /ɪə/ column. Mark /iː/ as correct and tell the class why — a printed key that is wrong is a very good ten-second lesson about checking things yourself.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
