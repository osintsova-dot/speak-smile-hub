🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Build person nouns with the suffixes -ist, -er and -or (canoeist, climber, footballer, sailor, spectator, surfer, swimmer, winner, cyclist, competitor) with the right spelling, and write an online comment of about 70 words that makes a suggestion, gives reasons and links them.
What is genuinely hard
-er and -or are the same sound in English — a weak /ə/ — so the ear is no help at all and only the eye chooses. Some bases also change: canoe → canoeist, swim → swimmer. In the writing, the difficulty is not ideas but linking: students produce three separate sentences where the task wants one connected argument.
Typical error to expect
«sailer», «competiter», «footballor» — -er and -or are the same sound, so only the eye chooses. Word formation is pure spelling marks in the exam.
Why the lesson is built this way
Vocabulary first and short, because it feeds the writing: the comment is about a sports club, and the person nouns are the words it needs. The suffix rules are extracted, then tested by spelling in a relay, because spelling is exactly what the unit test and the exam pay for. The writing half is model-first: two real online comments are mined for the four things that make them work (a suggestion, an extra suggestion, reasons, links), then planned, written and peer-checked in class. The final/finalist distinction closes the loop between the two halves.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Sailor. Climber. Competitor. Footballer. Two of those endings sound exactly the same and only one of them is right each time. Then: your school wants ideas for a new sports club, and seventy words to say them in.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, three minutes of repairing the class's own state-verb errors, and the bridge into the writing lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what's the best thing about today so far? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — delayed feedback from lesson 1. Four sentences on the board, two of them broken in exactly the way the class broke them: I am thinking he is the best player. · She's training for the final at the moment. · I'm not believing that score. · They play in the school team every Saturday.
  3. Pairs, sixty seconds: find the two broken ones and repair them. Take the repairs from two different pairs, then rub out the wrong versions in front of the class.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page twenty-two, bottom half. Three endings, one sound — and then you're writing to your school website.

👦 Students do: spot, repair, and say the corrected sentence aloud. The errors are theirs, which is why nobody argues

Why: Vocabulary · three suffixes, one sound

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: the rule, the sorting, then the words in sentences

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 on the board: Ntando runs — he is a runn___ . He competes — he's a competit___ . Let the class fill both, then say the uncomfortable truth out loud: Those two endings sound exactly the same. Only the spelling is different.
  2. ex.2 (EP): sort the eight words into three columns. Warn about the two spelling changes before they start — canoe → canoeist, swim → swimmer.
  3. Check on the board and read each column aloud twice, as a group: the grouping is the only reliable memory hook here.
  4. ex.3 individually: five sentences. Check by nomination, and make the student SPELL the word aloud before you write it up.
  5. ex.4 last, because it is the subtle one: final or finalist, medal or medallist. Ask the sorting question — Is this an event or a person?
📺 On screen: three columns on the board headed -ist, -er, -or — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: sort, spell aloud, then apply. Everyone spells at least one word for the whole class
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE tasks 26–31 (word formation — person nouns). Building a person noun from a verb with the right ending is the commonest single item type of that section.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: add a five-minute board round: six sentences with the base word in capitals (SAIL, CLIMB, COMPETE, WIN, CYCLE, SWIM). It fits inside the spelling relay without extending it.

Why: Vocabulary · the spelling relay

WHAT WE DO: ten rounds of build-and-spell, points for spelling only

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three teams, one runner per round, rotating so everyone writes.
  2. You call the base (sail, climb, canoe, swim, cycle, compete, spectate, surf, win, football); the team agrees; the runner writes the person noun on the board.
  3. Two points for correct spelling, zero for the right idea spelled wrong. Say that before round one — it is the same rule as the unit test.
  4. Last two rounds are the hard ones: swimmer and competitor. Let them fail once, then fix it publicly.
  5. Finish with a thirty-second Kagan Showdown: everyone writes medallist, all reveal together.
📺 On screen: the board divided into three columns, one per team — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: the team decides, one member writes, everyone checks. Nobody is passive because the team cannot score without agreeing

Move break · name the sport, name the person
standing circle
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that keeps the suffixes in the mouth

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Circle. You throw a soft ball and say a sport (sailing, climbing, cycling, football, swimming, surfing).
  2. Whoever catches it says the person noun in a full sentence: A person who sails is a sailor. Then throws it on with a new sport.
  3. Two rounds. In round two the sentence has to be about a real person: My cousin is a climber.
  4. Freeze and sit: Page twenty-three. Your school needs a thousand pounds and wants your ideas.
📺 On screen: no technology in this stage — a ball and a circle, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone speaks at least twice; the second round forces the words into real sentences

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: the Runners Inc. post, then the two comments under it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: read the school website post. One question: what is Runners Inc.? Get the whole answer, not half of it, and write it on the board.
  2. Do NOT let them read the comments yet — say so. The prediction is the point: pairs, sixty seconds, how would YOU raise a thousand pounds? Collect four ideas.
  3. Now read the two comments. Which of the class's ideas appeared?

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: the suggestion phrases and the linking words, harvested and grouped

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3: pairs find every phrase Zoe and Nat use to suggest something. Collect on the board in one column.
  2. Then add the phrases they do NOT use but could — How about…, Shall we…, Let's… — in a second colour, and drill all six chorally with the right intonation (a suggestion falls at the end; a real question rises).

Why: 🎯 Writing out · the online comment

WHAT WE DO: ex.5: about 70 words, written and finished in class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Silent mode, palm down: sixteen minutes, no talking, phones face down. The checklist is on the board and the Prepare to write box is open.
  2. Target on the board as a range: 65–80 words. Count them and write the number at the end.
  3. Circulate with a pen but do not rewrite: a dot in the margin marks a line with an error and the writer hunts it. Say that once at the start.
  4. At minute 13 the two-minute warning, then the rule: Last two minutes are for spelling and the link words — no new sentences.
  5. Fast finishers write a second comment that disagrees with their own suggestion.
📺 On screen: the visible timer and the three-line checklist, both on the board — nothing to fail
👦 Students do: write about 70 words alone and finish. Nothing goes home unfinished

Peer review + polish
pairs
Why: Peer review + polish

WHAT WE DO: a three-question checklist swap, then two minutes to repair

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap comments. The reader answers three questions IN WRITING at the bottom: what is the suggestion? how many reasons are there? which link words did you find?
  2. If any answer is «none», the reader draws an arrow to where it should go. No grammar correction in someone else's text.
  3. Two minutes back with your own comment: repair what your reader could not find.
  4. Collect. Read two anonymous best comments aloud and say precisely why they work — the suggestion, the two reasons, the link.
📺 On screen: no technology in this stage — paper and pens, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: read someone else's comment with a job, then repair their own

Structure
Why don't we organise a non-uniform day? · Why not encourage people to do a sponsored run? · What about posting it on social media? · In addition, we could all wear something green. · That would be helpful as well.
Form
-ist (canoeist, cyclist) · -er (climber, footballer, swimmer — with doubling) · -or (sailor, spectator, competitor); suggestion frames + linking words (in addition, also, as well).
Pron. watch
competitor /kəmˈpetɪtə/ — the stress is on the SECOND syllable, not the first · canoeist /kəˈnuːɪst/ has three syllables · spectator /spekˈteɪtə/ · and the whole point of the suffix lesson: -er and -or are the same weak sound /ə/, so sailor and climber rhyme perfectly and are spelled differently
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«sailor» and «climber» — do the endings sound different? → no, identical. So what decides the spelling? → memory and the eye, nothing else.
«He was a finalist.» Is that the event or the person? → the person. The final is the event.
«Why don't we…?» Am I asking a question or making a suggestion? → a suggestion. The question mark is a disguise.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Everyone writes «sailer» and «competiter»The relay is a spelling relay, with the point given for the spelling and not for the idea. Group the words on the wall by ending, and make the class read each group aloud twice — the grouping is the only mnemonic that works here.
The comments are three unlinked sentencesThe checklist demands a link word and the peer reviewer has to point at it with a finger. Put the four link words on the board (in addition, also, as well, and) and make them countable.
Students suggest but never explainThe model teaches it: Nat gives THREE reasons in five lines. Count them together before anyone writes, and put «at least two reasons» in the checklist.
Somebody finishes the comment in four minutesFast finishers write a second comment DISAGREEING with their own suggestion — it doubles the language and it is genuinely harder.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 26–31 (word formation — person nouns with -er, -or and -ist are a named line of the codifier) and task 37.

-er and -or sound identical in English (/ə/), so only the eye chooses: sailor but climber, competitor but footballer. The exam pays for the spelling, not for the meaning. The online comment written today also drills the linking phrases (in addition, also, as well) that lift a written answer out of the three-sentence zone.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
