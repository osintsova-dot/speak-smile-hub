🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Listen for three different purposes — to match four speakers to statements (Culture), to complete six sentences with the exact words heard (Listening Part 3), and to choose between three written options (Listening Part 4) — and answer the examiner's personal questions in Speaking Part 1.
What is genuinely hard
Each of the three listening tasks fails in its own way. Matching fails when a student decides after one sentence. Sentence completion fails when the student improves the words they heard — writes children instead of kids, or monkey instead of monkeys — and the mark goes. Multiple choice fails because the printed option is never the spoken wording. And Speaking Part 1 fails for the opposite reason to everything else: the answers are too short, because the candidate treats a conversation as an interrogation.
Typical error to expect
In sentence completion, the two Russian-candidate classics: rewriting what was heard, and writing three words where the instruction says one or two. In Speaking Part 1, the one-word answer — Yes. Pizza. — which is worth almost nothing when one extra sentence would double it.
Why the lesson is built this way
The lesson is built as one long listening arc with the load rising: the Culture recording first, because matching four speakers is the easiest of the three tasks and the topic is Unit 13's, so the vocabulary supports the ear; then sentence completion, which is the most precise; then multiple choice, which is the most abstract. The Culture spread is not decoration — it belongs to this block by the book, it recycles the social-media language, and its Useful language box (making generalisations) is what the speaking half needs to stop the answers being one word long. Speaking Part 1 closes the lesson in threes, with a real examiner script, and two of its eight questions are this block's own topics — food and time online.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Today you hear four teenagers, one park announcement and one tennis player — and then somebody in this room becomes the examiner. Three different listening jobs, and each one fails in a different way. By the end you will know which one is yours.”

Listening Part 3 · sentence completion
individual → pairs → class
Why: Listening Part 3 · sentence completion

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3, tracks 86 and 87: predict the kind of word, hear the trap, then do the task

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Name the format: Listening Part 3 — sentence completion. One monologue of about three minutes, six sentences with a gap in each, heard twice. One or two words, or a number, a date or a time. Then the tip that IS the task: Read the sentences before the recording starts and decide what KIND of word is missing, but write nothing. Then write what you hear, unchanged — do not improve it, do not paraphrase it. The exam marks the words that were said.
  2. ex.1, two minutes in pairs: what KIND of information is missing in each of the six gaps? what kind of information each gap needs: 1 a time · 2 a type of animal · 3 a verb · 4 a type of animal · 5 a name · 6 a date Nothing is written into the gaps yet — that is part of the instruction.
  3. ex.2, track 86, the trap question: three times are mentioned and only one is the answer. the trap question, track 86: the answer is 8.45 — six thirty is when visitors normally leave, and seven p.m. is when the last tour starts. Three times are said and only one answers the question Ask what the other two times were for — that question is the whole skill.
  4. ex.3, track 87: the full task, played twice. Between the two hearings, ninety seconds of pair comparison and marking the disputed gaps.
  5. Check: the Appleton Animal Park task, track 87: 1 8.45 / eight forty-five · 2 monkeys · 3 riding · 4 lion · 5 The Sandwich Place · 6 (the) 23 / 23rd / twenty-third (of) May. For gaps 1, 5 and 6, ask three students to read their answer aloud and ask the room whether that is what was SAID — this is where rewriting gets caught.
  6. Thirty seconds on the spelling of The Sandwich Place and on why monkeys is plural. Both are marks.
📺 On screen: the six sentences on the page, with the kind-of-word notes made in pairs. Backup: if the audio fails, the audioscript (TB p.307–308) can be read aloud twice at recording speed — the task is unaffected, and this is why the audioscript pages are in the materials column
👦 Students do: predict six word types, catch a trap, then complete six gaps with two hearings and a pair check between them

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2, track 88: read the options for their differences, then choose

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

And the tip: Underline the key words in the questions AND in the options before the recording starts. The interview follows the order of the questions, so if you lose one, jump to the next rather than staying with the lost one.
  2. ex.1 in pairs, three minutes: underline the key words in the six questions AND in the options. The Teacher's Book asks for both, and the second half is the one that matters. the six questions: who suggested she try tennis · what she did before she was eleven · how she felt when serious training started · what happened at her first big competition · how she felt about missing an international competition · what she would like to do in future
  3. Ask the question that makes the difference: In question 5, what is the difference between 'upset that she had an injury' and 'sad that she wouldn't see a new country'? Thirty seconds, in pairs.
  4. ex.2, track 88, first hearing. Then a second hearing immediately — the exam gives two and so do we.
  5. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: the interview with the tennis player Ruth Leyton, track 88: 1 C · 2 B · 3 A · 4 B · 5 C · 6 A. For every item, ask what Ruth actually SAID — never the printed option, always her words.
  6. One minute of accounting: how many changed on the second hearing, and how many of those changes were right?

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: the questions of Phase 1 and Phase 2, and what back-up questions are for

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Name the format: Speaking Part 1 — a short conversation with the examiner, two to three minutes. Phase 1 is name, age, where you live and who with; Phase 2 is eight general questions. Candidates answer the examiner, not each other. And the tip: These questions can be prepared in advance, so prepare them.   3. ex.2, one minute: why are there back-up questions? Because the examiner uses them to help a candidate who did not understand — and using one costs the candidate nothing.

🎯 Speaking out · the exam, in threes
groups of three
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · the exam, in threes

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: the whole of Part 1 run three times, with the roles rotating

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of three: one examiner, two candidates. The examiner reads the script from p.131 exactly as printed, including the greeting and the mark-sheet line.
  2. Round one, five minutes: full Part 1, Phase 1 and Phase 2. The examiner has one extra job — if an answer is one word, use the back-up question.
  3. Rotate roles. Round two, five minutes. Rotate again. Round three, five minutes — by the end everyone has been the examiner once and a candidate twice.
  4. While they work, monitor with a sheet: six good answers WITH names, six one-word answers WITHOUT them. Interrupt nobody.
  5. Two minutes at the end: the six good answers read out with names, and one question to the room — What did these six have that the short ones didn't?
  6. Mixed ability: pairs who need more practice repeat Phase 2 with a different partner; stronger students answer the same questions but must use two generalising phrases in every answer.

👦 Students do: every student speaks as a candidate twice and runs the examiner's script once, in a group of three

Grammar Plus U13 + U14
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can handle three different listening task types — matching, sentence completion and multiple choice — knowing what each one is testing, and can answer Speaking Part 1 questions with an extra sentence rather than a single word.
  • Subsidiary. Making generalisations (I tend to, in general, for the most part, overall); predicting the kind of word a gap needs; reading three options for the difference between them before listening.
  • Personal. The one instruction that decides the whole lesson: in sentence completion, the words you write are the words you heard. Do not improve them. Say it before the first recording and again before the second.
By the end, students can…
  • match four speakers to what they said, and change an answer on the second hearing
  • write one or two words into a gap exactly as heard
  • choose between three options that never use the recording's words
  • answer a personal question with one extra sentence

Structure

Form
generalising adverbials sit at the front of the sentence and take a comma (In general, …; Overall, …) or straight after the subject (I tend to …; I generally …) · in sentence completion the answer is one or two words, a number, a date or a time — never a clause.
Pron. watch
the exam's own difficulty is numbers and times under speed: eight forty-five, the twenty-third of May, half past six. Drill the three shapes before the recording, because the gap that goes wrong is almost always a number · and the generalising phrases run together: /ɪnˈdʒenrəl/, /fəðəˈməʊspɑːt/
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
The recording says «monkeys». You write «monkey». Is that a mark? → no. Why not? → because the exam marks the words that were said.
The option says «disappointed» and the speaker says «I really wanted to win». Same thing? → yes. Will the word disappointed be in the recording? → almost certainly not.
The examiner asks what kind of food you like and you say «Pizza». How could that be worth twice as much? → one more sentence: why, when, who with.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Three recordings in one lesson is a lot of sittingThe move break is at minute thirty and is non-negotiable, and the speaking half is on their feet in threes. Between the two exam recordings there is a two-minute pair comparison, which is also movement.
Answers are 'improved' in sentence completionThe rule is said twice and tested once: after the first hearing, ask three students to say their answer aloud and ask the room whether that is what was SAID. Catching one rewritten answer publicly is worth more than any instruction.
The Culture article eats the lessonIt is a fourteen-minute stage with two tasks, not a reading lesson. If it overruns, drop the six-sentence completion (ex.2) and keep the summary choice and the highlighted words — the listening is what today is for.
Speaking Part 1 answers stay one word longThe generalising phrases from the Culture page are on the board when the speaking starts, and the rule is one sentence plus one. The examiner in each three has a job: if an answer is one word, ask the back-up question.
The marked tests dominate the first ten minutesPapers back at the door, face down, one sentence each. The two hardest items go on the board for ninety seconds in the frame stage and are not discussed again.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
