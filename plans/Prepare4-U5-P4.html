🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Describe food with the twelve Unit 10 adjectives (juicy, sour, raw, spicy, bitter, sweet, frozen, fresh, delicious, disgusting, horrible, tasty) — anchored to real tastes, pronounced right, and used in real opinions.
What is genuinely hard
Not the meanings — the sound-spelling traps (sour, raw, juicy, delicious) and the positive/negative sorting under pressure: tasty and disgusting both «about taste», one is a compliment, one is an insult. Plus opinions that arrive without reasons.
Typical error to expect
receipt (silent p!) · delicious · disgusting · juicy — the block's misspelling traps; a misspelt word scores nothing, in a class test and later in the exam
An opinion with no reason attached — I think sushi is disgusting full stop — scores as no opinion at all in the speaking [national exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
The adjectives arrive from Isla's recorded blind-taste experiment — eight foods, one by one — because words anchored to tastes stick where lists don't. The reading about Ollie is careful reading in exam shape (questions-to-paragraphs, one-word gaps) run as an ordinary text about an extraordinary eater, and it quietly seeds lesson 5's grammar: «Ollie's eaten everything» vs «I made a curry last week» both live in it. The productive stage is a taste panel in groups of six with one question each — nobody can hide — and the mini-debate recycles Unit 6's agree/disagree phrases onto the insect question. Log errors; lesson 5 opens with them.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“There is a fruit so smelly it's illegal on public transport — and a boy in England who says it tastes incredible. He has eaten everything from ants to zebra. Today: his story, twelve words to describe any food on Earth, and your own verdict — what's the most delicious and the most disgusting thing YOU have ever eaten?”

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and open the food wall

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two columns on the board: 😋 + | 🤢 − — the FOOD WALL, empty for now; every adjective gets placed by a student during the lesson.
  2. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: twelve words that can describe any food on Earth, one blind-taste experiment, and one boy who has eaten a zebra.
  3. Set the bar: A food opinion needs a because. «Disgusting» is half an answer; «disgusting because it's raw» is an answer.

WHAT WE DO: About you + the eight photos

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

  4. The About you video (06) is optional decoration if the app is open — thirty seconds, no task.

Vocabulary · the blind taste test
solo → pairs
Why: The adjectives arrive from Isla's recorded experiment, in order, food by food — twelve describing words anchored to eight real tastes instead of a list.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2–5 (tracks 43–46): Isla's experiment, food by food

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Play track 43 — ex.2 gist: What is Isla asking Ali to do? Key: track 43 — Isla is asking Ali to taste different types of food with his eyes covered: a blind taste test for her science project.
  2. Play track 44 — ex.3: number the photos in tasting order. Key: track 44 — tasting order: 1 D pineapple · 2 F lemon juice · 3 B sushi · 4 E curry · 5 A coffee · 6 H cake · 7 C frozen peas · 8 G bread.
  3. Play track 45 — ex.4: match the eight adjectives to the foods, pairs check between plays. Key: track 45 — 1 b juicy–pineapple · 2 c sour–lemon juice · 3 d raw–salmon · 4 a spicy–curry · 5 h bitter–coffee · 6 g sweet–cake · 7 f frozen–vegetables · 8 e fresh–bread.
  4. Play track 46 — ex.5: Ali's four verdicts. Key: track 46 — 1 delicious (pineapple) · 2 disgusting (sushi) · 3 horrible (lemon juice) · 4 tasty (curry); sort the four into positive/negative on the board.
  5. After each answer a student places the adjective on the +/− wall and justifies in one sentence.
  6. Drill the sound traps, three choral repetitions: sour /saʊə/ · raw /rɔː/ · spicy /ˈspaɪsi/ · delicious /dɪˈlɪʃəs/ · juice /dʒuːs/ · salmon /ˈsæmən/ — the l is SILENT · vegetables /ˈvedʒtəbəlz/ — three syllables.
📺 On screen: tracks 43–46 in the whiteboard app (Backup: read the taste test from the audioscript, TB p.267–268 — play Isla and Ali in two voices; the matching works identically by ear)
👦 Students do: listen four times at four depths, match, place words on the wall. Expect bitter–coffee and sour–lemon to anchor fast; frozen–vegetables to need the photo. Typical error: sour /suːə/

WHAT WE DO: Ollie's interview: hook → gist → questions to paragraphs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hook, books closed: At ONE year old, Ollie ate a leg of his brother's pet turtle. Now he's sixteen. Guess what he eats today. Take three guesses, then: everything from ants to zebra.
  2. Pre-teach on the board: turtle /ˈtɜːtl/ · insect · illegal (recycled from Unit 5!) · recipe /ˈresɪpi/ — note illegal returning from Unit 5.
  3. ex.1 — five questions to five paragraphs, solo, 4 minutes. Key: questions to paragraphs: 1 b (why start the website) · 2 a (most interesting thing eaten) · 3 d (most disgusting) · 4 e (best food) · 5 c (ever been ill).
  4. TB mixed ability: weaker readers underline the key words in the questions first and scan for those.
  5. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Paragraph three — number fours!
📺 On screen: track 47 is the recording of the interview — optional (Backup: skip the audio entirely; the stage is built on silent reading)
👦 Students do: guess, scan, match questions to paragraphs. Expect the durian paragraph to generate questions — promise the smell story in the debrief. Typical problem: matching by first line only — the key words fix it

Why: The one-word-per-gap round forces re-reading for precision; the word match (giant, recipe, flavour) hands the speaking stage its vocabulary.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 one-word gaps + ex.3 word work

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.2 — one word per gap, eight items, pairs with Kagan RallyCoach: the partner must hear the LINE that decides. Key: one word per gap: 1 two · 2 dad · 3 carry · 4 disgusting · 5 meat · 6 can · 7 sweet · 8 ill.
  2. ex.3 fast — five words to meanings. Key: 1 a giant = very large · 2 a recipe = instructions for cooking · 3 b flavour = how food tastes · 4 a lamb = meat of a young sheep · 5 b low-quality = isn't very good. Drill recipe /ˈresɪpi/.
  3. Colour with the TB backgrounds: two billion people eat insects; crickets are complete protein, like soya — Remember that number for the debate.
  4. Talking point if the pace allows: What food or drink from your country would you give Ollie? · Will more people eat insects in the future?
📺 On screen: the gaps checked by voice against the page — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: re-read for precision, match the words. Expect gap 7 (sweet — the miracle berries) to be the aha-moment of the text. Typical error: two words squeezed into a one-word gap

  2. Six calls, full volume, sit down.
📺 On screen: six headers in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed

56–72′
🎯 Speaking out · the taste panel
groups of 5–6
Why: The productive outcome: the twelve adjectives in real opinions about real food, in groups, with every member holding one question — nobody can hide.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6 run as a panel: one question each, everyone answers

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of five or six; each member OWNS one ex.6 question: most delicious ever? most disgusting? raw food — yes or no? spicy food? vegetarian meals? sweet things?
  2. Round one: each owner asks their question around the group, notes the best answer. The because-rule is law.
  3. Frames on the board: The most delicious thing I've ever eaten is… · I've never tried… · It tastes/tasted really…
  4. Round two — Kagan Timed Pair Share across groups: report your group's best discovery to a partner from another group.
  5. You walk and log: adjective misuse, missing becauses, pronunciation slips — SILENTLY.
  6. Debrief: each group's owner of the «most disgusting» question reports the winner. Build a class top-3 of horrors on the board.
📺 On screen: the three frames in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: ask, answer with reasons, report across groups. Expect the superlative + present perfect frame to be borrowed wholesale — that is planting for lesson 5. Typical error: the most tasty — logged, not corrected

Mini-debate · insects on the menu
pairs → class
Why: The unit's Talking point run with the Unit 6 agree/disagree phrases — an opinion with a reason attached, which is the exam-proof shape of an opinion.

WHAT WE DO: the Talking point with Unit 6's phrases recycled

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The motion on the board: In twenty years, people in our town will eat insects.
  2. Revive the Unit 6 menu in one minute: Personally, I think… · I'm not sure I agree… · Maybe you're right, but…
  3. Assign sides for round one (left partner argues FOR — remember the two billion), 90 seconds each way, then free choice for round two.
  4. Two pairs perform their best exchange; the class reacts only with menu phrases.
  5. Close with Ollie's line: Ollie says insects taste nutty. Would you try one for £10? Hands up.
📺 On screen: the motion and three phrases in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: argue assigned sides with phrases and reasons. Expect the two-billion fact to be the FOR side's anchor — that is why it was planted at 44′. Typical error: flat «no» — point at the menu

WHAT WE DO: self-check against the aim, homework, teaser

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Point at the food wall: Twelve words. One sentence each: name a food and describe it with two adjectives — one from each column if you dare. Three or four students answer.
  2. Thumb-vote: the twelve words? the sounds? opinions with because? — thumb up, sideways or down.
  3. Homework, books open: WB p.40, Vocabulary tasks 1–4 — show the word snake so nobody is lost at home.

WB Unit 10 (p.40–43)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can use the twelve food adjectives in personal opinions with reasons — pronounced and sorted (positive/negative) correctly.
  • Subsidiary. Careful reading in two exam-shaped passes (SB p.59); listening to the four-part taste test (tracks 43–46); an opinion with a reason in the debate.
  • Personal. Never say «exam format» out loud — the reading is just a text. Drill the four pronunciation traps by ear BEFORE the words are seen. Keep a FOOD WALL going: +/− columns, filled as the lesson runs.
By the end, students can…
  • name the twelve adjectives and sort them positive/negative
  • pronounce sour, raw, juicy, delicious, salmon right
  • read a long text in two passes (match, then one-word gaps)
  • give a food opinion with a reason (It's delicious because it's fresh)

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Bitter» — is coffee without sugar sweet or bitter? → bitter. Is bitter always bad? → no: dark chocolate is bitter and people pay extra for it.
«Raw» — cooked or not? → not cooked. Is raw fish always dangerous? → no: sushi is raw and millions eat it daily.
«Tasty» and «delicious» — which is stronger? → delicious: tasty is good, delicious is wow.

Assumptions: SB p.58–59, syllabus lesson 29. Units switch today: the money wall moves to the side, the food wall opens. Of the twelve adjectives the class usually owns three or four (sweet, fresh, maybe spicy from menus). The present perfect is two lessons old and appears here only receptively («the most disgusting thing I've ever eaten») — do not teach the contrast yet.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
