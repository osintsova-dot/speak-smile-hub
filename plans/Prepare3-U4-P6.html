🏠HomeworkYour own three-picture story (35 words, past tense) + Listening WS 4
🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Write a 35-word story from three pictures entirely in the past, and choose the right option from three when all three are mentioned in the recording.
What is genuinely hard
In the story: holding one tense from the first word to the last, and not forgetting the middle picture. In the listening: everything you see or read in the options is said out loud, so recognising a word proves nothing.
Typical error to expect
A story that starts in the past and slides into the present halfway through. In the exam a tense that does not match the question is a zero for that point [exam report]
A word from the option was said in the recording → they tick it. A matching word is not a correct answer; meaning decides [exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
This is the one lesson of the block where the exam format IS the topic, so it is framed from the first minute as «one skill, different wrapping». The writing half comes first because it needs the freshest heads and because it closes the block's grammar (past narrative) rather than opening anything new. The listening half is deliberately run twice — once as a walk-through with the audioscript line named out loud, once in silence under exam conditions — because the strategy only counts if it survives real conditions. The distractor analysis at 62′ is where the actual teaching happens: seeing the trap being built is what stops you falling into it. The last ten minutes open the school trainer and do the SAME skill in the Russian wrapper, live, so the transfer is experienced rather than promised.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“One skill, two wrappings. Today you do a Cambridge task and then the Russian version of exactly the same thing — and you will see that only the packaging changed. One warning about the listening: you will hear something about ALL THREE options. Hearing a word is not an answer.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, three minutes of collective feedback on the block test, and the bridge into the exam lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — test feedback, not a post-mortem. Hand the papers back face down. On the board, the three errors that cost the class the most marks, no names. Turn your paper over. Find one of these three in your own work. That is your line for today.
  3. Nothing else about the test today: the analysis clinic is a separate slot.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page one hundred and twenty-seven. Today is the one lesson in this block about exams — and about the fact that there are two of them.
📺 On screen: the three most expensive errors written in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: take their papers back and find one of the three errors in their own work. Expect relief that the score is not being read out. Typical problem: someone wants to argue a mark — one line, «see me after», and move on

WHAT WE DO: the map of the lesson: the same skill asked in two different wrappings

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Draw two boxes on the board with one arrow between them: on the left three options, listen twice; on the right the same words. One of these is a British exam. One is Russian. Which is which? They cannot tell — that is the point.
  2. Say the honest sentence: Some of you will take a Russian exam in two years. Some of you won't. Today's skill is for both of you, because it is the same skill.
  3. And the second honest sentence, about the writing half: ⚠️ Honest note for the teacher: this task has NO analogue in the OGE. We keep it because it is the best past-tense writing in the book and it feeds two things that ARE examined — the linking words of criterion K2 in task 35, and the storytelling of speaking task 3.
  4. The plan of the lesson in three lines on the board: story from pictures → listening with three options → the Russian version of the listening.
📺 On screen: the two boxes and the three-line plan in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: compare the two boxes and fail to tell them apart. Expect one student to ask which exam they will take — answer factually, in one sentence, and move on. Typical problem: anxiety; the «one skill, two wrappings» line is what removes it

WHAT WE DO: SB p.127 ex.1: the baking-competition example, taken apart

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the task instruction out loud, all three lines of it: Three pictures in a row and one instruction: «Look at the three pictures. Write the story shown in the pictures. Write 35 words or more.»
  2. The four strategies, one line each, straight from the book's Exam tips box: look at all three pictures and work out the WHOLE story before you write a word; · put something from every picture into the story — a picture with nothing about it is a lost mark; · tell it in the past tense from the first sentence to the last; · join it up: so, and, but, because, when, however.
  3. Read the sample answer together: SB p.127 ex.1, the worked example — a baking competition. Sample answer: «Last week, Jim saw a poster at school about a cooking competition. He loves baking, so he decided to enter it. He went home and baked an amazing chocolate cake. Jim took his cake to the competition and was very happy and surprised when he got first prize for it.»
  4. Take it apart with the TB p.255 questions: Analysis questions from TB p.255: is there something about every picture? (yes) · what tense is it in? (past — the best tense for telling a story) · why is the boy given a name? (it reads naturally and lets you vary between the name and «he») · is it longer than 35 words? (yes) · which linking words are used? (so, and ×2, when) · what does «it» refer to at the end of the second sentence? (the competition — it avoids repeating the word).
  5. Kagan Think-Pair-Share on the one that matters: Find the linking words. How many are there? (so, and ×2, when).
📺 On screen: the three example pictures and the sample answer on the screen so the class can annotate the same text (Backup: everything is printed on SB p.127 — work from the book and copy the four strategies onto the board)
👦 Students do: read the model and find its parts. Expect them to notice the past tense before you name it. Typical problem: they think 35 words means exactly 35 — the CCQ removes it

WHAT WE DO: SB p.127 ex.2: the music-competition story, built together then written alone against the clock

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Build the story ORALLY first, with the past-simple questions from TB p.255 — no writing yet, 4 minutes: Past-simple questions to build the story with before anyone writes (TB p.255): What kind of competition was this one? (a music competition) · What was the girl doing in the second picture? (she was practising the guitar) · How did the other people feel? (annoyed, because the music was too loud) · Did the competition go well? (yes, everyone was enjoying themselves).
  2. Elicit three or four linking words onto the board and leave them there: so · and · but · because · when · however.
  3. Two minutes on the past endings while the story is still oral: decided /ɪd/ · practised /t/ · played /d/. Say each, class repeats, then they say all three in one breath.
  4. WRITE: 8 minutes, silent mode, alone. Something about every picture. Past tense from the first word to the last. Thirty-five words or more.
  5. Two minutes before the end: Underline every verb. Is every one of them past?
📺 On screen: the three pictures on the screen and an 8-minute timer (Backup: the pictures are printed on SB p.127 — keep the book open at that page and time it on the wall clock, calling «two minutes» in writing on the board)
👦 Students do: build the story out loud, then write in silence. Expect 40–55 words from most. Typical error: the middle picture disappears — the underlining check at the end is where they catch it themselves

Improve · swap and repair
pairs
Why: Peer repair against a checklist, then the model. Comparing your own text with a good one teaches more than any correction written in red.

WHAT WE DO: peer repair against a three-item checklist, then the model answer

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap papers. Kagan RallyCoach with a fixed checklist: (1) is there something about every picture? (2) is every verb past? (3) how many linking words? Write the number in the margin.
  2. The rule of the stage: You may not rewrite your partner's story. You may only ask a question about it.
  3. Only NOW show the model answer (TB p.255): Model answer, TB p.255: «Last month Charlie saw a poster at school about a music competition. She loves playing the guitar, so she decided to enter it. She went home and practised a lot. Her family put their hands over their ears because they thought she sounded terrible. However, when she played on stage in the competition, everyone thought she was amazing. Charlie had a great night.»
  4. One question about the model, to the class: Which adjectives make it better? (terrible, amazing, great) — and which word joins the surprise? (however).
  5. Students add one adjective and one linking word to their own story in a different colour. That is the whole improvement.
📺 On screen: the model answer on the screen, revealed only at this point (Backup: write the model answer on the board while the pairs are checking, and turn the board round or uncover it when they finish)
👦 Students do: check a partner's story against three items, then improve their own. Expect the linking-word count to be one or two, and the model to have four. Typical error: they correct the partner's spelling instead of asking about the content

WHAT WE DO: SB p.128: the worked example (track 150) with the answer proved out loud

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The format in two lines: Five short conversations, one question each, three PICTURES as the options. The recording is played twice.
  2. ex.1 first, before any audio: Look at the three pictures for question 1. Write down words you think you will hear. Take them — desk, sofa, posters (TB p.256).
  3. ex.2: read the question carefully. Is it asking what Ted HAS or what he WANTS? (wants). Then play track 150.
  4. Prove the answer with the audioscript line, out loud: SB p.128 question 1 (track 150): «What would Ted like to get for his room?» — a desk, a sofa, some posters. TB p.256: the question asks about what he WANTS, not what he has. Answer B — the audioscript line is «The only thing I need now is a sofa».
  5. The three strategies onto the board, where they stay for the next two stages: you will hear something about every picture — only one of them answers the question; · read the question before the recording starts and work out exactly what is being asked; · the second listening is for checking, not for starting again.
📺 On screen: track 150 in the board app, with the three pictures on the screen (Backup: the pictures are printed on SB p.128 and the audioscript is on TB p.305 — read it out yourself, twice, at natural speed)
👦 Students do: predict words, read the question, then hear the answer proved. Expect them to choose A before the question is read properly — that is the demonstration. Typical problem: they want the answer immediately; make them name the question first

Listening Part 1 · exam conditions
solo, silent
Why: Timed, silent, played twice: first listening to answer, second to check. That habit is the whole difference between four marks and two.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.128–129 ex.3 (track 151): questions 2–5, timed, played twice, no help

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Give them 90 seconds with the pictures and the questions before anything plays. Ask the TB p.256 orientation questions: Question 2 — where are all these things? Question 4 — what do pictures A and C have in common? (both show water).
  2. Play track 151. First time you answer. Then thirty seconds of silence with pens down.
  3. Play track 151 again. Second time you check. Change an answer only if you can say why.
  4. Check with Kagan Showdown — A, B or C on the mini-whiteboard, all boards up at once. Key: 2 C · 3 A · 4 A · 5 B (TB p.256).
📺 On screen: track 151 in the board app (Backup: read the audioscript from TB p.305 aloud yourself, twice, at natural speed and with no extra help — the pictures are in the students' own book)
👦 Students do: listen twice under exam conditions, then reveal together. Expect 3 out of 4. Typical error: question 3 (the price of the bag) — three prices are said, and the one that matters is the one after «from today»

62–70′
Why the wrong answer is wrong
pairs → class
Why: Analysing distractors teaches more than checking answers: they see the trap being built, so they recognise it next time.

WHAT WE DO: the distractor analysis: for each question, name why the OTHER two are wrong

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Numbered Heads Together: each pair takes one question and must produce a sentence beginning B is wrong because …
  2. Feed them the evidence lines where they are stuck: 2 (What is still in the car?) — C: «The other one [bag of shopping]'s on the back seat I think.» A and B are things that have already been taken out. · 3 (How much is the bag?) — A: «from today that one is £15». The other prices are said in the recording too, which is the trap. · 4 (Where did they go camping last year?) — A: «It was lovely by that lake, wasn't it?» · 5 (What are the boys going to do now?) — B: «Let's go to that new café in town.»
  3. Name the trap out loud, once, and write it on the board: Where marks go: all three pictures are mentioned. Students tick the first one they recognise instead of waiting for the sentence that answers the actual question.
  4. The rule they take away, in their own words: Hearing a word is not an answer. Ask three students to say it back before you move on.
📺 On screen: the four questions and their evidence lines on the screen, revealed one at a time (Backup: read the evidence line out from TB p.256 for each question and write the key phrase on the board)
👦 Students do: explain why each wrong option is wrong. Expect this to be harder than choosing the right one, which is exactly why it is here. Typical error: «because it's wrong» — push for the evidence line every time

WHAT WE DO: SB p.129 (tracks 152–153): the Bodyfit Gym notes — five gaps, the same skill as SB p.45

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Remind them where they have met this: Exactly the same skill as this unit's SB p.45 listening — and exactly the same skill as OGE tasks 6–11.
  2. Play track 152 (the example) first and ask the book's question: Why is Saturday right and Friday wrong (TB p.256)? Saturday is the day of the club; Friday is the last day you can book. Both are said.
  3. Predict before you play the task: TB p.256 — predict the kind of answer first: 1 a price · 2 a morning time · 3 a first name OR a surname (not both — it is one word) · 4 an article of clothing · 5 something connected to sport. Warn them: gap 3 will be spelled out, and gap 4 has a negative in it, so they are listening for what is NOT allowed.
  4. Play track 153 twice, with the silent thirty seconds in between. Pairs compare only after the second play. Key: 1 12.50 · 2 10.20 · 3 Hughie · 4 trousers · 5 towel.
  5. Say the trap after the check: Where marks go: numbers written out in words and then misspelt, and a spelled-out name written down without checking it back on the second listening.
📺 On screen: tracks 152 and 153 in the board app, with the notes on the screen (Backup: read the audioscript from TB p.305–306 yourself, twice for each part, at natural speed; the notes are printed in the students' own book)
👦 Students do: predict, listen twice, then compare. Expect gap 3 (the spelled name Hughie) to be the one that decides the score. Typical error: gap 4 answered with what you SHOULD wear — the negative in the gap was read too fast

WHAT WE DO: the same skill in the Russian wrapper: four questions, three options, live in the school trainer

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Open the trainer link on the screen and say what it is in one sentence: Four short recordings, one question each, three options A/B/C. In the OGE these are tasks 2–5, four marks. Every recording is played TWICE.
  2. Point out the topic before you start: The first one is a hiking instructor briefing a group — the Unit 7 topic, so the words (hiking, trousers, boots, fire) are already theirs.
  3. Do question 1 together, out loud, with the same move as the Cambridge version: What exactly is the question asking? Then listen.
  4. Questions 2–4 in pairs on one screen or one phone, 4 minutes. The app plays the recording and marks it.
  5. Key: 1 — option 2 · 2 — option 1 · 3 — option 3 · 4 — option 2. The app shows the key and the evidence line the moment students submit.

  7. And name what is the same: Same skill as Listening Part 1 in the coursebook: three options, the answer is the one the speaker MEANS, not the one whose words come up in the recording.

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can tell a three-picture story in the past in about 35 words with linking words and answer 3-option listening questions by meaning rather than by matching words.
  • Subsidiary. Completing a set of notes from a recording (Listening Part 2, SB p.129); naming why a wrong option is wrong.
  • Personal. Say the honest thing out loud about the picture story: it has no analogue in the Russian exam. We keep it because it is the best past-tense writing in the book. Children trust a teacher who says which parts are for the exam and which are for the language.
By the end, students can…
  • build a story from three pictures before writing a word
  • hold the past tense across a whole short text
  • join a story with so, and, but, because, when
  • say why the other two options in a listening question are wrong

Structure

Form
past simple throughout the story; linking words so · and · but · because · when · however. In the notes task: one word, a number, a date or a time — never more.
Pron. watch
the /d/, /t/ and /ɪd/ endings of the regular past: decided /ɪd/ · practised /t/ · played /d/. Two minutes inside the writing stage, not a separate block.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Write 35 words or more.» Is 30 words enough? → no. Is 60 a problem? → no: «or more» means more is fine.
«Look at the three pictures.» How many of them must appear in the story? → all three. A picture with nothing about it is a lost mark.
«You will hear something about each picture.» So if I hear «sofa», is B the answer? → not necessarily. The question decides, not the word.
In the notes task, gap 4 asks what NOT to wear. Am I listening for allowed or forbidden clothes? → forbidden. Read the gap before the recording starts.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
