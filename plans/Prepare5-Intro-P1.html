🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Get every student speaking English within four minutes of the bell, agree a class contract that will be enforceable all year, and turn «I want to improve my English» into three named, checkable goals in language terms.
What is genuinely hard
Ten weeks of silence. In September teenagers understand far more than they can produce, read that gap as «I have forgotten everything», and go quiet — which makes the gap worse. The second difficulty is goal-setting: left alone, students write «to speak better», which cannot be checked in December and therefore motivates nobody.
Typical error to expect
The September silence — after ten weeks without English, students understand far more than they can produce, and they read that gap as «I have forgotten everything». Naming it out loud in lesson one is worth more than any revision exercise.
Why the lesson is built this way
Nothing is taught today and everything is established. The line-up puts every student in front of six different partners in ten minutes, so the first English of the year is said sideways to one person rather than out loud to the room. The contract is written BY the class, in English, in six lines — a rule you wrote yourself is a rule you can be held to. The year map is shown as a real object (the contents pages of their own book) rather than as a promise, and the goals stage converts wishes into sentences with a date and a verb in them.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“You've had ten weeks off. Right now you understand more English than you can say — that's normal, and it lasts about two weeks. Today: who we are, how this room works, and exactly where we're going between now and May.”

0–5′
Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting in a circle, names learned, and the first English of the year said by everybody

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what's the best thing about today so far? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — name and one word. Stand in a circle. Each student says their name and ONE English word for their summer. You go first and make yours ordinary rather than impressive (Oksana. Rain.).
  3. Go round twice: the second time, each student says the name and the word of the person on their left. That is how names get learned, and it is also the first listening task of the year.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): set the day up: Three things today: how this room works, where we're going this year, and what YOU want out of it. Nothing to write down for the first twenty minutes.
📺 On screen: no technology in this stage — a circle and your voice, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone says at least three words of English in the first four minutes, and everyone is listened to once

Speaking line-up · how was your summer?
two lines, standing
Why: Speaking line-up · how was your summer?

WHAT WE DO: six questions, six partners, everyone speaking at the same time

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two lines facing each other (Kagan Line-Ups). Hand out six question cards, one per pair of positions: Where did you spend the summer? · What was the best thing you did? · Did you use any English? · What did you read or watch? · What are you looking forward to this year? · What's one thing you did for the first time?
  2. Ninety seconds per pair, then one line moves one place to the right. Six rounds.
  3. Two rules, said once before they start: Answers are two sentences minimum. If you can't say a word, describe it.
  4. Do NOT correct anything during this stage. Circulate, listen, and note two or three names against what they can already do.
  5. After the sixth round, one whole-class harvest, thirty seconds: Tell me one interesting thing you heard — about somebody else, not about you.
📺 On screen: the six questions on cards in their hands — printed in advance, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: every student speaks for about six minutes in total and hears six different people. The simultaneous format means nobody performs in front of the room on day one
6 question cards (2 sets) · timer or bell
20–34′
The class contract
groups of four → board
Why: The class contract

WHAT WE DO: six rules, written by the class, in English, that will be used all year

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of four, four minutes: Write four rules for this room. Two about what WE do, two about what the TEACHER does. The second half is the one that makes them take it seriously.
  2. Collect on the board — but write nothing until a student has said it in English. Reshape their wording rather than replacing it.
  3. Aim for six lines and make sure these four ideas are among them, prompting with a question if they do not appear: English first, mistakes are information, phones face down, everybody speaks.
  4. The English-first rule needs its own thirty seconds, because it is the one that gets misremembered: An idea can start in Russian. It only counts when it's said in English. I'll help you get there.
  5. Everyone copies the six lines onto the inside cover of their Student's Book. That is where it lives all year, not on a poster that falls off the wall in October.
📺 On screen: the six rules built up on the board in the students' own words — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: write four rules in fours, negotiate the final six as a class, then copy them into their own book
board · Student's Books
34–38′
Move break · find someone who
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute mingle that recycles the summer questions at speed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands with a slip of paper listing six things: …went to the sea · …read a book in English · …learned something new · …got up before eight every day · …travelled by train · …did nothing at all for a week.
  2. Find a different person for each line and write their name. The question has to be asked properly — Did you…? — not shown on the paper.
  3. Three minutes, fast, no sitting. Then one summary sentence from you using three names.
  4. Sit them down for the year map: Books out. Page two.
📺 On screen: one slip per student, printed in advance — nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone asks and answers about twelve questions in three minutes, standing and moving
1 slip per student
38–52′
The map of the year
individual → pairs → class
Why: The map of the year

WHAT WE DO: the contents pages of their own book, read as a real document

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open at the contents pages (SB p.2–5). Do not talk over them: give a task instead.
  2. Pairs, four minutes, three questions on the board: 1 Find a unit you're actually interested in. 2 Find a grammar point you remember from last year. 3 Find something you've never heard of.
  3. Harvest all three, quickly, by nomination. The third question is the useful one — it tells you what the year looks like from their side.
  4. Now the shape of the year in ninety seconds, with the numbers written on the board: twenty units in ten blocks · two units then a review and a test · a Culture page or a Life Skills page every four units · eight exam lessons across the year.
  5. Point at the back of the book: Page one hundred and twenty — the exam section. Page one hundred and forty-one — every word you'll learn this year, listed. Page one hundred and forty-seven — the grammar reference. That's where you go when you're stuck.
📺 On screen: the three questions on the board, and the students' own contents pages open in front of them. Backup: the book is the document, so nothing can fail
👦 Students do: search their own book with three questions, then hear the year's shape in numbers rather than in promises

52–60′
Where the exam sits — thirty seconds of honesty
class
Why: Naming the exam once, early and calmly is what stops it leaking into every lesson as anxiety. Silence about it does the opposite.

WHAT WE DO: the state exam named once, put in its place, and left alone

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Say it once, calmly, and do not expand: Some of you will sit the state exam at the end of school. This course is not an exam course — it's an English course — but most of what the exam tests is what we do here anyway: reading long texts, writing emails, listening for detail, speaking with reasons.
  2. Then the practical part, which is what they actually want to know: there is an optional trainer with real exam tasks, the link comes in the homework, and using it changes no mark in this room.
  3. Take questions for no more than three minutes. If the conversation grows, park it: Come and ask me after the lesson — I'll give you a straight answer, but I'm not spending our first lesson on it.
  4. Close the topic deliberately and move: Right. Your turn. What do YOU want by December?
📺 On screen: nothing written up — this stage is deliberately unrecorded, so there is nothing to fail
👦 Students do: listen, ask if they want to, and see the subject placed in proportion rather than avoided

Goals · three sentences with a date in them
individual → pairs
Why: Goals · three sentences with a date in them

WHAT WE DO: wishes converted into checkable targets

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two goals on the board, one useless and one usable: I want to improve my English. against By December I want to write an email without a dictionary. Ask the class what the difference is.
  2. Extract the rule from them and write it up: A goal needs a VERB and a DATE.
  3. Four minutes, silent: each student writes three goals — one about speaking, one about writing or reading, one entirely their own.
  4. Kagan Think-Pair-Share, ninety seconds each way: read your goals to your partner; the partner's job is to ask How will you know? about each one.
  5. Two minutes to repair. Then the goals go on the inside cover of the book, under the contract, and you tell them the truth: I read these in lesson four, and we look at them again in December.
📺 On screen: the two example goals on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: write three goals, have them challenged by a partner, then repair and record them where they will be found again

72–86′
🎯 Speaking out · two-minute interviews
pairs → class
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · two-minute interviews

WHAT WE DO: the first real production of the year, with a listening job attached

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. New pairs — someone they did not talk to in the line-up. Each student interviews the other for two minutes with their own questions. Four questions minimum, and one of them has to be a follow-up invented on the spot.
  2. The listener takes three notes only. No full sentences.
  3. Then the report, which is where the language work is: each student introduces their partner to the class in three sentences, from the notes. Third person, so the grammar shifts.
  4. You take one note per student as they report — this is the diagnostic that matters more than any test, and it is why this stage is not cut when time is short.
  5. Do not correct anything in public today. One private word each at the door if needed.
📺 On screen: no technology in this stage — pairs, notes and voices, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: every student is interviewed, interviews someone, and speaks to the whole class for about thirty seconds — on day one, which sets the expectation for the year

Wrap-up + homework
desks → door
Why: Self-assessment against the aim they heard at the start, so the lesson closes the loop it opened. Homework comes from their own workbook, not from a photocopy.

WHAT WE DO: self-check against today's aim, then the homework said once and written on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Back to the aims: Hands up if you spoke English to at least six different people today. Hands up if your goals have a date in them.
  2. Exit at the door: each student says one thing they are looking forward to this year.
  3. Homework said once and written on the board: books to every lesson, goals on the inside cover. Add the honest line: Next lesson is vocabulary from last year, and there's a short measurement at the end. It doesn't go in any report — it tells me how to teach you.
📺 On screen: the homework on the board while they pack — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: self-assess, then one sentence each at the door

Structure
Where did you spend the summer? · What was the best thing you did? · How much English did you use? · By December I want to be able to… · This year I'm going to…
Form
past simple questions for the summer, going to and want to be able to for the goals — no new grammar is taught today.
Pron. watch
the first English of the year is slow and quiet; do not correct pronunciation at all today except where meaning fails. The one thing worth modelling is the question intonation that makes a line-up sound like a conversation rather than an interrogation
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«By December I want to be able to write an email without a dictionary.» Can we check that in December? → yes. That is what makes it a goal rather than a wish.
«I want to improve my English.» How would we know if you had? → we wouldn't. Rewrite it with a verb and a date.
The contract says English first. Does that mean Russian is banned? → no. It means an idea may start in Russian and then gets said in English before it counts.

Assumptions: Syllabus lesson 1, the first lesson of the year. The group finished Prepare 4 in May and has had ten weeks without English. Books have been bought but not opened. Some students may be new to the group — the line-up is built to absorb them without a separate introduction stage.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Nobody speaks in the first ten minutesThe line-up makes it impossible not to: everyone speaks simultaneously to one person, sideways, with a question on a card. There is no whole-class speaking in the first half of this lesson for exactly that reason.
The contract becomes a list of teacher's rulesYou write nothing on the board until a student has said it. If the class produces only three rules, add the fourth as a question: What should happen when somebody makes a mistake?
Goals come out as «to speak better»Every goal is rewritten twice: once with a verb (write, understand, say), once with a date (by December, by May). Two examples of yours go on the board first, one good and one useless.
A new student in an established groupPut them second from the end of the line-up so they meet everybody, and pair them in the interview stage with the student who speaks most — not with the strongest.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: nothing specific today — and that is deliberate. The exam is named once, in the year map, and then left alone.

The first lesson of the year sets the working culture, and a culture built on exam fear produces silent teenagers. What the exam DOES get today is thirty seconds of honesty in the year map: those of you sitting the state exam in grade 11 will find that this course covers most of its reading and writing skills, and there is an optional trainer link in the homework. That is all. The goal-setting stage then makes each student name a personal target in language terms, not in marks.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
