🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use five reporting verbs (announce, demand, explain, insist, suggest) with the right pattern, listen for gist in six short conversations (Listening Part 2), and describe a photograph for about a minute using speculation language (Speaking Part 2).
What is genuinely hard
The pattern, not the meaning: suggest, insist and demand take that + subject + verb, and Russian uses an infinitive after all three — «he suggested to go» is the sentence that will appear. The listening is hard because the printed option never uses the recording's words. And the speaking fails in a way that has nothing to do with language: candidates run out of things to say after twenty seconds, because they describe only what is certain and never speculate.
Typical error to expect
«He suggested to go», «she insisted me to stay» — suggest, insist and demand take that + subject + verb, not an infinitive. They are the three that break most often because Russian uses an infinitive after all of them.
«He said me», «she told that» — tell takes a person (tell somebody that), say does not (say that / say TO somebody). One Russian verb, two English patterns, and the test has it as a whole item.
Why the lesson is built this way
The reporting verbs come straight out of yesterday's grammar — same sentences, richer verbs — so the lesson is an upgrade rather than a new topic, and the pattern rule is derived from six examples the class can already read. The pair activity on p.138 and p.140 is a genuine information gap: your partner reads a situation and you choose the verb. The listening is prepared the exam's way — underline the key words in the question AND circle them in the options — and checked with the proof phrase, because in Part 2 the proof is always a paraphrase. The speaking half is built backwards: describe first, hear a model, reconstruct the phrase bank from memory, then do the exam task in threes with the timer running.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“One minute is a long time when you are looking at a photograph and you have already said everything you are sure about. Today: the words that fill the other forty seconds — it looks like, it might be, I don't know for sure but perhaps.”

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: the pattern derived from six examples, then produced

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: read the six examples and let the class guess the meanings — dictionaries allowed, thirty seconds. Then, in mixed-ability pairs, two more sentences for each verb, spoken not written.
  2. Complete the rule box together and put it against the board line: the rule for the three difficult ones: suggested / insisted / demanded + THAT + subject + VERB — and that is optional in the first two examples but the pattern is not. Point out that that is optional in the first two examples, but the PATTERN is not.
  3. ex.2, three minutes: choose the reporting verb for four contexts, with announce given as the example. 1 explain · 2 demand · 3 suggest · 4 insist (the example is announce) Check meaning as you go: demand is asking in a way that expects no refusal; explain is making something easy to understand; suggest is offering an idea to consider.
  4. ex.3, four minutes individually: the verb AND the form after it. 1 explained; should / had to finish · 2 demanded; gave · 3 suggested; see · 4 insisted; help Volunteers read the whole sentence at the check — the whole sentence, because the pattern only exists in one.
  5. Thirty-second drill, three times, whole class: She suggested that we went. He insisted that I helped. They demanded that he gave it back.
📺 On screen: the pattern line with the six example sentences beneath it — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: guess meanings, build two extra sentences per verb in pairs, then produce four patterns in writing

WHAT WE DO: ex.2–3, track 62: the exam task, then checked with the paraphrase named

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Track 62, first hearing, straight through. They answer as they listen.
  2. Pairs compare, 90 seconds, and mark the ones they disagree on.
  3. Second hearing, whole. Say what it is for: Not to decide again — to check the two you rejected.
  4. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Listening Part 2, track 62: 1 C · 2 B · 3 B · 4 A · 5 C · 6 C — and the proof phrases are worth taking aloud: «why did he end up…?; I hate it when they change stuff» · «I just can't get there for the start» · «like I was part of the whole thing myself» · «the character's pretty true to life» · «selecting the films; do a good job on» · «decide on your message»
  5. For every item, write the PROOF PHRASE on the board next to the option, so the paraphrase is visible: «I hate it when they change stuff» is why the answer is C, not because anybody said the word disappointed.
  6. One minute of accounting: how many changed on the second hearing, and how many of those changes were right? The second number is the useful one.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2, track 63: their own description first, then a model to compare with

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 in pairs, three minutes: look at the photograph on the page and make notes — who, what, where, what else. Do not let them speak in full sentences yet; notes only.
  2. Take thirty seconds of the room's ideas, then play track 63 and ask the one comparison question: What did Marcus say that you didn't?
  3. Two or three students report their comparison to the class. The answer is almost always the same — he guessed, and they only described.
  4. Write the difference on the board in two words: DESCRIBE + GUESS. That is what fills a minute.
  5. Play the recording again if the room wants it — it is short and the second hearing is where they notice the phrases.
📺 On screen: the two-word rule on the board. Backup: if track 63 fails, read Marcus's description aloud from the audioscript (TB p.301) — the comparison task is unaffected
👦 Students do: make notes on a photograph in pairs, then compare their own description with a model

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: the two columns filled from memory, then ticked against the recording

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two minutes to study the Prepare to speak box. Then books CLOSED.
  2. Pairs, five minutes against the clock: write as many phrases as you can remember into the two board columns — DESCRIBING and SPECULATING. Teams call them out, you write them up.
  3. Open the books and complete the columns: Speculating: It looks like a … · It could be … · Maybe they're … · They might be … · I don't know for sure, but perhaps … · She seems to be … — Organising the description: In the foreground there are … · In the background I can see … · On the left / right … — and the rule that beats all of them: keep talking until the examiner stops you, and do not describe anything that is not in the picture
  4. Play track 63 again and everyone ticks the phrases they actually hear. track 63 — the phrases Marcus actually uses: I can see… · It looks like they are… · I don't know what it's called · In the foreground there are… · I think the… · It might be…
  5. Thirty seconds on intonation, because it is what makes speculation sound like speculation: It might be a market falls at the end. Said flat, it sounds like a fact; said rising, it sounds like a question.
  6. The columns stay on the board for the exam task. That is not cheating — it is how the phrases get into their heads.

🎯 Speaking Part 2 · one photograph, one minute, in threes
groups of three
Why: 🎯 Speaking Part 2 · one photograph, one minute, in threes

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 (SB p.138): the exam task with a visible timer, run twice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of three: one examiner, two candidates. Send them to p.138 and decide who is A and who is B.
  2. Two minutes to look at the photographs and note useful vocabulary; collect a few words on the board. Then the checklist of what can be described, built by the class: who the people are, what they are doing, where they are, what they are wearing, the weather, the objects.
  3. Round one: A talks for ONE MINUTE, timed by the examiner, while B listens. Then B talks about the other photograph. The examiner's job is the clock and the back-up prompts — talk about the people, the place, the other things.
  4. Rotate roles and repeat with a different partner. Everybody speaks twice and examines once.
  5. Monitor with a sheet: six good phrases WITH names, six errors WITHOUT. Interrupt nobody.
  6. Two volunteer pairs repeat for the class, three minutes: the class listens with the phrase columns visible and marks every phrase they hear used.
  7. Then ex.5, three minutes in threes: the questions about different forms of entertainment, as many reasons as they can. Fast finishers write two sentences of opinion on question 3.
  8. Deferred correction, two minutes: good phrases with names first, then the errors, unattributed, repaired by the class.
  9. Exam information for Speaking Part 2 is SB p.132 — and say aloud that this is where next lesson starts.
📺 On screen: the phrase columns stay up during the task and the good phrases with names go up at the end — no devices, this is the speaking stage
👦 Students do: every student speaks for two full minutes as a candidate and runs the clock once as examiner

Structure
He announced that the instructor was going to be late. · He insisted that she did the course. · They suggested that I saw The Boundary. · It looks like they are waiting for somebody. · In the foreground there are two people.
Form
suggest / insist / demand + THAT + subject + verb (never an infinitive) · announce that · explain that · tell SOMEBODY that · speculation: it looks like + clause, it could / might be + noun, they seem to be + -ing.
Pron. watch
the film words move their stress when they change class and the class will keep it still: DIRector but diRECT · PERformance but perFORM · REcording (noun) against reCORDing (verb) · ANimated has four syllables and the stress on the first · and in reported speech the backshifted forms are unstressed and swallowed — he'd, they'd, she'd — which is why students do not hear the shift in the recording
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«He suggested to go to the cinema» — possible? → no. Say it properly: he suggested that we went to the cinema.
The recording says «I just can't get there for the start» and the option says «he arrives late». Same thing? → yes. Will the word late be in the recording? → probably not. That is Part 2.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«He suggested to go» survives everythingThe pattern goes on the board as one line with the three verbs grouped: SUGGEST / INSIST / DEMAND + that + subject + verb. Every answer in the stage is read as a whole sentence, and the whole sentence is what makes the pattern audible.
Listening Part 2 answered by matching a wordQuestion 1 is worked through as a class and the proof phrase is written on the board beside the option, so the class sees the paraphrase. Then the rule is stated once and referred back to.
The photo description dies after twenty secondsThe phrase bank is reconstructed from memory before the task and stays on the board during it. And the timer is visible: a minute is a target, not a threat, and pairs are told to keep going until it beeps.
Two confident students do all the class feedbackAll the speaking is in threes and everybody is the examiner once. Public repetition is by rotation and never the same pair twice.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 2 (the true / false / not stated dialogue) and the oral task 3 (describing a photograph aloud).

Listening Part 2 is gist listening where the printed option never repeats the recording's words — the same trap as the Russian task 2, where a statement is 'not stated' precisely because the words are there and the meaning is not. And the speaking half is the closest match in the whole course to the Russian oral photo description: one candidate, one photograph, about a minute, and speculation phrases (it looks like, it might be) are what fill it.
