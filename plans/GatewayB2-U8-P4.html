🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Present and interpret numerical data out loud: proportions (a third · one in three · the majority of · just over a quarter · four-fifths), trends (rose sharply · fell gradually · doubled · fluctuated · stayed the same) and the degree adverbs that make a number mean something (significantly · dramatically · slightly · steadily). The outcome is a one-minute spoken presentation of a pie chart to a partner who has a different one.
What is genuinely hard
Numbers are where fluent students suddenly stop being fluent. Three specific difficulties: saying fractions aloud (two-thirds and four-fifths are the two hardest consonant clusters in the language), the article (the 40% of people is the default Russian rendering and it is wrong), and the stress shift between an ˈincrease and to inˈcrease. And the task itself is not description — it is comparison and comment, which students avoid because listing is easier.
Typical error to expect
«The 40% of people», «a half of the damage was caused» — no article before a percentage, and half can go with or without of but never with a: half the damage / half of the damage. With one in ten there is no of at all.
Also expect the amount of disasters for a countable noun (the NUMBER of disasters) and increased on 20% (increased BY 20%, or increased TO 20% if it is the new total — two different sentences and students use them interchangeably).
Why the lesson is built this way
This is a whole ninety minutes on speaking, which is what the syllabus asks for, so the shape is: language in, language practised, language performed, language corrected. The chart in exercise 1 is met as a set of six true/false statements rather than as a description task, because judging somebody else's sentence is far more diagnostic than producing your own. Fractions get their own short drill with the recording. The Speaking bank is then used before it is read: the class builds sentences about a line graph and only afterwards checks the bank. The final twenty minutes is an information-gap — Student A has one chart, Student B has a different one on page 149 — so both partners have a genuine reason to listen.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
Between 1900 and 1919 there were 157 recorded disasters in the United States. Between 2000 and 2019 there were 8,067. That is not a small increase — it is more than fifty times. Now say that sentence well, out loud, in one minute, to somebody who cannot see your chart. That is today. And it is the exact skill the hardest task on the Russian exam paper pays for.

WHAT WE DO: greeting at the door, then reporting passives about the climate

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — the last time the weather changed your plans. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — rewind, the reporting passive, aimed at data. On the board: It is believed that … · It has been reported that … · … is known to have …
  3. RallyRobin: one sentence each about the climate or the weather using one of the three frames. Two minutes, then three aloud.
  4. You will get It is believed that the climate is getting warmer. Take it and push once: Believed by whom? On what evidence? — and there is the bridge to a lesson about figures.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): «It is believed» is what you say when you have no numbers. Today you have numbers. Page 104.
📺 On screen: the three frames written on the board. Backup: dictate them.
👦 Students do: one reporting-passive sentence each about the climate, then three read aloud.

5–13′
Lead-in · which disaster costs the most?
pairs → whole class
Why: A prediction that is about to be publicly falsified is the cheapest possible way to make a chart interesting, and the ranking on the board gives the next stage something to argue with.

WHAT WE DO: the Teacher's Book warmer — a guess that the chart will immediately settle

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. Write on the board and say it: In a typical year, which kind of natural disaster or extreme weather do you think causes the most economic damage?
  2. Pairs or threes, three minutes. Send them to page 106 (the Check it vocabulary list) if they need the words — that is the Teacher's Book's own suggestion and it recycles lesson 1 at the same time.
  3. Take four guesses and write them on the board in a rank order the class agrees on. Do not confirm anything.
  4. Then the honest frame for the whole lesson: You are all going to be wrong about at least one of those, and in about ten minutes you will see exactly how wrong. That is what data is for.
  5. Books open at page 104. Give them fifteen seconds just to look at the pie chart, no task.
📺 On screen: the class's guessed ranking written on the board and left up for comparison. Backup: none needed — board and voice.
👦 Students do: guess and rank four disaster types in pairs, using the vocabulary list on page 106 if needed.

13–25′
Reading the chart · six statements, true or false
desks → pairs
Why: Judging a statement forces the student to parse the language of proportion precisely, which producing their own sentence lets them avoid. It is also the fastest diagnostic of who can and cannot read «just over a third».

WHAT WE DO: SB p.104 exercise 1 — judging somebody else's sentences about data, which is harder than writing your own

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 1, six True/False statements about the pie chart «Economic damage by disaster type, 1995–2016». Individually first, five minutes, then check with a partner.
  2. The chart: storms 38% ($875bn) · floods 24% ($550bn) · earthquakes and volcanoes 20% ($643bn) · extreme temperature 5% ($106bn) · drought 2% ($49bn) · wildfire 2% ($47bn).
  3. Answers, and take each one with the reasoning, not just the letter: the pie chart, economic damage by disaster type 1995–2016: 1 True (storms 38%) · 2 False (earthquakes and volcanoes 20%, which is not «just over a third») · 3 False (floods 24% is MORE than earthquakes and volcanoes 20%) · 4 True (droughts 5% — less than a tenth) · 5 False (floods 24% — just under a quarter, not just over) · 6 True (storms 38% + floods 24% = 62%)
  4. Statements 2 and 5 are the ones that separate the class, and both hinge on one word. Put the two expressions on the board side by side and drill the difference: just OVER a third (more than 33%) versus just UNDER a quarter (a little less than 25%). Ask: Is 24% just over a quarter or just under? Is 20% just over a third?
  5. Fast finishers (Teacher's Book): write two more true/false sentences about the same chart and read them to the class at the end of the stage for the others to judge.
  6. Compare the chart with the board ranking from the lead-in and let the room react. Then the follow-up question: Which of these are a problem in our country? Does any of it surprise you?
📺 On screen: the two expressions (just over / just under) written on the board. Backup: the chart is in the students' own book — nothing to project.
👦 Students do: judge six statements against a pie chart, justify each, then write two of their own if they finish early.
SB p.104 · board
25–35′
Fractions and percentages · the drill nobody else does
desks → chorus
Why: Proportions are the one part of this language that is a pure pronunciation problem, and a student who cannot say «four-fifths» will simply avoid it for the rest of their life. Three minutes of choral drilling fixes it.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.104 exercises 2a and 2b with track 46 — six conversions, then the pronunciation that fails

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 2a, two minutes: convert six fractions and percentages both ways. Say the instruction the Teacher's Book insists on: Write them in figures, not in words.
  2. Exercise 2b: play track 46 and check. Answers: track 46: 1 25% · 2 one-fifth · 3 one-tenth · 4 two-thirds · 5 50% · 6 four-fifths.
  3. Now play it again, pausing after each item, and drill each one chorally twice. This is the stage that makes the difference and it is the stage most teachers skip.
  4. The three that break every class, on the board with the phonetics: two-thirds /tuːˈθɜːdz/ · four-fifths /fɔːˈfɪfθs/ · three-quarters /θriːˈkwɔːtəz/. Drill each four times, slowly then at speed.
  5. Then the grammar note that costs marks, from the Teacher's Book, written up: Three-quarters OF the disasters occurred in the summer. · The majority OF the avalanches were small. · About seven in ten tornadoes happen in the US. — no of with one in ten · Half the city / half of the city — both fine, but never a half of.
  6. Finish with a fast round: you say a percentage, the class says the fraction, and back again. Ten items, thirty seconds.
📺 On screen: track 46 played twice — once straight, once with pauses. Backup: if the audio fails, read the six items aloud yourself: 25 per cent · one-fifth · one-tenth · two-thirds · 50 per cent · four-fifths, and drill them chorally exactly as you would with the recording. Nothing is lost.
👦 Students do: convert six fractions in figures, check with the recording, then repeat each one chorally twice and race percentages against fractions.

35–39′
Stir · line up by number
open floor
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies. It also forces every student in the room to say a fraction aloud at least twice, which is the one thing the lesson must not leave to chance.

WHAT WE DO: four minutes on their feet that makes them say figures aloud without a script

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand each student a card with a percentage or a fraction on it — ⅓ · 12% · ¾ · 50% · ⅕ · 66% · 90% · ⅛ · 25% · 40% and so on.
  2. Line-Ups: Line up from smallest to largest. You may not show your card. You have to SAY your number.
  3. Ninety seconds, then walk the line and have each student read their number aloud in order. Any mispronunciation is corrected instantly by the whole class repeating it.
  4. Second round, thirty seconds: Now swap cards with somebody and line up again.
📺 On screen: the number cards. Backup: write the numbers on scraps of paper by hand — the stage needs nothing else.
👦 Students do: line up by value using only spoken numbers, then read the whole line aloud in order.

39–51′
Trends · the graph, and the Speaking bank used before it is read
pairs → desks
Why: Meeting the trend language inside a text about a real graph, and only then meeting it as a list, is what makes the Speaking bank a tool rather than something to be learned by heart.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.104 exercises 3 and 4 — the movement verbs and the adverbs that grade them

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 3: the line graph «Natural disasters in the US, 1980–2016» with a short text underneath in which the trend language is in bold. Read the text aloud yourself, a sentence at a time, and after each one elicit a translation or a paraphrase — that is the Teacher's Book's method and it is faster than explaining.
  2. Explain only what does not survive elicitation, which is normally one word: fluctuate = change frequently, up and down.
  3. Exercise 4, pairs, five minutes: find the word in the Speaking bank that means each of ten things (go up · go from 20,000 to 40,000 · go from 40,000 to 30,000 · one in three · slowly and in small amounts · important · in a sudden and surprising way · most of the people in a group · 50% · three times more).
  4. Answers: 1 rise / increase · 2 double · 3 fall / decrease · 4 a third · 5 gradually / steadily · 6 significant · 7 sharply / dramatically · 8 the majority · 9 half · 10 triple.
  5. Then the two pronunciation points the Teacher's Book flags, drilled and written up: half /hɑːf/ versus halve /hɑːv/ — noun and verb; and the stress shift an ˈincrease versus to inˈcrease, same for decrease. Drill both pairs in sentences, never in isolation.
  6. Close the stage by making them look at the Speaking bank as a whole and telling them what it is: Numbers and proportions on top, trends underneath. Everything you need for the next twenty minutes is on that box. Do not memorise it — use it.
📺 On screen: the two pronunciation pairs written on the board. Backup: the graph and the Speaking bank are in the students' own book; no display needed.
👦 Students do: paraphrase a text about a graph sentence by sentence, then match ten meanings to Speaking-bank items and drill two minimal pairs.

Controlled build · six sentences about one chart
pairs
Why: Twenty minutes of performance built on nothing produces twenty minutes of fluent inaccuracy. Seven minutes of written accuracy first, with a peer check against explicit criteria, is what makes the next stage worth listening to.

WHAT WE DO: a short accuracy stage between the input and the performance, so the performance is not the first attempt

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Back to the pie chart on page 104. Six sentences about this chart, in pairs, written. One with a fraction, one with a percentage, one with «the majority», one comparing two segments, one with «just over» or «just under», and one that is a COMMENT, not a fact.
  2. Give the model for the sixth, because it is the one they will not produce: What I find surprising is that drought and wildfire together account for less than five per cent — I would have expected far more.
  3. Five minutes. Circulate and fix articles as you go: the 38 per cent → 38 per cent.
  4. Rally Coach: pairs swap sheets with another pair and check six things — no article before a percentage, the number of for countables, increase BY versus increase TO, a fraction spelled with a hyphen, of used correctly, and one genuine comment present.
  5. Take two of the comment sentences aloud. Those are the ones worth marks.
📺 On screen: the model comment sentence written on the board. Backup: dictate it.
👦 Students do: write six sentences to a specification in pairs, then check another pair's against six criteria.

58–76′
🎯 Practice makes perfect · the information gap
pairs, simultaneously
Why: An information gap is the difference between describing a chart and communicating it. Running it as simultaneous pairs means every student speaks for two full minutes; a whole-class version would give two speakers and ten spectators.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.104 exercises 5a and 5b with page 149 — two different charts, one minute each, everybody speaking at once

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pair them and assign roles clearly: Student A uses the chart on page 104 — Number of disasters in the US, 1900–2019 (1900–1919: 157 · 1920–1939: 245 · 1940–1959: 478 · 1960–1979: 1,488 · 1980–1999: 4,777 · 2000–2019: 8,067). Student B turns to page 149 — Number of people affected by disasters in the US, 1900–2019 (1.3mn · 2.8mn · 1.3mn · 6.0mn · 12.3mn · 7.8mn).
  2. Do not show your chart. Your partner has a different one and they cannot see yours. That single rule is what turns a description into communication.
  3. One minute of silent looking first — the Teacher's Book is explicit about this and it matters: absorb the information before you start speaking.
  4. Round 1, Student A speaks for one minute. Student B is the audience: no interrupting, take notes, and prepare two questions to ask at the end. Then Student B asks them.
  5. Round 2, Student B speaks. Same rules, reversed.
  6. Exercise 5b, and this is the real point of the pairing: Now put the two charts side by side. The number of disasters went up more than fifty times. Did the number of people affected go up fifty times? What happened after 1999? The answer — the number of people affected FELL between 1980–1999 and 2000–2019 while the number of events rose — is genuinely surprising, and arguing about why is the best three minutes of the lesson.
  7. Rotate partners and run one more one-minute round each with the new partner. The second telling is always better and it is the one to listen to.
  8. Circulate with a notebook. Collect six errors: three of form, three of missing range. Do not correct anything out loud.
📺 On screen: nothing on screen — both charts are printed in the students' own book, one on page 104 and one on page 149. Backup: if a student's book is missing, pair them as Student B with a photocopy or let them share and take the audience role first.
👦 Students do: look silently for one minute, present a chart for a full minute to a partner who cannot see it, ask two questions, then compare two different charts and explain the discrepancy.

Delayed correction + EGE parallel · task 38 is this lesson
whole class
Why: Delayed correction lands when the class does the fixing, and the exam link here is not a parallel at all — it is the identical skill, which makes it worth the five minutes.

WHAT WE DO: the six collected errors fixed, then the honest exam link for those who need it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Delayed correction, four minutes. Board the six errors you collected, anonymised, three of form and three of missing range. Thirty seconds in pairs on each, then take the corrections.
  2. The three that are always there: the 38% of damage, the amount of disasters, it increased on 50%. Fix all three in writing and leave them up.
  3. EGE parallel, five minutes. Say who it is for: This is for the people taking the exam in May.
  4. Task 38 of the Russian paper gives you a table of figures and asks for two hundred to two hundred and fifty words. It is the hardest task on the paper, and the commonest way to fail it is to retell the table: «Twenty per cent chose X. Thirty per cent chose Y.» That is a list, and it scores almost nothing.
  5. Write the two moves that score, and they are exactly today's language: (1) compare — almost twice as many … as …, whereas only a fifth …; (2) comment — What I find striking is that …, This may be because …
  6. Everything you did in the last twenty minutes was that. The only difference is that in May you write it instead of saying it.

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will be better able to present and interpret statistical data orally, using the language of proportion, trend and degree, in the context of natural disasters in the United States.
  • Subsidiary. Saying fractions and percentages accurately; the stress shift between noun and verb (increase, decrease); asking follow-up questions about a presentation; comparing two sets of data and accounting for a discrepancy.
  • Personal. Do not skip the choral drilling of the fractions. It feels primary and it is the single highest-value ninety seconds in the lesson.
By the end, students can…
  • say fractions and percentages aloud accurately, including two-thirds and four-fifths
  • describe a trend with a verb and a grading adverb (rose sharply, fell gradually)
  • use the majority of, one in three and just over a quarter correctly
  • present a chart for a full minute to somebody who cannot see it
  • compare two data sets and comment on a discrepancy rather than listing figures

Structure
Just over a third of the damage was caused by storms. · The majority of the disasters were floods. · About one in three tornadoes happens in the US. · The number of disasters rose sharply between 1988 and 1993. · It fluctuated a lot and then almost tripled. · What I find surprising is that …
Form
no article before a percentage: 38 per cent of the damage, never the 38 per cent · fractions take of before a determiner (three-quarters of the disasters) but one in ten takes none · half works with or without of, never with a · the number of + countable plural, the amount of + uncountable · increase BY the difference, increase TO the new total · noun and verb differ in stress: an ˈincrease / to inˈcrease.
Pron. watch
numbers are where fluent students suddenly stop being fluent. Drill two-thirds /tuːˈθɜːdz/ (the θ is the whole problem), a quarter /ˈkwɔːtə/, four-fifths /fɔːˈfɪfθs/ — the hardest consonant cluster in English. Then the stress pair that changes with word class: an ˈincrease (noun) vs to inˈcrease (verb), same with decrease
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
just over a third: Is 38% more than a third or less? — More. Is it a lot more? — No, just a little. And 20% — is that just over a third? — No, it's less than a quarter.
the majority: Is it more than half? — Yes. Is it all of them? — No.
fluctuate: Does it go up? — Yes. Does it go down? — Also yes. Once or many times? — Many times.
ICQ before the information gap: Do you show your chart? How long do you speak? What does your partner do while you speak — interrupt, or take notes and ask two questions at the end?

Assumptions: Students can read a pie chart and a line graph and know the basic vocabulary of increase and decrease from B1. Fractions said aloud, grading adverbs and the article rules around percentages are new or unstable, and the habit of listing rather than comparing is deeply established.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The presentation becomes a list of six numbers read off the page.Two safeguards: the partner cannot see the chart, and the controlled build stage required one comment sentence before anybody performed. If a pair still lists, stand next to them and ask the audience member What did they find surprising? — the speaker has to supply it.
«Two-thirds» and «four-fifths» are avoided for the rest of the year.That is what the choral drill and the line-up are for. Every student says at least two fractions aloud in front of others before the performance stage, so avoidance is not available.
Student B cannot find the chart on page 149.Say the page number twice and have them find it and hold the book up before you start the timer. Thirty seconds spent here saves two minutes of confusion in the middle of a timed task.
The information gap finishes early because both partners rush.One minute is a long time to speak and they will stop at forty seconds. Time it visibly and do not let anybody stop early — you still have twenty seconds, keep going is where the useful language comes out. Then the compulsory two questions fill the rest.
Nobody produces a comment, only facts.Model one yourself in the controlled build stage and write it on the board, and make the sixth sentence of the specification compulsory. A comment is a genre move, not a language item — it has to be shown, not explained.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

The same skill is tested by: Task 38 (the project essay with a table or a chart) and tasks 39–40 (the oral).
