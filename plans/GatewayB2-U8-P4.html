🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Both productive skills of the unit in one lesson: the speaking task rehearsed in pairs until it holds, and the written genre taken apart down to a plan of paragraphs — the clean copy written at home.
What is genuinely hard
Numbers are where fluent students suddenly stop being fluent. Three specific difficulties: saying fractions aloud (two-thirds and four-fifths are the two hardest consonant clusters in the language), the article (the 40% of people is the default Russian rendering and it is wrong), and the stress shift between an ˈincrease and to inˈcrease. And the task itself is not description — it is comparison and comment, which students avoid because listing is easier.
Typical error to expect
«However the weather is bad, we will go» — however introduces a NEW sentence and is followed by a comma; the joining word is although or even though. Write both correct versions side by side and leave them on the board all lesson.
«Despite of the rain», «despite it was raining» — despite takes a noun or an -ing form, or despite the fact that + clause. Never despite of.
«In my opinion, I think that …» — one opinion marker per sentence, not two.
Why the lesson is built this way
Speaking first, while the room is awake and the model is fresh; writing second, because a plan survives the walk home and a first draft does not. Nothing is cut from either half except the part that works better alone: the twenty minutes of silent writing, which happen at home where there is time to think.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two things today, and both of them are what the exam actually asks for: speak with a reason, and write a text that answers every point. We build both here — you write the clean copy where nobody is rushing you.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting at the door, then reporting passives about the climate

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — the last time the weather changed your plans. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — rewind, the reporting passive, aimed at data. On the board: It is believed that … · It has been reported that … · … is known to have …
  3. RallyRobin: one sentence each about the climate or the weather using one of the three frames. Two minutes, then three aloud.
  4. You will get It is believed that the climate is getting warmer. Take it and push once: Believed by whom? On what evidence? — and there is the bridge to a lesson about figures.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): «It is believed» is what you say when you have no numbers. Today you have numbers. Page 104.
📺 On screen: the three frames written on the board. Backup: dictate them.
👦 Students do: one reporting-passive sentence each about the climate, then three read aloud.

Reading the chart · six statements, true or false
desks → pairs
Why: Judging a statement forces the student to parse the language of proportion precisely, which producing their own sentence lets them avoid. It is also the fastest diagnostic of who can and cannot read «just over a third».

WHAT WE DO: SB p.104 exercise 1 — judging somebody else's sentences about data, which is harder than writing your own

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 1, six True/False statements about the pie chart «Economic damage by disaster type, 1995–2016». Individually first, five minutes, then check with a partner.
  2. The chart: storms 38% ($875bn) · floods 24% ($550bn) · earthquakes and volcanoes 20% ($643bn) · extreme temperature 5% ($106bn) · drought 2% ($49bn) · wildfire 2% ($47bn).
  3. Answers, and take each one with the reasoning, not just the letter: the pie chart, economic damage by disaster type 1995–2016: 1 True (storms 38%) · 2 False (earthquakes and volcanoes 20%, which is not «just over a third») · 3 False (floods 24% is MORE than earthquakes and volcanoes 20%) · 4 True (droughts 5% — less than a tenth) · 5 False (floods 24% — just under a quarter, not just over) · 6 True (storms 38% + floods 24% = 62%)
  4. Statements 2 and 5 are the ones that separate the class, and both hinge on one word. Put the two expressions on the board side by side and drill the difference: just OVER a third (more than 33%) versus just UNDER a quarter (a little less than 25%). Ask: Is 24% just over a quarter or just under? Is 20% just over a third?
  5. Fast finishers (Teacher's Book): write two more true/false sentences about the same chart and read them to the class at the end of the stage for the others to judge.
  6. Compare the chart with the board ranking from the lead-in and let the room react. Then the follow-up question: Which of these are a problem in our country? Does any of it surprise you?
📺 On screen: the two expressions (just over / just under) written on the board. Backup: the chart is in the students' own book — nothing to project.
👦 Students do: judge six statements against a pie chart, justify each, then write two of their own if they finish early.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.104 exercises 3 and 4 — the movement verbs and the adverbs that grade them

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 3: the line graph «Natural disasters in the US, 1980–2016» with a short text underneath in which the trend language is in bold. Read the text aloud yourself, a sentence at a time, and after each one elicit a translation or a paraphrase — that is the Teacher's Book's method and it is faster than explaining.
  2. Explain only what does not survive elicitation, which is normally one word: fluctuate = change frequently, up and down.
  3. Exercise 4, pairs, five minutes: find the word in the Speaking bank that means each of ten things (go up · go from 20,000 to 40,000 · go from 40,000 to 30,000 · one in three · slowly and in small amounts · important · in a sudden and surprising way · most of the people in a group · 50% · three times more).
  4. Answers: 1 rise / increase · 2 double · 3 fall / decrease · 4 a third · 5 gradually / steadily · 6 significant · 7 sharply / dramatically · 8 the majority · 9 half · 10 triple.
  5. Then the two pronunciation points the Teacher's Book flags, drilled and written up: half /hɑːf/ versus halve /hɑːv/ — noun and verb; and the stress shift an ˈincrease versus to inˈcrease, same for decrease. Drill both pairs in sentences, never in isolation.
  6. Close the stage by making them look at the Speaking bank as a whole and telling them what it is: Numbers and proportions on top, trends underneath. Everything you need for the next twenty minutes is on that box. Do not memorise it — use it.
📺 On screen: the two pronunciation pairs written on the board. Backup: the graph and the Speaking bank are in the students' own book; no display needed.
👦 Students do: paraphrase a text about a graph sentence by sentence, then match ten meanings to Speaking-bank items and drill two minimal pairs.

30–45′
🎯 Practice makes perfect · the information gap
pairs, simultaneously
Why: An information gap is the difference between describing a chart and communicating it. Running it as simultaneous pairs means every student speaks for two full minutes; a whole-class version would give two speakers and ten spectators.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.104 exercises 5a and 5b with page 149 — two different charts, one minute each, everybody speaking at once

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pair them and assign roles clearly: Student A uses the chart on page 104 — Number of disasters in the US, 1900–2019 (1900–1919: 157 · 1920–1939: 245 · 1940–1959: 478 · 1960–1979: 1,488 · 1980–1999: 4,777 · 2000–2019: 8,067). Student B turns to page 149 — Number of people affected by disasters in the US, 1900–2019 (1.3mn · 2.8mn · 1.3mn · 6.0mn · 12.3mn · 7.8mn).
  2. Do not show your chart. Your partner has a different one and they cannot see yours. That single rule is what turns a description into communication.
  3. One minute of silent looking first — the Teacher's Book is explicit about this and it matters: absorb the information before you start speaking.
  4. Round 1, Student A speaks for one minute. Student B is the audience: no interrupting, take notes, and prepare two questions to ask at the end. Then Student B asks them.
  5. Round 2, Student B speaks. Same rules, reversed.
  6. Exercise 5b, and this is the real point of the pairing: Now put the two charts side by side. The number of disasters went up more than fifty times. Did the number of people affected go up fifty times? What happened after 1999? The answer — the number of people affected FELL between 1980–1999 and 2000–2019 while the number of events rose — is genuinely surprising, and arguing about why is the best three minutes of the lesson.
  7. Rotate partners and run one more one-minute round each with the new partner. The second telling is always better and it is the one to listen to.
  8. Circulate with a notebook. Collect six errors: three of form, three of missing range. Do not correct anything out loud.
📺 On screen: nothing on screen — both charts are printed in the students' own book, one on page 104 and one on page 149. Backup: if a student's book is missing, pair them as Student B with a photocopy or let them share and take the audience role first.
👦 Students do: look silently for one minute, present a chart for a full minute to a partner who cannot see it, ask two questions, then compare two different charts and explain the discrepancy.

The model · what each paragraph is FOR
desks → pairs
Why: Genre knowledge is what B2 writing is actually short of. Naming the repeated internal shape of the three middle paragraphs converts a 190-word task into three small identical tasks.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.105 exercises 2 and 3 — structure before language

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 2: read the model essay, three minutes. One question only — Which of YOUR points from exercise 1 does the writer include? Hands up for each of the three.
  2. Ask the honest question straight afterwards: Do you agree with this writer? Why? Take two answers. An essay read as an argument is read better than an essay read as a specimen.
  3. Exercise 3, the structural analysis, and this is where the lesson turns. Pairs, four minutes: What is the PURPOSE of each of the five paragraphs?
  4. Answers: the five paragraphs of the model: 1 introduce the topic · 2 first reason with supporting arguments · 3 second reason with supporting arguments · 4 third reason with supporting arguments · 5 your opinion and conclusion.
  5. Now make them count, because it is the difference between a good essay and an average one: Look at paragraph two. How many sentences? What does the first one do? What do the other two do? The pattern — point, reason, example — is identical in paragraphs 2, 3 and 4. Write it on the board in three words and leave it up.
  6. Then the sentence that saves ten essays: Your three middle paragraphs are all the same shape. Once you have the shape, you are writing three of the same thing, not three different things.
📺 On screen: the paragraph purposes and the three-word pattern (point → reason → example) written on the board and left up. Backup: the model is in their own book.
👦 Students do: read the model against their own ideas, then identify the purpose and internal shape of five paragraphs in pairs.

Writing bank · the grammar of a linker
desks → pairs
Why: This is the actual difficulty of the genre and it is grammatical, so it gets a full block and a permanent board record rather than a footnote. The three-way rewrite at the end is the transfer test.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.105 exercises 5 and 6 — the difficulty of the lesson, taught head-on

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 5: look at how although, even though and however are used in the model, then read the Writing bank.
  2. Put the two shapes on the board and leave them up for the rest of the lesson, written out in full: Even though the country is a long way away, we must help it. / We must help the country even though it is a long way away. versus The country is a long way away. However, we must help it.
  3. Say the two rules in one line each: A joining linker connects two halves of ONE sentence and there is no full stop before it. An introducing linker starts a NEW sentence and takes a comma after it.
  4. The comma rule, from the Teacher's Book, because it is markable: Although my dad has a bike, he almost always uses his car (comma, because the clause comes first) versus My dad almost always uses his car although he has a bike (no comma).
  5. Exercise 6: the twenty-two linkers in the box sorted into J (join) and I (introduce). Pairs, five minutes. Answers: J (join two halves of one sentence): and so, despite (the fact that), even though, since, so that, whereas, while. I (introduce a new sentence): all things considered, as a result, as far as I'm concerned, consequently, firstly/secondly/lastly, furthermore, in addition, in conclusion, meanwhile, moreover, nevertheless, on the one/other hand, therefore, to sum up, what's more.
  6. Then the Teacher's Book's second sort, which is worth two minutes because it is how they will actually search for a word while writing: five function headings on the board — CONTRASTING · CONCLUDING · RESULTS · ORDER · ADDING — and the class assigns the linkers to them. Not all are used.
  7. Extra activity, ninety seconds (Teacher's Book): on the board, The planet is getting hotter. Some people deny that this is happening. Pairs write three different combinations — with although at the front, with although in the middle, and with however. Take all three and write them up.
📺 On screen: the two structural shapes and the comma rule written on the board and left up for the writing stage. Backup: dictate them; the stage is board-and-book only.
👦 Students do: sort twenty-two linkers into two grammatical classes and five functions, then join the same two ideas three different ways.

Joint planning · the real task, on the board
whole class → individual
Why: A shared skeleton removes the blank-page problem without removing the thinking, and forbidding full sentences at the planning stage is what stops the essay being written twice.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.105 exercise 7a — the plan built together so that nobody starts from a blank page

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the actual essay title aloud, twice: «The world is a better place to live in today than it was a hundred years ago.» Do you agree? Notes: 1 health · 2 technology · 3 your own idea.
  2. Sixty seconds of hands: Who agrees? Who disagrees? Who is somewhere in the middle? All three positions are writable and say so — You are not marked on your opinion. You are marked on whether you support it.
  3. Build the skeleton on the board together, three minutes, taking ideas from the room: P1 introduce the topic and state your position · P2 health (point, reason, example) · P3 technology (point, reason, example) · P4 your own idea (point, reason, example) · P5 restate and conclude.
  4. Third ideas that work, if the room is stuck: education, travel, rights and equality, food, or — the interesting one — the counter-argument that the environment is worse.
  5. Then three minutes of silent, individual note-making on the actual plan. Notes only — say it explicitly: No full sentences yet. If you write sentences now, you will write the essay twice.
  6. Last instruction before the pens move, from the Teacher's Book, written on the board: Five paragraphs. Different kinds of linker. And your conclusion must follow from what you actually wrote.
📺 On screen: the five-paragraph skeleton built on the board and left up throughout the writing stage. Backup: no display dependency — the board is the tool.
👦 Students do: take a position, contribute to a shared skeleton, then make their own notes in silence.

WHAT WE DO: the writing task set properly, so the clean copy is worth marking

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the task aloud from the book and have one student say it back in their own words. If they cannot, it will not be written correctly either.
  2. Point at the plan they have just built: This is what you write from. Not from memory of the model — from your own plan.
  3. Board the four criteria they will be marked against: every point answered · paragraphs · the register of the genre · linkers that actually link. Same four as the unit test.
  4. Word count and deadline said once and written on the board. Bring it on paper next lesson; peer check happens in class.
📺 On screen: the task and the four criteria, left on the board to be copied. Backup: none.
👦 Students do: copy the task, the criteria and the word count

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can produce the speaking task of the unit with reasons and examples and have a full paragraph plan for the written genre, ready to be written up at home.
  • Subsidiary. Using the Speaking bank without reading from it; taking a model text apart; choosing linkers; peer feedback against criteria.

  • describe a trend with a verb and a grading adverb (rose sharply, fell gradually)

Structure
Just over a third of the damage was caused by storms. · The majority of the disasters were floods. · About one in three tornadoes happens in the US. · The number of disasters rose sharply between 1988 and 1993. · It fluctuated a lot and then almost tripled. · What I find surprising is that …
Form
no article before a percentage: 38 per cent of the damage, never the 38 per cent · fractions take of before a determiner (three-quarters of the disasters) but one in ten takes none · half works with or without of, never with a · the number of + countable plural, the amount of + uncountable · increase BY the difference, increase TO the new total · noun and verb differ in stress: an ˈincrease / to inˈcrease.
Pron. watch
numbers are where fluent students suddenly stop being fluent. Drill two-thirds /tuːˈθɜːdz/ (the θ is the whole problem), a quarter /ˈkwɔːtə/, four-fifths /fɔːˈfɪfθs/ — the hardest consonant cluster in English. Then the stress pair that changes with word class: an ˈincrease (noun) vs to inˈcrease (verb), same with decrease
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
just over a third: Is 38% more than a third or less? — More. Is it a lot more? — No, just a little. And 20% — is that just over a third? — No, it's less than a quarter.
the majority: Is it more than half? — Yes. Is it all of them? — No.
although vs however: «Although it is far, we must help» — how many sentences? — One. And «It is far. However, we must help» — how many? — Two. Can I say «However it is far, we must help»? — No.
despite: What comes after «despite» — a full sentence or a noun? — A noun or an -ing. Can I say «despite of»? — Never.

Assumptions: Students can read a pie chart and a line graph and know the basic vocabulary of increase and decrease from B1. Fractions said aloud, grading adverbs and the article rules around percentages are new or unstable, and the habit of listing rather than comparing is deeply established.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The presentation becomes a list of six numbers read off the page.Two safeguards: the partner cannot see the chart, and the controlled build stage required one comment sentence before anybody performed. If a pair still lists, stand next to them and ask the audience member What did they find surprising? — the speaker has to supply it.
«Two-thirds» and «four-fifths» are avoided for the rest of the year.That is what the choral drill and the line-up are for. Every student says at least two fractions aloud in front of others before the performance stage, so avoidance is not available.
Somebody writes about the model's topic instead of the real one.It happens every year, because both are on the same page. Say the title twice, write it on the board, and have one student read it back to the class before the planning stage.
The essay is not finished in twenty-two minutes.Most will finish; some will not. Anyone still writing at the bell hands in what they have and finishes at home in a different colour, so you can see which part was written where. Do not extend the stage — the peer check is where the criteria are learned.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

The same skill is tested by: Task 38 (the opinion essay) and task 37 (the personal email).

The essay written today is 140–190 words with a five-paragraph plan, three points and a conclusion. The Russian task 38 is 200–250 words with a fixed six-point plan and a data table, and task 37 is 100–140 words with three questions answered and three asked. Different lengths, one competence: paragraphing on purpose and signposting with linkers. The linker work of this lesson is worth marks under К2 in both Russian tasks, where the commonest failure is an essay written as one block.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
