🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use eight phrasal verbs (believe in, depend on, get into, go for, join in, keep on, miss out, work out) as fixed chunks, and discuss options with a partner until you agree, using the language of agreeing and disagreeing.
What is genuinely hard
A phrasal verb cannot be reasoned out from its parts, and the preposition is not negotiable: believe IN, depend ON, join IN, miss out ON. Russian speakers reach for the preposition that matches the Russian verb and produce «believe to», «depend of». The speaking difficulty is different and it is about behaviour: reaching an agreement means changing your mind out loud, which teenagers find harder than any grammar in this book.
Typical error to expect
«believe to», «depend of», «join to» — a phrasal verb is one word with a preposition welded on, and the preposition cannot be reasoned out. Believe IN, depend ON, join IN, miss out ON. They are learned as chunks or they are lost.
«I missed out the party» — miss out takes ON before the thing you missed. The Student's Book text has it right and the class will read straight past it.
Why the lesson is built this way
The phrasal verbs are taught in the only way that works — in a text, as chunks, with the meaning deduced and then immediately reused in a second text. The listening is the gentlest exam-style task of the block and it is placed in the middle deliberately, because it is also the most encouraging thing on the page: five teenagers describing something they achieved in a year. The speaking half is the final one of the course and it is built to be enjoyable rather than assessed: a model discussion analysed for phrases, then Speaking Part 3 on summer activities, then Part 4 on the summer itself, which is the conversation the class actually wants to have in May.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Five people your age are about to tell you what they achieved in one year. One ran a half marathon, one got a recording contract, one kept a blog going every single week. Today: the eight phrasal verbs they use to say it — and then the last discussion of this course, which is about your summer.”

Vocabulary · eight phrasal verbs

Why: Vocabulary · eight phrasal verbs

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: found in a text, matched to meanings, then reused in a second text

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 as a class, sixty seconds: underline the phrasal verbs in the two sentences from the article they read on Tuesday. Answers: join in, depends on. Ask HOW they found them, and take the honest answer: by spotting the preposition first.
  2. ex.2 in mixed-ability pairs, five minutes: eight sentences matched to eight meanings. Do item 1 together. Answers: 1 e (go for it = try to get or achieve), 2 h (keep on = continue), 3 d (get into = become a member of a team), 4 f (join in = take part with others), 5 c (depend on = be affected by), 6 a (believe in = trust that somebody can do something), 7 g (work out = understand after thinking carefully), 8 b (miss out on = not have something you would enjoy).
  3. Every confirmed verb goes on the wall in two colours, written by the student who answered.
  4. Say the honest thing about work out — it has three meanings in English (exercise, calculate, understand) and only one of them is here. Naming the ambiguity prevents it becoming a problem.
  5. ex.3 individually, five minutes: Bradley's message to Frankie, eight gaps. Ask the gist question first — what has Bradley been doing that leaves him no free time? Answer: training for the Olympic Games. Then the gaps: 1 missed out, 2 got into, 3 believed in, 4 keep on, 5 go for, 6 depends on, 7 join in, 8 work out.
  6. Check by reading whole sentences aloud, and each time ask for the preposition separately: Missed out — and then?
📺 On screen: the two-colour wall filling up, written by students — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: find phrasal verbs by their prepositions, match eight meanings in pairs, then fill eight gaps in a real message

Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer on the half that gets lost

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Four corners: IN · ON · INTO · OUT.
  2. You call a verb — believe, depend, get, go, join, keep, miss, work. Everybody walks to its preposition. Two of them are deliberately awkward (go FOR and work OUT), so allow the argument and settle it at the wall.
  3. The first person to arrive says a whole true sentence with the chunk in it.
  4. Eight verbs, eight walks. Then freeze and sit: Page one hundred and fifteen. Five people your age, one year, one achievement each.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–5: the paired task on SB p.140, then the summer questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 🎯 ex.4 = Speaking Part 3, SB p.140. Set the situation: a girl is going to do a summer activity course and here are the different things she could do. Talk about the benefits of each and then decide which would be best.
  2. Rule, called out loud at ninety seconds as in lesson 61: no decision until every option has had a sentence.
  3. Pairs, four minutes, with the three speaking boxes visible. Then NEW pairs and run it again, four minutes — the second run is always twice as good and it costs nothing.
  4. Class vote, two minutes: the most and least popular summer activity, with one reason each. It is a real poll and it should produce an argument.
  5. ex.5 = Speaking Part 4, ten minutes, Kagan Timed Pair Share then open pairs: what do you usually do during the summer holidays · is it important to plan what you are going to do · do you prefer to spend time with friends or family · do you prefer being at home or outdoors · would you like longer summer holidays.
  6. Every answer takes two parts, as in lesson 61: the answer, then a reason or an example. The partner gives a back-up prompt if only one part arrives.
  7. Four minutes of delayed correction from your notes: six sentences on the board, names removed, repaired by the class. Choose two with a phrasal-verb preposition error so the lesson closes where it opened.
  8. Close the course content out loud, because it deserves marking: That was the last new page of this book. Everything from here is putting it together.

Form
verb + fixed preposition, learned as one chunk · miss out ON something · get INTO a team · keep ON doing · work something OUT.
Pron. watch
achieve /əˈtʃiːv/ — two syllables, stress on the second, and the class will say a three-syllable version · imagine /ɪˈmædʒɪn/ keeps its stress in the middle · in a phrasal verb the PREPOSITION carries the stress and that is how a listener hears it: I missed OUT, we worked it OUT · and in verbs with two objects the sentence stress lands on the last word, which is why «she gave me a BOOK» and «she gave the book to ME» mean slightly different things
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I missed out the party» — right? → no. Miss out ON the party. The preposition is not optional.
«Work out» in «work out how to do everything» — is anyone exercising? → no. It means understand after thinking carefully.
In the discussion you have two minutes and you must AGREE. Can you just say «I agree» at the start? → no. Talk about all the options first; the agreement is the end, not the beginning.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The prepositions get swappedColour is the intervention. The preposition goes on the wall in a different colour from the verb, and every correction is a point at the colour rather than a sentence.
«Work out» has three meanings and they all appearSay so openly and give all three — exercise, calculate, understand. Ambiguity that is named stops being a problem; ambiguity that is hidden becomes one.
The pair agrees in ten secondsSame rule as lesson 61 and they know it: no agreement in the first ninety seconds, and every option gets a sentence before any of them gets a decision. Call the ninety seconds out loud.
Fast finishers in the vocabulary stageSB p.140: the nine personal questions with the phrasal verbs — what you join in with at school, what you have missed out on recently, how you work out the meaning of a new word. They come back to them in the paired stage.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
