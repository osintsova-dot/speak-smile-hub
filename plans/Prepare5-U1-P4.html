🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Build negative words with the prefixes un- and dis- (unkind · unpleasant · unlucky · unhappy · unreliable · uninteresting · unfriendly · disagree · disappear · dislike) and know which prefix a word takes; and use the answer-extension move — a short answer becomes a full one with a reason and a detail — across the two exam formats introduced today, Listening Part 3 (note completion) and Speaking Part 1 (talking about yourself).
What is genuinely hard
The prefix is not free choice: it is fixed per word, and Russian's single не- gives no clue which one English wants (disagree but unhappy, dislike but unkind). And the exam habit is the opposite of the school habit: in Part 1 a truthful «yes» loses marks, while in Listening Part 3 the strongest students write their guesses into the gaps and then can't hear the real answer.
Typical error to expect
«disreliable», «unagree», «Yes.» (full stop) — the wrong prefix on a word, and the one-word exam answer. The prefix error is lexical, so it is fixed by a tendency (dis- leans to verbs and opinions; un- leans to qualities) plus a checked list, not by a single rule. The one-word answer is fixed by making the reason compulsory.
Why the lesson is built this way
The prefixes arrive as a puzzle — two example pairs (interesting/uninteresting, agree/disagree) and one question: which prefix goes on verbs? — so the rule is noticed, not delivered. The two exam skills are taught back to back on purpose: both are secretly the same skill, adding information. Listening Part 3 rewards a candidate who predicts the type of missing word before the audio; Speaking Part 1 rewards a candidate who never stops at «yes». The lesson ends with a mingle in which a one-word answer is simply not allowed to count.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two students walk into the same speaking exam. Both are asked: do you like studying English? The first says «No.» The second says «No, I don't like it because I think it's hard — I prefer Maths.» One of them passes Part 1. Today: how the second answer is built — and the prefixes un- and dis- that let you say exactly what you don't like.”

Vocabulary · un- and dis-
SB p.16 ex.1–3
class → pairs
Why: The rule is a puzzle the students solve, not a table they copy. Then it is stress-tested on seven words and inside a real text, so the tendency is felt before it is trusted.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: discover the two prefixes, build seven negatives, then the text

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: read the two example pairs together — interesting → uninteresting, agree → disagree. Ask the two questions on the page: How do un- and dis- change the meaning? Which prefix do we often add to verbs? Draw out the tendency: un- leans to qualities and states, dis- often lands on verbs and opinions (agree, like, appear). Write the tendency on the wall, but warn: This is a lean, not a law — we check each word.
  2. ex.2 individually, then RallyCoach pairs — one writes the negative, the other coaches by naming the prefix out loud first: un- or dis-? Say it, then write it. Seven items.
  3. Check by nomination and drill the stress, because the test rewards it: un- is unstressed (unKIND, unRELIABLE); dis- carries the stress (DISagree, DISappear). Watch the spelling of disappear — dis + appear, one s, two p's.
  4. ex.3 (EP): complete the text with the box words, adding a prefix only if needed. Do gap 1 together (uninteresting), then pairs. The trap gaps are 5 and 6, where the positive word is correct — lucky and agree stay as they are.
📺 On screen: SB p.16 on the visualiser if you have one, otherwise the open book. Backup: none needed — this is a pen-and-book stage
👦 Students do: solve the rule, build seven negatives while coaching a partner, then repair a text. Everyone writes the ten target words into the vocabulary list at the back of the notebook

Move break · Flip it
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also spends the new prefixes at speed. Teenagers who have been sitting for a quarter of an hour need to stand, and the negation habit is drilled while they move.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that turns positive words negative at speed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. ▶ Flip it: you (or the screen) say a positive word — kind, happy, agree, lucky, reliable, like, pleasant, appear — the whole class shouts the negative form with the correct prefix and claps once on the stressed syllable.
  2. Then Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with the prefix cards: each student has one card (positive on the front, negative on the back). Meet a partner, quiz them, swap cards, move on. Three minutes, fast.
  3. Freeze. Two lightning rounds from you at the front — a wrong prefix means sit for one turn, then back up. Keep it warm, not cruel.
📺 On screen: the positive words one at a time if you drive it from the board. Backup: read them from a list — the game does not need the screen
👦 Students do: everyone negates aloud and quizzes at least four partners. Nobody watches; every card is in a hand

Why: The whole task turns on one habit taught before the audio: predict the TYPE of missing word, never write a guess in the gap. That is the single skill Part 3 — and the Russian exam's own gap-fill — rewards.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 (track 06): predict the word type, then listen twice for the six gaps

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 as a 60-second warmer, pairs: Have you ever written a quiz? Who was it for? What did you ask?
  2. ex.2 is the strategy, and it is done BEFORE any audio. Read the six-line note «Notes for writing a quiz». For each gap ask one question only: What KIND of word is missing here — a name? a topic? a number? a date? an adjective? Write the type above each gap. Gap 4 («…or shy, for example») is clearly a personality adjective — flag it.
  3. Say the exam rule out loud and write it on the board: Consider the answer, but do NOT write it in the gap before you listen. If you fill it with a guess, you stop hearing the real word.
  4. ex.3, track 06: play once, they write. Play again, they check and complete. The answer is one or two words, or a number, or a date, or a time.
  5. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: pairs agree each answer, you call a number, that student answers for the pair and says where in the audio they heard it.
📺 On screen: track 06 (the teacher describing the quiz project). Backup: if the audio fails, read the audioscript (TB p.285) aloud yourself at a steady pace — the task is unchanged; read it twice
👦 Students do: predict the type of every gap, then listen twice and complete. Everyone commits a prediction in writing before the audio, so everyone has a stake in the answer

Brain break · one word is not enough
standing, whole class
Why: The mid-lesson stir doubles as the set-up for the whole speaking half. Everyone feels, in their own body, why a one-word answer fails before they are asked to fix it.

WHAT WE DO: the stand-up yes/no demo that opens the speaking block

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. Fire quick yes/no questions at the room: Do you like Mondays? Do you play sports? Do you study English? They may answer with ONE word only — Yes. No.
  2. After thirty seconds stop: Boring, isn't it? Now — same questions, but you may not sit down until you add «because…». Fire the same questions; a full answer earns the seat.
  3. Last one standing gives the fullest answer they can to the whole class. Sit.
📺 On screen: nothing — this is a voice-and-body break, no gadgets
👦 Students do: everyone answers twice — once in one word, once with a reason — and feels the difference

Why: Model before produce. The students hear two real candidates and extract the phrases themselves, so the «Prepare to speak» box becomes a tool they noticed, not a list they were handed.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 (track 07): Ahmed and Sandrine, and the phrases that turn a short answer into a full one

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1, track 07: listen to Ahmed and Sandrine. Tick the eleven questions they actually answer, and settle the key question: Do they just say yes or no, or do they add more? They add — every time.
  2. Model the shape on the board with the class — I Do → We Do → You Do. Take Ahmed's real answer and label its three moves: «No, I don't like it because I think it's hard — I prefer Maths.» → POINT (no) → REASON (because it's hard) → DETAIL (I prefer Maths).
  3. ex.2, track 07 again: read the «Prepare to speak» box, then listen for which phrases they use. Tick them on the board — I like it because… · I don't like it because… · I prefer… · I usually… · I sometimes… · I think it's…
  4. Turn the box into tiered frames so nobody is stuck and nobody is capped: heavy support — Yes, I do. I like … because …; lighter — I usually …, but I sometimes …; lightest — a bare prompt, «reason + detail», no frame. Point students at the tier they need, and tell the strong ones the frames are a floor, not a ceiling.
📺 On screen: track 07 for both exercises, and the POINT–REASON–DETAIL labels on the board. Backup: read the interview from the audioscript (TB p.285) in two voices with a strong student
👦 Students do: listen for the phrases, label the three moves, then say one framed answer to a neighbour before the free stage

🎯 Speaking out · Part 1 in pairs
SB p.17 ex.3
pairs, then rotate
Why: The productive stage where the whole lesson is spent: every student answers six exam questions, and no answer counts unless it carries a reason and a detail. The prefixes get to work too — half the honest answers are negative.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: the six exam questions, each answered with a reason and a detail

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3: the six questions on the page (surname, where you're from, your house, sports, bedtime, reading). Kagan Timed Pair Share — A is the examiner and asks all six, B answers in full; 40 seconds per answer is plenty. Then swap roles.
  2. Circulate with a slip and log — do NOT correct on the spot. You are collecting: one-word answers, wrong prefixes (disreliable), missing reasons.
  3. Rotate partners once (RallyRobin down the row) so every student runs the six questions twice, with two different examiners. The second run is visibly fuller than the first — say so.
  4. Push the negatives out on purpose: At least one of your six answers today must use a dis- or un- word — «I dislike…», «My town is quite unfriendly to cyclists…».
  5. Take two strong pairs to the front for thirty seconds each, as models, not as a test.
📺 On screen: the six questions on the board for the ones who read slowly, and the POINT–REASON–DETAIL strip still up. Backup: none needed — this is a talk stage
👦 Students do: answer twelve exam turns each (six questions × two rounds), always with a reason. Everyone speaks almost continuously for eighteen minutes

80–84′
Error clinic
seated, whole class
Why: Delayed correction, public but anonymous. The class repairs its own errors, which fixes them far better than the teacher's red pen would.

WHAT WE DO: four answers heard in the room, repaired by the class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write four answers you heard, without names — two broken (a wrong prefix, a one-word answer) and two good.
  2. Pairs, sixty seconds: which two are wrong? Repair them — the prefix and the missing reason.
  3. Rub out the wrong versions; leave the two good answers on the board for the wall.
  4. Name the two patterns once: Every prefix mistake today was one word wearing the wrong prefix — we check the word, not a rule. And a «yes» with nothing after it is not an answer in this exam.
📺 On screen: the four answers in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: spot and repair errors that were theirs, in public but anonymously

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«They disagree about everything.» Do they have the same opinion or a different one? → a different one. And which prefix — un- or dis-? → dis-, it's a verb about an opinion.
«I'm very unlucky today.» Good luck or bad luck? → bad. Un- makes the quality negative.
Listening Part 3, gap 4: «…or shy, for example.» What KIND of word is missing? → a personality adjective. Do we write our guess in the gap before we listen? → no.
«Do you like studying English?» Is «Yes» a good exam answer? → no. What must come after it? → a reason, and then a detail.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Wrong prefix — «disreliable», «unagree»Say it plainly: the prefix is fixed per word, not free choice. Teach the tendency (dis- leans to verbs and opinions, un- to qualities) but make the checked list on the notebook sheet the authority. Test two words in the wrong order later in the lesson.
Students write their guess in the Listening gap before the audioMake it a rule with a reason: a guessed word in the gap makes your ears stop working. In ex.2 they may only write the TYPE of word above the gap (a name, a number, an adjective), never the word itself.
One-word yes/no answers survive into the speaking exam taskThe ban is already up. In the mingle, a bare «yes» is handed straight back once: Say that again — «yes, because…». Make one negative (un-/dis-) word compulsory across the six answers.
Spelling — «dissapear», «uninteresting» doubled wrongBuild the words on the board from the two parts with a visible plus sign: dis + appear, un + interesting. The join is where the letters live or die.
The paraphrase in the listening is missed (audio «facts» → note «people's interests»)Replay that one line and ask the class to say both versions aloud. Naming the paraphrase is the skill; do it once slowly and it transfers.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skills are tested by: word-formation (tasks 26–31) — where a negative prefix on a given root is one of the most repeated operations — plus the listening section (tasks 1–9) and the speaking part.

Prefixes un-/dis- are raw word-formation currency: the task literally hands you a root and asks for the right derived form, and the negative prefix is a standard move. The Listening Part 3 habit — predict the type of the missing word, spot the paraphrase, do not pre-fill the gap — is exactly what the Russian gap and matching tasks reward. And Speaking Part 1's answer-extension (point → reason → detail) is the backbone of the oral part, where a one-word answer scores nothing.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
