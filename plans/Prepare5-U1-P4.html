🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Build opposites with the prefixes un- and dis- (unkind, unlucky, unpleasant, unreliable, disagree, dislike, disappear), complete a set of exam notes with one or two words heard (B1 Listening Part 3), and answer personal questions the way the Speaking exam expects — never in one word.
What is genuinely hard
Choosing between the two prefixes is not free: un- lives on adjectives, dis- prefers verbs, and there is no rule Russian can lend. In the listening the difficulty is not understanding — it is the discipline of writing down exactly what was said, without improving it.
Typical error to expect
«disfriendly», «unagree» — un- sits on adjectives, dis- prefers verbs (disagree, dislike, disappear). And the stress moves: unKIND but DISagree — the ear helps here.
Rewriting what you heard — «understanding of others» instead of «Understanding others». In note completion the words are dictated: change them and the mark disappears.
Why the lesson is built this way
Vocabulary comes first and is deliberately short, because it feeds the two skills stages: the listening notes contain sociable, and the speaking answers are full of like / dislike. The listening is taught the exam way in three moves — predict the KIND of word, listen, then check the spelling against the recording, with the rule said aloud: do not change the words you hear. The speaking half is model-first: two real candidates are heard answering, the class harvests their phrases, and only then do the students answer the same questions in a rotation that makes everyone speak for six minutes.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“In the speaking exam, an answer of one word costs you marks. Today you hear two teenagers doing it properly — and then you do it. First, though: the two little prefixes that turn friendly into its enemy.”

door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, repair of the class's own -ing errors, and the promised spelling check

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what's the best thing about today so far? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — delayed feedback from lesson 3. Four sentences on the board, two of them broken in exactly the way the class broke them: I'm interested in learn Japanese. · She's crazy about playing the guitar. · We're tired of to wait. · Nobody here is afraid of speaking English.
  3. Pairs, sixty seconds: find the two broken ones and repair them. Take the repairs from two different pairs and rub out the wrong versions in front of the class.
  4. Thirty-second spelling check as promised last lesson: you say six adjectives (anxious, jealous, sensible, loyal, sociable, thoughtful), students write them, partners swap and mark.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page sixteen, bottom half. Two small pieces at the front of a word — and they can turn a friend into a problem.
📺 On screen: the four sentences in chalk plus six words dictated — no gadgets, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair, then write six words and mark a partner's paper. The promised test happens, which is why the promise works next time

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: the rule, seven opposites, then a text where two words need no prefix

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: read the two example sentences aloud and ask the two questions on the page. Establish the rule in the students' words, then write it up.
  2. Sort before you practise: two columns on the board (ADJECTIVES / VERBS) and the seven base words placed by the class — kind, pleasant, lucky, agree, appear, like, reliable, happy.
  3. ex.2 individually, then check with the ear test: say each word and let the class mark the stress — unKIND, unLUCKy, DISagree, DISappear. The Teacher's Book flags this and so does the unit test.
  4. ex.3 (EP): the text about online friendships. Warn them first: Two of these six need NO prefix. Read the whole sentence before you write.
  5. Check by nomination. Item 5 (lucky) and item 6 (agree) are the designed traps — ask why each one stays positive.
📺 On screen: the two columns on the board with the words sorted into them — no gadgets, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: sort, build, then read for meaning rather than pattern. Everyone answers at least once during the nominated check
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE tasks 26–31 (word formation). Negative prefixes are the most printed word-formation item of the whole section.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: add a five-minute round on the board: six sentences with a base word in capitals at the end (KIND, LUCK, PLEASE, RELY, HAPPY, AGREE). It fits inside the prefix relay without extending it.

Why: Vocabulary · the prefix relay

WHAT WE DO: eight rounds of build-and-spell, with the stress marked

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three teams. You call a base word (friendly, agree, happy, reliable, appear, interesting, like, lucky); one runner per team writes the negative form on the board and marks the stressed syllable.
  2. Two points for the right prefix, one extra for the right stress mark. Spelling errors cost the point — say so before round one.
  3. Rotate the runner every round so that all eight team members write at least once; the rest of the team must agree on the word before the runner may move.
  4. Finish with the two words the unit test asks for and the book never prints: unhappy and uninteresting. Say that out loud — it buys you credit when they meet them on the paper.
📺 On screen: the board split into three columns, one per team — no gadgets, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: the whole team decides, one member writes. Nobody sits: the team cannot score without agreeing first

28–32′
Move break · agree, disagree
standing, two walls
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that turns the new verb into a physical habit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two walls: I AGREE and I DISAGREE. Read out six opinions, one at a time — Online friends are real friends. · Fourteen is too young for a phone. · Homework at weekends should be banned.
  2. Everyone walks. One student from each wall gives one reason, in one sentence.
  3. Anyone may change wall after hearing a reason — announce that, because it is the only rule that makes the walk mean anything.
  4. Sit down on the last opinion and go straight into the exam listening: Page seventeen. Now you're listening to somebody explaining how to write a quiz.
📺 On screen: two A4 signs on opposite walls — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: move, justify, and be allowed to change their mind in public. The target verbs are said dozens of times without a single exercise

Why: Exam notes · predicting the word

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: what the notes are about, and what KIND of word is missing

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 as a thirty-second question: has anyone ever written a quiz? Who for? Two answers only.
  2. ex.2 is the stage that earns the marks. Pairs read the six gapped notes and decide the TYPE of word missing in each: a name, a noun, an adjective, a date, an email address.
  3. Collect the predictions on the board next to the gap numbers. Insist on precision at gap 4: What kind of adjective? Look at the sentence — it's the opposite of shy.
  4. Read the exam information aloud from the box: six gaps, one or two words, a number, a date or a time. Then the rule again, pointing at the board: Write the words you hear.
📺 On screen: the six gaps and the class's predictions written up on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: predict before they listen. The prediction is what turns listening into searching

Why: Listening Part 3 · in exam conditions

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 (track 06): the task, then the check that costs nobody anything

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Play track 06 straight through, no pauses, exam conditions. Students write.
  2. Pairs compare — but only compare, no discussion of spelling yet.
  3. Play again, pausing after each answer, and let them repair their own notes in a different colour. That second colour is the point: they can see what they missed the first time.
  4. Check as a class and take the honest count: Hands up if you wrote something that MEANT the right thing but wasn't the words. In the exam that's zero.
  5. Gap 5 (the date) and gap 6 (the email name) are where marks actually vanish — spell vickers on the board and accept all the official date variants.
📺 On screen: track 06 twice. Backup: if the audio fails, read the monologue from the audioscript (TB p.285) at natural speed, once straight through and once with pauses — the task is unchanged
👦 Students do: listen twice, repair in a second colour, then face the mark honestly

🎯 Speaking out · the Part 1 rotation
two circles, standing
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · the Part 1 rotation

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: six personal questions asked and answered against the clock

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two circles, inside facing outside (Kagan Inside-Outside Circle). The inside circle are examiners with the six questions from ex.3; the outside circle answer.
  2. Ninety seconds per pair. On the signal the outside circle moves one place to the left. After three rounds, swap roles.
  3. The standard is on the board and it is checked: two sentences minimum, and one phrase from the Prepare to speak box in every answer. Examiners are told to say Why? to any one-word answer — it is the politest correction in the room.
  4. Monitor with a pen: write down two excellent answers word for word, and three errors without names.
  5. Sit. Read the two excellent answers aloud and say exactly what made them work — the reason, the example, the phrase.
📺 On screen: the six questions and the Prepare to speak phrases on the board while they rotate — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: every student answers six questions and asks six questions. That is roughly six minutes of continuous speaking each, which no open-class discussion can match

Delayed correction
seated, whole class
Why: Delayed correction

WHAT WE DO: three sentences from the rotation, repaired by the class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the three errors on the board, anonymously and in full sentences.
  2. Pairs, sixty seconds each: repair them. Take the repairs from three different pairs.
  3. Name the pattern, not the person: if two of the three are the same kind of error, say so and put a ring around it.
  4. Leave the repaired sentences on the board while they pack — the last thing they see is the correct version.
📺 On screen: the three sentences in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair their own errors in public and anonymously

Structure
I disagree with you. · That was really unkind. · He disappeared for two weeks. · I like it because… · I don't like it because… · I prefer… · I usually… · I think it's…
Form
un- + adjective (unkind, unlucky, unpleasant, unreliable, unhappy, uninteresting) · dis- + verb (disagree, dislike, disappear); the prefix does not change the rest of the word.
Pron. watch
anxious /ˈæŋkʃəs/ — two syllables, no «ks» · sensible /ˈsensəbl/ against sensitive /ˈsensətɪv/ (different words, not a pair) · jealous /ˈdʒeləs/ · and the prefix stress rule that the test rewards: un- is unstressed (unKIND), dis- carries the stress (DISagree, DISappear)
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«unreliable» — can I trust this person? → no. And which part of the word tells me that? → un-, at the front, unstressed.
«I disagree» — is that a verb or an adjective? → a verb. Which prefix prefers verbs? → dis-. Say it and hear the stress: DISagree.
In the notes task, I hear «Understanding others». Can I write «to understand other people»? → no. Same meaning, no mark.

Assumptions: SB p.16 (bottom)–17, syllabus lesson 7. Prefixes un-, in- and im- were taught in Prepare 4 Unit 1, so un- is revision and dis- is the new half. Present simple questions are secure; the work in the speaking stage is about the length and shape of the answer, not the question.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students invent «disfriendly» and «unagree»Two columns on the board, adjectives and verbs, and the words sorted into them physically before any exercise. Then the ear test: say unKIND and DISagree and let the class hear where the stress goes.
In the listening they write the meaning, not the wordsSay the rule before the first play and write it on the board: Write the words you hear. Do not improve them. After checking, ask how many people wrote something that meant the right thing but scored zero — the honest hands teach faster than you do.
One-word answers in the speaking stageThe model does it first: Ahmed and Sandrine both add information, and the class counts how many sentences each answer has. Then the rule for the rotation: Minimum two sentences. The second one starts with because, or I usually, or I prefer.
The rotation collapses into chattingIt is timed, it is signalled, and it moves: ninety seconds per partner, then the outside circle steps one place to the left. Nobody has time to drift.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 26–31 (word formation — the most trainable line of the paper) and tasks 1–9 (listening for detail).

Negative prefixes ARE word formation: un- and dis- are the two the exam prints most often, and it prints them expecting a spelling, not a guess. The note-completion listening is the exam's discipline in miniature: write what you hear, do not improve it — a rewritten word scores zero even when the meaning is right.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
