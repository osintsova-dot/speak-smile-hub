🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use change as a verb and as a noun in eight different meanings, and turn a fact file into connected prose — the reading skill the last writing task of the year is built on.
What is genuinely hard
change is one word in both languages, which is why nobody looks at it twice — and then a change of address, change planes and change a ten-pound note turn out to be three unrelated things. The second half is hard for a different reason: a fact file has no grammar in it, and putting the grammar back is the whole task.
Typical error to expect
He was born in 15th June / We met in Monday instead of on — on with dates and days, in with months and years [high priority, A2/B1]
I want to be doctor instead of a doctor — a singular job needs a/an, and this unit is full of jobs [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The unit opens on the word, not on the topic, because change carries the whole unit and appears in the exam as both a noun and a verb. Twelve minutes on eight meanings looks generous and is not: the sorting is what makes the word visible. Then two texts, deliberately in this order. Joe's blog is a story with a twist — he would rather have his old life back — and it is read for meaning, with the past passive all over it and not named, exactly as the cereal text worked on Tuesday of last week. The Kevin Pearce pages are the opposite: a fact file and the same information written out as prose, with ten gaps between them. Filling those gaps is reading across from notes to sentences, which is precisely what the biography in lesson 67 will ask them to do from a blank page. Nothing about the writing task is mentioned today; the skill is simply practised.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Change your shoes. Change planes. Change a ten-pound note. Change your life. One word, four completely different things, and the last one is what this unit is about. Today you meet two people whose lives changed in a week — one of them because his mother wrote a song, the other because he fell off a snowboard.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a fast trade round on Unit 19, and the bridge into Unit 20

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — Unit 19 rewind. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade as a hot round with the ingredient and make/do cards, two trades each. Quiz, coach, trade — go!
  3. Then one question to the whole class, and it closes the unit: Tell me one thing that is made in a factory and one thing that is made at home.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page one hundred and twelve. Last unit of the year — and it starts with one word that means eight different things.
📺 On screen: printed word cards from Unit 19 — the cards in their hands are the medium, so no backup is needed; if the set has gone missing, read the words out and students give the phrase back
👦 Students do: trade cards, then produce two passives. Expect make and do to be noticeably better than on Thursday. Typical problem: do a mistake still surfaces in one or two — worth a single correction and no more

Lesson frame

Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write four phrases on the board, one under the other: change your shoes · change planes · change a ten-pound note · change your life
  2. Same word. Four completely different things. Which one is this unit about? Take answers for 30 seconds.
  3. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: today is that word and two people whose lives changed — one in a week, one in a second.
  4. Set the bar: Every answer today comes with the line from the text that proves it. No line, no answer.
📺 On screen: the four phrases in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: argue about which meaning the unit is about. Expect them to pick the last one immediately, which is right. Typical problem: somebody translates all four into one Russian verb and concludes they are the same — that is the observation the whole vocabulary stage is built on

8–20′
Vocabulary · change as a verb and a noun
pairs → class
Why: One word, eight meanings, two word classes. Sorting them is worth twelve minutes because the same word carries the entire unit and appears in the exam in both classes.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.112: four verb meanings and four noun meanings, sorted and then used

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Say the thing that makes the exercise make sense before it starts: The first four sentences have a verb. The last four have a noun. Sort them before you match anything.
  2. ex.1 in pairs, 5 minutes. Key: 1 b · 2 d · 3 a · 4 c · 5 h · 6 e · 7 f · 8 g.
  3. Then the concept questions, and take them slowly: TB p.221, three questions that fix the two word classes in ninety seconds: Where can you always change money? (in a bank) · Why might a footballer have a change of shirt? (the first one gets wet at half time) · If something changes your life, is that positive or negative? (it can be either, or both). And the parallel worth naming: run, sleep and work are verbs and nouns in exactly the same way.
  4. Elicit two more words that are both verb and noun with no change at all — run, sleep, work — and leave them on the board.
  5. ex.2 sends them to SB p.139: seven situations, one sentence each with change. Solo, 4 minutes, then Kagan RallyCoach in pairs — the partner has to say whether it is the verb or the noun.
📺 On screen: the eight sentences on the screen in two groups (Backup: they are printed on SB p.112 — work from the page and draw the VERB and NOUN columns on the board, filling them in as the pairs report)
👦 Students do: sort by word class, then match, then produce. Expect the four verb meanings to be fast and the four noun ones slow. Typical error: a change of shoes read as «different shoes» instead of «a second pair»

20–28′
Lead-in · six life changes
groups of 3
Why: Six pictures give the class the topic before a word of the text is read, and they surface the vocabulary the reading will need.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.112 and the About you box: six pictures, and the changes in their own lives

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.221: books closed, students write down what has changed in their lives in the last six months (I have joined a basketball club) and share. If nothing has changed, they discuss what the good and bad sides of such a change would be.
  2. Then ex.3, in threes, 3 minutes: six pictures, six life changes — What has changed for this person?
  3. Feed the language in as it is needed: move house · change schools · get married · have an accident · move country. These come back properly in lesson 3, so do not drill them now.
  4. Two questions from TB p.221 about the pictures before the reading: Where is the plane going to and from? What happened when he played football?
  5. Kagan Think-Pair-Share on the one that opens the text: Which of these six would be the hardest?
📺 On screen: the six pictures on the screen (Backup: they are printed on SB p.112 — the groups work from the paper book and you write the six changes on the board as they are named)
👦 Students do: describe six pictures and talk about their own six months. Expect the moving-house picture to produce the most personal answers. Typical error: He moved to another school for changed schools — accept it, and write the phrase up next to it

28–40′
Reading · Joe's blog
solo → pairs
Why: A first-person story with a genuine twist at the end — he would rather have his old life back. The grammar of the next lesson is all over it, met today as meaning only.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 p.112 (track 143): read once, and find which of the six pictures is his

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pre-teach one word (TB p.221): release — when a company releases a film or a recording it makes it available to buy. Test it fast: What was released last month that you wanted?
  2. Set the task: Two minutes. One question only — which picture is Joe's? Key: picture C.
  3. Play track 143 so they hear the whole blog read at speed while they follow.
  4. One question to the whole class before the detail work, because it is the point of the text: His life got better in every way. Read the last sentence. Is he happy?
  5. For your own reference: SB p.112, Joe's blog «Life changes». Joe is 15. His mum wrote and sang him songs at bedtime — they were not written by other people, they were all her own work. She changed her job and the family had to move from London to New York; he was excited but sad to leave his friends. While they were changing planes in Dublin, his mum started talking to a man who worked for a record company, and he asked her to send him some songs. A year later her first album was released and sold a million copies in a week. They moved from an uncomfortable apartment to a big one with a view of Central Park, and after that everything was done for them: the food was cooked by someone else, the apartment was cleaned, Joe was driven to school, and he and his mum were flown everywhere in a private plane. The last line is the one worth reading twice: he would still like to go back to his old life in London.
📺 On screen: track 143 in the board app with the blog on the screen (Backup: it is printed on SB p.112 — read it aloud yourself at natural speed while the class follows in the book, and put the one gist question on the board)
👦 Students do: read fast for one answer, then react to the ending. Expect the last sentence to genuinely surprise them, which is the whole reason the text is in the book. Typical problem: they want to argue about whether he is right — let it run for sixty seconds

Reading · Joe in detail
pairs
Why: Seven questions that cannot be answered by matching words, because the text tells the story in the order it happened and the questions do not.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5 p.112: seven questions that need the line from the text

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Seven questions in pairs, 5 minutes. The rule from the lesson frame: Every answer comes with the line that proves it. Key: He remembered that his mum always sang him songs (at bedtime). · They moved because his mum got a job in New York. / because his mum changed her job. · He was (very) excited but sad to leave his friends. · His mum met a man who worked for a record company. · Yes — it sold a million copies in a week. · It was very big and it had a great view of Central Park. · His old life (in London)..
  2. Kagan Numbered Heads Together to collect: Question five — number twos, boards up!
  3. Question 5 is the one worth stopping on: How do you KNOW people liked the album? The text never says «people liked it» — it says it sold a million copies in a week. That is the difference between what is said and what is shown.
  4. Fast finishers (TB p.221): write two questions you would like to ask Joe, and the class answers them in role at the end of the stage.
📺 On screen: the seven questions on the screen, revealed one at a time (Backup: they are printed on SB p.112 — pairs work from the page and you write the proof lines on the board as they are quoted)
👦 Students do: answer with evidence, then report by number. Expect question 4 to be the one that is skimmed — the man in Dublin is easy to miss. Typical error: question 3 answered with «excited» only, when the text says both excited AND sad

WHAT WE DO: two minutes of movement between the first text and the second

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. One thing that has changed in your life this year — one sentence each, round the room. Then sit down.
  2. Take them fast, do not comment, and do not correct — the point is the switch of gear, not the grammar.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required

50–60′
Reading · the Kevin Pearce fact file
class → pairs
Why: A fact file is the most compressed text there is: dates and noun phrases with all the grammar taken out. Reading it before the biography means the biography is about language rather than about facts.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.114: what the photos and the fact file tell you before a sentence is read

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pairs describe the three photos, 2 minutes: a snowboarder in the air, a man doing balance exercises in a gym, three men in LOVE YOUR BRAIN T-shirts. What do you think happened between the first photo and the second?
  2. Possible answer: He was a snowboarder and he had an accident which affected his brain..
  3. Pre-teach three words (TB p.225): slopestyle — racing downhill around obstacles · halfpipe — an artificial ramp shaped like a raised swimming pool · swallow — to move food from your mouth into your stomach.
  4. Read the fact file together, 3 minutes. Ask two questions that show what a fact file is: How many verbs are there in it? (almost none) What is it made of? (dates and noun phrases).
  5. Background, if the room needs it: TB p.225: brain injuries are classed mild, moderate or severe. Mild cases usually recover in about three weeks; Kevin's would be classed severe, with a serious effect on quality of life. The commonest causes are violence and transport accidents — especially motorbikes — construction work and extreme sports, and men are twice as likely as women to suffer one. Prevention is what works: seat belts, air bags, helmets in American football, referees taking a footballer off after a head injury. Also worth knowing before the text: slopestyle is racing downhill around obstacles, a halfpipe is an artificial ramp shaped like a raised swimming pool, and reel in The Crash Reel means both the wheel that film is wound on and the verb «to move from side to side as if about to fall».
📺 On screen: the three photos and the fact file on the screen (Backup: everything is printed on SB p.114 — describe the photos from the paper book and read the fact file aloud line by line with the class following)
👦 Students do: describe the photos and read a fact file. Expect the accident to be guessed from the second photo. Typical problem: the room goes quiet at the brain injury — give the facts plainly and move to what he did next

60–74′
Reading · the biography with ten gaps
solo → pairs
Why: The same information twice, once as notes and once as prose — so the ten gaps are filled by looking across from one to the other. That is genuine reading, and it is also how a biography is written.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.114 (track 145): the same life written out as prose, with ten gaps

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Name the move before they start: Everything you need is in the fact file above. You are not guessing — you are looking across.
  2. One demonstration with gap 2, because it is the only one that needs arithmetic: Born 1987. First competition January 1997. So he was …? (ten) — and the fact file never says the number.
  3. A second demonstration with gap 5, because it teaches the strategy: What kind of word goes after «was very badly»? (an adjective or a participle).
  4. Solo, 7 minutes, silent mode. Then play track 145 to check while they follow. Key: 1 New Hampshire · 2 ten · 3 2002 · 4 seven · 5 hurt / injured · 6 walking · 7 shown · 8 Love Your Brain · 9 Since · 10 live.
  5. Kagan Showdown on the four that go wrong most: gaps 4, 7, 9 and 10.
  6. For your own reference: SB p.114, the Kevin Pearce fact file and the biography built from it. Born in New Hampshire on 1st November 1987; entered his first snowboarding competition in January 1997, aged ten; moved to California with his brother Adam in 2002 and trained full-time at Mammoth Mountain; won the Slopestyle event in New Zealand in August 2006 and the open halfpipe in Switzerland in January 2008. In December 2009 he crashed while training in Utah and received a serious brain injury; he spent three months in hospital and it took him a year to relearn walking, talking and swallowing. His brother Adam left work to look after him. In 2013 the Sundance Film Festival showed The Crash Reel, a documentary about his life; in 2014 the brothers set up the Love Your Brain Foundation, which teaches yoga and meditation to people with brain injuries. Since 2014 he has given talks in schools and hospitals about looking after your brain and wearing a helmet. He moved back to Vermont in 2017.
📺 On screen: track 145 in the board app with the gapped biography on the screen (Backup: it is printed on SB p.114 — students work from the page and you read the completed text aloud yourself, twice, pausing at each gap for the class to call out the answer)
👦 Students do: fill ten gaps by reading across from the notes. Expect 7–8 out of 10. Typical errors: gap 4 (seven — it needs counting from 2002 to 2009) and gap 9 (Since, capitalised, because it starts the sentence)

Structure
Can you change a ten-pound note? · We had to change planes in Dublin. · I'll email you my change of address. · That will make a change. · He was born in New Hampshire. · A year later her first album was released.
Form
change + object (a note, planes, a jacket, your life) against a change of + noun (shoes, address). Dates: on 1st November, in 2002, since 2014.
Pron. watch
the /tʃ/ of change — not /ʃ/ and not /ts/. And the dates read aloud: the first of November nineteen eighty-seven, two thousand and two.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Can you change a ten-pound note?» Am I asking for different money or for smaller money? → smaller money, the same amount.
«We changed planes in Dublin.» Did we buy a new plane? → no. We got off one and onto another.
«Bring a change of shoes.» How many pairs am I asking for? → one extra pair, so two altogether.
«My grandparents hate change.» Is change here a verb or a noun? → a noun, and an uncountable one — no a in front of it.
Kevin was born in 1987 and entered his first competition in January 1997. How old was he? → ten. The fact file never says it, and that is gap 2.

Both past tenses are secure and the present simple passive was taught last week; the past passive appears constantly in both texts today and is deliberately not named. ⚠️ The syllabus calls this lesson «SB p.112–113» and gives it the life-changes phrases — both are wrong: p.113 is the grammar page (lesson 66) and the life-changes phrases are on p.115 (lesson 67). What p.112 actually teaches is change as a verb and a noun.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
