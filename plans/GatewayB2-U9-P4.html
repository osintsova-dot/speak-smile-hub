🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Take part in a real discussion in English using two families of expressions: checking that your listener has understood you (Do you see what I mean? What I mean is … Are you with me?) and asking someone to clarify (Are you saying that …? I'm not sure what you mean by … Could you go over that again?), on the topic of e-sports and video games.
What is genuinely hard
Not the language — the behaviour. Russian classroom habit is to answer the teacher and stop; a discussion needs a student to notice that the other person has lost them and to say so, out loud, in English. The second difficulty is prosody: these expressions are questions and they need rising intonation, and said flat they sound aggressive rather than cooperative.
Typical error to expect
Silence instead of «Sorry, I'm not with you» — the student who has not understood simply waits, and the conversation dies. In the oral exam that silence is a lost mark for interaction; in a real discussion it is a lost point.
Flat intonation on «Do you see what I mean?» — a falling tone turns a check into a challenge.
Why the lesson is built this way
Ninety minutes on one Student's Book page, and it works because the page is a script for a whole lesson, not an exercise. The topic is genuinely arguable — should e-sports be in the Olympic Games — and the class takes a position before hearing the model. The two expression families are not presented as a list: they are heard inside a recorded discussion first, then sorted by function, then drilled for intonation, and only then used. The final stage is a real group discussion with observers counting the expressions, so the target language is visibly required and not just available.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Thousands of people fill an arena to watch other people play video games, and the winners earn more than most footballers. Should that be in the Olympics? Today you argue it out — and you learn the six phrases that keep an argument alive instead of letting it die in silence.”

WHAT WE DO: ninety seconds of gerund–infinitive recycling, then straight into the photograph

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one full sentence back: One sentence — what is the last thing you used your phone for today? Any answer counts as long as it is in English and it is a sentence.
  2. 2 minutes — yesterday's forms, out loud. Kagan RallyRobin in pairs, taking turns: six true sentences in ninety seconds using stop, remember, forget or like, with the gerund or the infinitive chosen on purpose.
  3. 1 minute — one pair performs, the class judges the meaning. Say the sentence, the room says what it means. That is the whole check and it takes sixty seconds.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Page one hundred and eighteen. Look at the photograph — that arena is full, and every one of those people came to watch somebody else play a video game.
📺 On screen: nothing to project — this is a speaking lesson from minute one
👦 Students do: produce six meaning-carrying sentences each, then judge two aloud

WHAT WE DO: the aim, the rule of the day, and the observer role explained once

Why: Lead-in · what e-sports actually are

WHAT WE DO: ex.1a and 1b: the photo, then the short article, with the experts in the room used

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1a in pairs, three minutes: what do you know about e-sports, and what do you think of them? If a pair knows nothing, they describe the photograph and speculate with They must be … · It might be …
  2. Find out who in the room plays or watches competitively. Those students are the experts for the next stage and are told so.
  3. ex.1b, four minutes, silent: read the article. One job — find TWO things you did not know.
  4. Take four facts to the floor, quickly. The ones that usually land: thousands watch live in arenas, the players earn salaries, and the two main types are first-person shooters and MOBAs.
  5. Put the ten topic words on the board as they come up: tournament, arena, league, prize money, competitive, addictive, violent, banned.
📺 On screen: the article is on the page and the photograph does half the work — nothing to project. Backup: none needed
👦 Students do: speculate in pairs, read a factual article with a specific job, and contribute two new facts

WHAT WE DO: ex.1c: the first real discussion of the lesson, before any model is given

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1c in Kagan Stand Up–Hand Up–Pair Up, six minutes: three different partners, ninety seconds each, same question — should e-sports be part of the Olympic Games?
  2. The instruction that makes it work: with each new partner you must add one argument you HEARD from the previous partner. The ideas travel round the room.
  3. Sit down and take a vote. Write the two numbers on the board — they get compared with the recorded discussion in fifteen minutes.
  4. Three reasons to the floor, one from each side, thirty seconds each. Do not comment or correct — this is fluency, and the correction is at minute seventy-eight.
📺 On screen: the class vote written on the board next to the rule of the day
👦 Students do: argue the same question with three different partners, carrying borrowed arguments forward, then vote

Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Seventeen-year-olds who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies quietly.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that also builds the argument bank for later

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Corners: four corners of the room labelled SPORT · NOT SPORT · DEPENDS · NO IDEA. Everyone goes to a corner and finds one reason to say to the person next to them.
  2. Two more rounds with two more statements from the article: Watching other people play is as good as playing. · Prize money makes it professional.
  3. After each round, one voice from each corner. One sentence, no speeches.
  4. Sit down straight into the recording: Two people are about to have exactly this argument. One of them will change how you talk.
📺 On screen: the four corner labels on sheets of paper, written by hand before the lesson
👦 Students do: commit to a corner three times and produce three reasons standing

44–52′
The two families + the intonation drill
class → pairs
Why: The two families + the intonation drill

WHAT WE DO: ex.3b: sorting the bank by function, then making the questions sound like questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3b, two minutes in pairs: complete the two headings. Answers — 1 b (checking someone has understood you), 2 a (asking someone to clarify).
  2. Elicit the meaning of the four that are opaque: Are you following me? / Are you with me? (do you understand), Could you go over that again? (explain it again), I don't understand what you're getting at (what are you trying to say).
  3. The intonation drill, four minutes, and it is the most valuable four minutes of the lesson. Backchain each Yes/No check with a clear rise: … what I MEAN? ↗ · … what I'm SAYing? ↗ · Are you WITH me? ↗
  4. Then the same expressions said with a FALL, deliberately, so the class hears how rude they become. That contrast is what makes the rise stick.
  5. Pairs, ninety seconds: one says any sentence about video games, the other tags a checking expression onto it with a rise. Swap.
📺 On screen: the two families written up in two columns on the board — this is the board the discussion stage will run on
👦 Students do: sort fourteen expressions by function, then drill five of them for rising intonation in pairs

Preparing the arguments

Why: Preparing the arguments

WHAT WE DO: ex.4: silent written preparation on the three questions, so the discussion has content

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the three questions with the class: are video games too violent · do violent games produce violent teenagers · should they be banned?
  2. Set the structure on the board so nobody stares at a blank page: My answer: yes / no. Reason 1 + example. Reason 2 + example. What someone who disagrees would say.
  3. Eight minutes, silent, written, individually. No talking at all — this is the stage that decides whether the discussion has anything in it.
  4. The fourth line is compulsory and it is the hardest: what would the other side say? A student who has written that line cannot be ambushed.
  5. Circulate and feed language, quietly and one word at a time. Write nothing on their pages.
📺 On screen: the four-line note structure on the board; the rest of the board still holds the two expression families
👦 Students do: write structured notes on three questions, including the counter-argument, in silence

🎯 Practice makes perfect · the discussion
groups of three
Why: 🎯 Practice makes perfect · the discussion

WHAT WE DO: ex.5: three real discussions, with observers counting the target language

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of three. Roles: two speakers, one observer with a tally slip. The observer counts checking expressions and clarification requests, by name.
  2. The target is written on the board and it is not negotiable: Everybody uses at least TWO checking expressions and ONE clarification request.
  3. Round 1, four minutes, question 1. Then rotate the observer role and change the question. Round 2, four minutes, question 2. Round 3, four minutes, question 3 — by now every student has observed once and spoken twice.
  4. 🎯 Between rounds, thirty seconds only: the observer reads the tally aloud. No advice, just numbers. The numbers do the coaching.
  5. Last three minutes: one group performs the most disputed question in front of the class, and the class tallies. Applause, not correction.
📺 On screen: the target sentence on the board; tally slips are quarter sheets of paper. Backup: if the room is too small for three groups to be audible, run two rounds in the corridor half of the room and swap
👦 Students do: speak in two rounds and observe in one, hitting a named target of expressions each time
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains Task 4 of the oral paper (the monologue with an argument) and the interaction marks throughout. The Russian oral rewards a candidate who keeps going and repairs misunderstanding; today's two families of expressions are exactly the repair kit, and they transfer word for word.

whole class
Why: Delayed correction · five sentences you actually said

WHAT WE DO: the errors you collected, repaired by the class, with two of them praised

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write five sentences on the board exactly as you heard them, names hidden. Three should be errors that block meaning, two should be excellent sentences you want repeated.
  2. The class repairs the three in pairs, ninety seconds, then one student writes each corrected version up.
  3. For the two good ones, ask what makes them good. Usually it is an example — the difference between «games are violent» and «the game I play has a fourteen certificate for a reason».
  4. Finish with the intonation, one last time: three checking expressions, in chorus, with the rise. Twenty seconds, and it is the thing they will remember on the way home.
📺 On screen: the five sentences on the board, unattributed — nothing digital
👦 Students do: repair three sentences in pairs, analyse two good ones, and drill the rise once more

Structure
Do you see what I mean? · Do you get what I'm saying? · Are you with me? · What I mean is … · That's not quite what I meant. · Are you saying that …? · I'm not sure what you mean by … · Could you go over that again? · Sorry, I'm not with you.
Form
checking = a Yes/No question with rising intonation, said AFTER your point · clarifying = a question or a hedge said BEFORE you answer, so that you answer the right thing · reformulating = What I mean is + a simpler version, never a louder one.
Pron. watch
rising tone on all five Yes/No checks — Do you see what I MEAN? ↗ · Are you WITH me? ↗ · falling tone on What I mean is … ↘ · the whole of «Do you get what I'm saying» reduces to about four beats in natural speech, which is why it is drilled and not just read
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Are you with me?» — am I asking where you are? → no, I am asking if you follow. And does my voice go up or down? → up.
«That's not quite what I meant.» Am I saying you are wrong? → no, I am saying I explained it badly. Which is politer, and which is safer.
If you have not understood, what do you do? → you SAY so. Silence is the only wrong answer in this lesson.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Two students carry the whole discussionGroup size is three, never four, and every group has a rotating OBSERVER with a tally sheet. An observer who has counted nobody's expressions for two minutes is told to hand the role over and join in.
The expressions are understood but never usedThey are required, not offered: each group is given a target — every member uses at least two checking expressions and one clarification request. The observer's tally is read out at the end.
Russian creeps in during the preparation stagePreparation is written, not spoken: notes only, in English, four minutes, no talking at all. The talking starts when the notes are finished.
A student genuinely does not know what e-sports areThe article in ex.1b answers exactly that, and the photo carries half the meaning. Pair the non-gamers with the gamers deliberately for stage three — the expertise gap makes the clarification language necessary rather than artificial.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 4 of the oral paper (the monologue with an argument) and task 38 (the written opinion piece).

The oral part of the Russian exam is scored on whether the candidate keeps talking coherently and answers the actual question. The two families of expressions from today's Speaking bank — checking that you have been understood, and asking for clarification — do the job the Russian oral calls «взаимодействие»: they turn a stalled turn back into speech instead of silence. The e-sports content is a bonus: video games and sport are two of the most frequent topic cards.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
