🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Why it is built this way (for the teacher): A pair of two broadcasts: a spoken one (agreeing on a day out — a skill that works both in the exam and in life) and a written one (instructions — a genre where order matters, not beauty). The 'planning floor' set-up: each partner has their own constraints (budget, time, weather), so you can't agree in silence — you have to make suggestions and object with live phrases. too and also is served as 'the editor's standard': instructions with also at the end of the sentence go back to the author.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Tomorrow you're going somewhere. Half the decisions are not yours: your partner has a budget, a bus timetable and a weather forecast, and you have your own. You get four phrases to make suggestions and four to answer — 'Why don't we…?', 'How about…?', 'OK, fine', 'Really? That's too early.' No plan, no trip. Then round two: you write the packing instructions for someone who has never been. Editors return texts with 'also' in the wrong place. Planning floor — open!”
In simpler words, if the class needs support: Tomorrow there is a day out, but half the decisions are not yours: your partner has their own budget, their own bus timetable and their own forecast. You can only agree with live phrases for suggesting and objecting. Then the second broadcast — we write the instructions on what to put in the bag and how. The editor sends back broken too and also.

🔥 The planning floor
pairs · moving

WHAT WE DO: a live planning meeting: everyone has their own constraints, they have to agree on the place, the transport, the time, the clothes and the lunch; then the partner changes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. I hand out the constraint cards: You've got 500 roubles · You're busy until 11:00 · You haven't got a waterproof jacket · You hate picnics · The forecast says showers.
  2. Five decisions: where, how you travel, what time, what you wear, what you eat. You must AGREE — and I need at least one disagreement on the way.
  3. Constraint: only phrases from the Key phrases; Russian costs the crew a point.
  4. Kagan Find Someone Who for the second round: Find someone who agrees to a picnic · who wants the early bus · who hasn't got a waterproof jacket. The name goes in the box only after a full-sentence answer.
  5. Change of partner after 6 minutes: New partner, new problem. Same phrases. We count: how many pairs got all the way to an agreement.
📺 On screen: constraint cards · Key phrases on the screen · timer (Backup: the same written on the board before the lesson)
👦 Students do: they walk around and argue for real. Expect: the information gap keeps the English going without reminders; disagreement gets said because it pays

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.56: the order of the three paragraphs and the 'what we pack and why' table

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: Three paragraphs, wrong order. Fix it. (TB key: 1 b — introduction · 2 c — the bottom of the backpack · 3 a — the top section).
  2. ex.2 the table. TB key: What? — top: water bottle and waterproof jacket; bottom: sleeping bag, clothes and cooking equipment. Why? — top: because you use these things during the journey; bottom: because the sleeping bag is big but light; because you don't need these things during the day.
  3. Notice: every paragraph says WHAT and WHY. A list is not instructions. — we underline the WHY lines in the model.
  4. Kagan Think-Pair-Share: Do YOU agree that heavy things go on top? Think — pair — share. 30 seconds for each step.
📺 On screen: SB p.56 · timer
👦 Students do: they see the 'what + why' frame as the shape of the writing. Expect: they notice that the heavy things go on top — and argue with it (which is good, it means they are reading)

WHAT WE DO: THINK (two groups of things + comparing with the partner + sentences) → PREPARE (three paragraphs) → WRITE → CHECK → peer review → the wall

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. THINK (ex.5): Beach holiday. Hand luggage and suitcase — three or four items per column. Then compare with your partner: what's missing? (TB options — hand luggage: snack, water bottle, book, passport, tickets, money; suitcase: T-shirts, sandals, sun hat, beach towel, wash bag).
  2. Now three sentences with the underlined phrases: 'It's important to put your mobile phone in your hand luggage.'
  3. PREPARE: three paragraphs (general introduction · suitcase · hand luggage). WRITE following the model on p.56.
  4. CHECK against the three questions in the book: are also and too in the right place? is there a comparative or a superlative? is there a modal? Then peer review (ex.6) — the partner underlines what is wrong and writes a comment, the author rewrites it (the TB order).
  5. Publication: the instructions go on the unit wall. Instructions are LIVE!
📺 On screen: editor's checklist · the unit wall · timer
👦 Students do: they write in class from a model they have just seen. Expect: the peer review catches 80% of the also errors before publication

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, learners can make, accept and refuse suggestions with 8 Key phrases in a real planning dialogue and write a 3-paragraph set of packing instructions using too and also correctly.
  • Subsidiary. The video with Kirsty and Sam (ex.1–3); ex.4 completing the dialogue; Skills boost THINK→PREPARE→PRACTISE→PERFORM; the model 'How to pack for a camping trip' (ex.1–2); the subskill too / also (ex.3–4); ex.5 their own instructions; ex.6 peer review.
  • Personal. We write in class, not at home — so I see the process. The newsroom rule: a set of instructions without three paragraphs and without an answer to CHECK is not published; the author fixes it, not me.
By the end, students can…
  • make a suggestion in four different ways
  • agree and disagree politely
  • write instructions in three clear paragraphs
  • put too and also in the right position

Target language
Lexis
We should (go by train).Let's (get the bus).Why don't we (take a picnic)?How about (getting the 9:00 bus)?OK, fine.Good idea.Really? That's too early.I'm not sure. I don't like (picnics).
Structure
We should go by train. · Why don't we take a picnic? · How about getting the 9:00 bus? · OK, fine. / Good idea. / Really? That's too early. · It's a good idea to take a sun hat. · It's also quite light.
Form
too — at the end of the sentence, usually after a list · also — after the verb be and BEFORE all other verbs ('Our friends also came', not 'Our friends came also') · US → UK: sneakers → trainers · awesome → brilliant (the dialogue uses the American variant)
Pron. watch
polite falling intonation in the suggestions: How about getting the 9:00 bus? ↘ · in Let's we don't swallow the /ts/ · should — the l is silent: /ʃʊd/
Concept checks (CCQs) · Assumptions
'Why don't we take a picnic?' — is that a reproach? → no, it is a suggestion: 'how about…'.
'Our friends came also' — do people say that? → no: Our friends also came (also before the main verb) or Our friends came too.
The instructions are ready but there is no paragraph about the hand luggage — do we publish? → no, the editor will send it back.

Assumptions: SB pp.55–57, KTP pair 35 (the KTP says pp.56–58 and 'a description of a country with sequencing words' — in fact it is Real-world speaking p.55 + Writing pp.56–57, the genre is INSTRUCTIONS, the subskill is too/also). The skeletons of the dialogue and of the text come ready from the book — the mission only frames them and adds constraints.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
They read the dialogue instead of playing itRole cards: each partner has their own constraints (a budget of 500 ₽ / busy until 11:00 / forgot the raincoat). The information gap makes the argument real.
They agree to everything and the disagreeing phrases never get saidThe quota: at least one 'Really? That's too early' or 'I'm not sure' per pair, otherwise the dialogue is not accepted (this is the TB requirement for PREPARE too).
The instructions turn into a list of thingsThe newsroom rule: three paragraphs following the model on p.56 (introduction · suitcase · hand luggage) and each of them has a WHY, not only a WHAT.
Classroom management — signals
«Eyes up in 3-2-1» — count down calmly → all eyes on you at «1». No shouting over noise, ever.
Visible timer — timer on screen = we work; timer stops = pens/hands down. The timer is the boss, not you.
Volume dial — arm up/down = louder/quieter; «radio whisper» for rehearsals.
«Boards up!» — mini-whiteboard rounds: everyone commits, all boards up together — nobody hides, nobody shouts first.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, class answers with two and freezes — instant reset mid-game.
English-first — ideas in Russian are welcome, POINTS only for the English version — recast and repeat.
