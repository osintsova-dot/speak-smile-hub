🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Run the book's own Review 3 spread (Units 9–12) to the end: the four vocabularies sorted and used, the two tense gates re-opened, the modals rewritten, and the closing exam-shaped cloze solved with reasons — plus a written revision map each.
What is genuinely hard
Honesty, not language: Units 9–10 are a month and a holiday away and were already tested twice, so re-teaching them would bore the room — while pretending they need no touch would strand the students the crossword is actually for. The lesson must hold both truths at once.
Typical error to expect
stomach ache (not stomachache with the letters scrambled) · cough (the gh says f!) · toothache and earache are ONE word · mosquito · a misspelt word scores nothing, in a class test and later in the exam
It mustn't be a fox for «этого не может быть» — Russian «не может быть» maps onto mustn't, but English impossibility is CAN'T; mustn't is prohibition, not deduction [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The split is named to the class in one sentence: two old units (spaced retrieval — a month is exactly the interval at which pulling words back pays most) and two new ones (the working core, fresh from Tuesday's test). The book's own tasks carry the lesson in their printed order — crossword, sixteen-word sort, tense conversations — with the КТП's grammar auction as the centrepiece: teams bid play-money on correct-or-not sentences built from the review's own Corpus items, because nothing makes a teenager read a sentence harder than having paid for it. The closing cloze (food + illness in one text) is the review's own finale and the honest proof the mixing worked. The lesson ends where the КТП points it: reflection — a written map of what each student revises at home, which turns a review into a plan. Entry tickets (the five-mistake lists from the test) are collected at the door.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Four units. Sixty minutes of games, one auction, and one text that mixes soup with flu. The old stuff from December — let's see what survived the holidays. The new stuff from this month — let's make it permanent. And you leave with a one-line plan of exactly what YOU revise at home. Not the class. You.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts. The rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a four-unit memory round, tickets in, and the bridge into the review

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — the four-unit hot round. Kagan Mix-Pair-Share, two partners: One thing you remember from the SHOPPING unit… and one animal fact that shocked you. Thirty seconds each way.
  3. Collect tickets at the door as they entered — the five-mistake lists from the test. Anyone without one writes it now, from memory, no drama.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the lesson: Books open, page seventy-four. Review three — four units, one hour, one auction. And an honest map first.
📺 On screen: questions by voice, tickets collected by hand — voice and paper are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: mingle, retrieve December, hand in tickets. Expect the mosquito fact to return first — shock is glue; expect two ticketless students — the write-it-now rule is kind and firm

Vocabulary · the money crossword
teams
Why: Unit 9 has not been touched since before the holidays — which is exactly why it opens the lesson: a month is the interval at which pulling words back out of memory pays most.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: the U9 crossword as a race — December's words pulled back

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Teams of three-four, books open, 5 minutes on the clock: ex.1, nine money clues. It is a race. Spelling counts — a misspelt word is an empty square. Key: ex.1 — the money crossword (Unit 9, retrieval): Across: 1 save up · 4 give it away · 5 checkout · 8 special offer · 9 take it back; Down: 2 price · 3 bank account · 6 receipt · 7 change
  2. Check fast, team by team, one clue each; receipt gets its silent-p ceremony one more time.
  3. Ten-second poll: Hands up if a word came back SLOWER than in December. Normal. That's why today exists. — first entries go onto revision maps.
📺 On screen: the crossword is on SB p.74 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: race the crossword, check, log the slow words. Expect save up and checkout instantly, special offer and take it back slower — exactly the point; the map line writes itself. Typical error: reciept — December's ghost, exorcised again

Vocabulary · four topics, one box
pairs
Why: The book's own sixteen-word sort runs all four units through one task — and shows everyone, quickly and kindly, which of the four topics needs their attention at home.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2: sixteen words sorted; ex.3: eight gaps filled

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.2 in pairs, 3 minutes: sixteen words, four topics, four words each. Key: ex.2 — four words to each topic from one box of sixteen: 1 Describing food and taste: bitter, fresh, raw, sour · 2 The body: chin, forehead, shoulder, toe · 3 Health: cough, cut, flu, stomach ache · 4 Animals: deer, mosquitoes, eagles, wolves.
  2. Fast check as a shout-round: you name the topic, pairs chorus their four.
  3. ex.3 — Kagan RallyCoach, one pen, eight sentences, two words per topic. Key: ex.3 — complete with two words from each topic: 1 cough · 2 bitter · 3 eagles · 4 forehead · 5 Mosquitoes · 6 fresh · 7 flu · 8 shoulder.
  4. The eagles line gets its ten seconds: female eagles are larger than males — True fact. The quiz unit never ends.
📺 On screen: the box and sentences are on SB p.74 — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: sort, chorus, complete with reasons. Expect bitter/sour to swap in weaker pairs — one taste mime fixes it; expect the body words to be fastest (they are the newest). Typical error: cough filed under body — it is an illness; the topic answers

30–40′
Grammar · the gates return
pairs → class
Why: The two tense gates of the half-year (perfect vs simple, will vs going to) in the book's own conversations — with the deciding question said out loud every time, because the reason is what transfers.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 perfect vs simple + ex.2 will vs going to — reasons compulsory

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1, four conversations — solo 3 minutes, then Kagan RallyCoach with the reason: Date or no date? Key: ex.1 — present perfect vs past simple conversations (Units 9–10, retrieval): 1 Have you ever seen a shark? / last year I swam with dolphins · 2 How long has Ruth had stomach ache? / She hasn't eaten anything since then · 3 We went to a Japanese restaurant last weekend / I have never tried Japanese food · 4 were over 20 minutes late this morning / Have they ever arrived on time?
  2. ex.2, four more — the deciding question rides again: decided before, or right now? Key: ex.2 — will vs going to (Unit 11, core): 1 they'll have lots of special offers soon / I'm going to buy these ones now · 2 we're going to leave in ten minutes / I don't think he'll want to watch · 3 Our teacher is going to have a baby in June / I'll send her a card · 4 I'm going to take my new jacket back / I'll come with you!
  3. Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together — number twos report ex.1, number threes ex.2.
  4. One line on the board per gate: a date → past simple · decided before → going to. They survive into block 7.
📺 On screen: the two gate lines in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: choose, justify, report by number. Expect the December gate (perfect/simple) to be rustier than Tuesday's — say so kindly; the map gets its grammar lines here. Typical error: Have you seen it yesterday? — the date rule, once, chorally

40–54′
Grammar auction
teams
Why: The КТП's own format: teams bid play-money on sentences that may or may not be correct. Nothing makes a class read a sentence harder than having paid for it.

WHAT WE DO: the КТП's centrepiece: teams bid on correct-or-not sentences

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Rules in one minute: each team starts with 100 play-money; ten sentences go up one by one; teams bid in tens to BUY sentences they believe are CORRECT; a bought correct sentence pays double the bid; a bought faulty one loses the bid — unless the team can FIX it aloud for half back.
  2. The sentence list: four from the review's own Corpus items (ex.4–5 — «I'll go to England this summer», «We are friends since I was six», «This change mustn't be correct», «I've known him for a long time») plus three of the class's own anonymised test errors from the tickets, plus three correct decoys.
  3. Keys for the Corpus items: ex.4 — ex.4 — Corpus choices (Units 10–12): 1 'm going to England · 2 have I met you before? · 3 I might be a bit late tomorrow · 4 I've known him for a long time · ex.5 — ex.5 — Corpus, correct the mistake: 1 mustn't → CAN'T be correct · 2 are friends since → HAVE BEEN friends since · 3 you like it → you'LL like it · 4 You have heard…? → HAVE you heard…?
  4. Kagan Numbered Heads Together inside teams: any member may be called to explain a purchase.
  5. Close the books on the auction with the tally — and the moral, said once: You read hardest when it costs you. Remember the feeling — it's free in tests.
📺 On screen: the ten sentences written on the board before the lesson and uncovered one by one — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: bid, buy, justify, fix for half back. Expect the mustn't sentence to sell high and explode — the best 40 play-money the class will ever lose; expect ticket-sentences to be recognised with groans of ownership. Typical problem: one loud bidder per team — the called-number rule keeps wallets honest

Modals · same meaning
pairs
Why: The review's rewrite task is the block's freshest grammar under exam pressure: same meaning, new modal — the exact transformation shape that separates knowing about modals from owning them.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: five rewrites with the modal given

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 solo, 4 minutes: rewrite each sentence with the bracketed modal, same meaning. Key: ex.3 — rewrite with the modal in brackets (Unit 12, core): 1 It might be broken · 2 It can't be fresh · 3 He could have a cold · 4 He must know your sister · 5 It might be a rat.
  2. Pair check as Kagan RallyCoach — the partner hears the evidence word (Perhaps → might · I'm sure it isn't → can't).
  3. Kagan Showdown, two calls: you say a Russian certainty («точно знает» · «не может быть»), teams write the modal and show on three — the mapping, tested one last time.
📺 On screen: calls by voice, answers on mini-whiteboards — voice and boards are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: rewrite, check by evidence, fire the mapping. Expect item 2 (can't be fresh) to be perfect — Tuesday's test drilled it; expect the Showdown to catch one last mustn't. Typical error: modal + to — bare infinitive, final choral fix

64–74′
The closing cloze
solo → pairs
Why: One text, four choices per gap, two units woven together (food + illness) — the review's own exam-shaped finale, run calmly with reasons named. It is also the honest proof the mixed review worked.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6: the exam-shaped text that mixes food and illness

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Frame it: One text, six gaps, four choices each. It mixes two units on purpose — soup, flu, sore throats and childhood memories. Last task of four units.
  2. Solo, 5 minutes, no talking — then pairs compare and must AGREE on differences, quoting the words around the gap. Key: ex.6 — the exam-shaped cloze «Eating when you're feeling ill» (six gaps, four choices each; B1 Reading Part 5 shape): 1 flavour · 2 spicy · 3 suffering · 4 sore · 5 caught · 6 memories. The text mixes Unit 10 (food) with Unit 11 (illness) — one text, two units, which is exactly what makes it the right closing task.
  3. Whole-class check: for each gap, one pair names the word AND the neighbouring word that chose it (suffering FROM · caught flu · sore throat).
  4. Fast finishers: write gap 7 — invent one more sentence for the text with a four-choice set of their own.
📺 On screen: the cloze is on SB p.75 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: solve alone, agree in pairs, justify by neighbours. Expect suffering/experiencing to be the splitter — the FROM clue settles it; expect pride when the two-unit mixing is handled — name it: this was the hardest task of the day. Typical error: choosing by translation instead of collocation — the neighbour rule answers

74–86′
🎯 Reflection · my revision map
solo → pairs → class
Why: The КТП asks this lesson to end in reflection, and it earns its minutes: every student leaves with a written map of their own weakest topic per unit — which turns a review into a plan.

WHAT WE DO: the КТП's reflection: four lines, one per unit, written and spoken

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The template on the board: U9: I need to revise ___ · U10: ___ · U11: ___ · U12: ___ — each line names a topic, a page or a word list. «Nothing» is not a line.
  2. Four minutes of silent writing — the day's evidence is all around: the crossword poll, the gate rust, the auction losses, the cloze.
  3. Kagan Timed Pair Share, one minute each way: read your map to your partner; the partner may challenge ONE line (Really? You aced the modals…) and suggest a swap.
  4. Poll the room by unit — hands up per box on the honest map: Whose map has U9 on it? U10?… — the class sees its own shape, and you photograph the moment for planning block 7's rewinds.
  5. Maps go home; the homework line is written UNDER the map.
📺 On screen: the map template in chalk on the honest-map boxes — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write four honest lines, defend them, see the class's shape. Expect U9 and the perfect/simple gate to dominate the poll — December is far; that is the finding, and it feeds your February rewinds. Typical problem: «everything is fine» maps — the no-nothing rule plus the partner challenge catch them

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«We already did this» fatigue on the U9–10 tasksThe honest map answers it before it forms: name the month gap, name the reason (retrieval), and keep the old-unit tasks short and competitive — the crossword is a race, not a worksheet.
The auction turns into gambling noiseStructure carries it: fixed budget (100), bids in tens, a sentence is only judged AFTER it is sold, and the judging is the class's job — the noise becomes listening the moment money is on the table.
The cloze runs late in a tired roomIt is deliberately placed after the reset and run solo-then-pairs with only six gaps; if time is truly short, gaps 1–4 in class and 5–6 as the fast-finisher line — never skip the reasons round.
Reflection maps written as «everything is fine»The frame forbids it: one topic PER UNIT, four lines, each line names a page or a word list — the map template on the board has four numbered slots.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
