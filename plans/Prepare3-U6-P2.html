🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Understand the overall meaning of six short city texts, and make a polite request — Could you …? Do you mind if I …? — instead of an order.
What is genuinely hard
Two different things, and both are invisible. In reading, the right answer never repeats the words of the text, so a matching word feels like a right answer and is not. In speaking, a Russian request is a polite imperative and an English one is a question — Give me a ticket is not rude in Russian and is in English.
Typical error to expect
A word from the option was said in the recording → they tick it. A matching word is not a correct answer; meaning decides [exam report]
Give me a ticket instead of Could I have a ticket, please? — a direct imperative where English needs a question. It is not rudeness, it is a missing structure [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The reading comes early, while attention is fresh, because it is the hardest thinking of the lesson: six unconnected texts, six questions, and the answer always paraphrased. The strategy is demonstrated on text 1 by the teacher out loud — the book's own walk-through — and only then do they work alone, because a strategy explained in the abstract is not a strategy. The uncountable nouns sit after the move break because they are a table, not a discovery, and because they belong to the same city vocabulary: traffic, money, information, luggage. The listening is short and it is input for the speaking that follows — they hear the twelve request phrases before they are asked to use them. And the productive stage uses a die, so that nobody can pick the easy combination of place and request; the other pairs guess the place, which is what makes the class listen to somebody else's dialogue.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“In Russian you can ask for a ticket politely and still use the word ‘give’. In English that sounds like an order. Today we learn the twelve phrases that make the difference — and before that, six signs, where the right answer never uses the same words as the sign.”

14–30′
Reading · signs, notices and messages
class → solo → pairs
Why: Six unconnected short texts, six questions about overall meaning. The skill is the one that decides most reading marks in any exam: the right answer says the same thing in different words.

WHAT WE DO: the six short texts on p.66: where you would see each one, then the six questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. Draw a box on the board and write in it Wet paint! What is this? (a notice) · Where would you see it? (on a seat in a park, a door in school) · Why is it there? (to warn you that you can get paint on your clothes).
  2. Write three places from p.64 on the board and ask what notice you might see there — by a river (Do not swim!).
  3. Books open. ex.1 — where would you see each of the six texts? Key: 1 on a website · 2 by a river · 3 in a shop · 4 at a train station · 5 in a café · 6 at school
  4. Two comprehension questions before the exam task, from TB p.129: What time is the first boat trip? (10 am) · Where are Tilly's friends waiting? (at the Silver Fish Café).
  5. Now the strategy, out loud, on text 1 only — you do the thinking and they watch. TB p.129, the strategy demonstrated on text 1: «What three things does this tell a visitor to the safari park?» (when it is open, how much it costs, how to book) — «Look at answer A: does the sign say children must go with an adult?» (no) — «Look at B: does it say the park is closed in some months?» (yes) — «Look at C: are there different prices in summer and winter?» (no) — «So which is correct?» (B).
  6. Read out the tip and put it in one sentence on the board. The tip from the page, and it is the whole task: look for the sentence that says the same thing as the text in different words. The text may say «big towns» where the option says «cities». Never choose an option just because it repeats a word.
  7. ex.2 — the other five questions, alone, 6 minutes, then checked with a partner. Key: 1 B · 2 A · 3 C · 4 A · 5 B · 6 C
  8. Kagan Numbered Heads Together for the class check: the student called must give the answer AND the line that proves it.
📺 On screen: the six texts on screen so the demonstration can be pointed at (Backup: all six are printed on SB p.66 — do the demonstration with the paper book held up and the three options written on the board)
👦 Students do: watch the demonstration, then answer alone and check in pairs. Expect two of the five to be wrong across the class, and expect both to be the option that repeats a word. Typical error: answering from the picture rather than the text

Vocabulary 2 · uncountable nouns
class → pairs
Why: Twelve nouns that behave in English exactly the opposite way to their Russian translations. Learnt as a table with much / many / a lot of, because that is the only place the difference ever shows.

WHAT WE DO: twelve uncountable nouns and the much/many/a lot of pattern: ex.1–3 p.67

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. What music do you like? — collect two answers. Music is uncountable. So we cannot say What musics… Ask for other uncountable nouns they know.
  2. Books open, ex.1 — complete the table. Concept-check two of them as you go (TB p.130): People who work in a company (staff) · It is metal and you buy things with it (a coin). Key: 1 furniture · 2 homework · 3 news · 4 traffic · 5 money · 6 food · 7 electricity · 8 information · 9 wildlife · 10 staff · 11 luggage · 12 jewellery
  3. TB p.130: a few and many are not used with uncountable nouns; much lives mostly in questions and negatives, a lot of in statements. Two lines on the board do the whole job: It hasn't got many big cities. Does it have many big cities? There are a lot of big cities. and There isn't much bread. Do you have much bread? There's a lot of bread.
  4. ex.2 — demonstrate the first item on the board (fruit/orange: How many ___ do you eat a week?) and ask the three questions that decide it: After many, countable or uncountable? (countable) · Which noun is countable? (orange) · Do we change the form? (yes — oranges). Then they complete the twelve. Key: 1 furniture · 2 projects · 3 article · 4 traffic · 5 coin · 6 food · 7 electricity · 8 information · 9 animals · 10 staff · 11 suitcase · 12 jewellery
  5. Books closed for the check: you read out a noun from ex.1, they give the matching one from ex.2. Fast, thirty seconds.
  6. ex.3 in pairs, 3 minutes: TB p.130 ex.3 sends pairs to SB p.137: one student chooses an uncountable noun and the other asks a question about it beginning How many …? — which is the trap, because with an uncountable noun the question has to be rebuilt.
📺 On screen: the table on screen while it is being completed (Backup: the table is printed on SB p.67 and the much/many examples go on the board in chalk — this stage does not need the screen at all)
👦 Students do: complete the table, then the sentences, then the pair task on p.137. Expect news, staff and luggage to be the three that surprise them. Typical error: a homework — the line on the board catches it
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare3_Unit11_Vocabulary2_Uncountable_Nouns_A5
A5 glue-in sheet — COUNT THE ITEM, NOT THE CATEGORY: twelve item → category pairs (suitcase → luggage · coin/note → money · desk/chair → furniture · task → homework · story → news · fact/article → information · necklace/ring → jewellery · shop assistant → staff · car/bus → traffic · meal → food · battery → electricity · animal/plant → wildlife) with the rules NO a/an, NO plural -s, use some / a lot of / much, the unit phrases a piece of luggage · an item of furniture and the crossed-out furnitures ✘ / a good news ✘
  • Glue the sheet in — the item can be counted, the category cannot.
  • Cross out the wrong forms yourself and say the right one aloud each time.
  • Cover the right-hand column and rebuild the categories from the items.
  • Write three sentences with a quantifier: some, a lot of, much.

Listening · six short conversations
solo → pairs
Why: Six requests in six places. Short recordings, one job each — and the phrases they are about to need are heard before they are used.

WHAT WE DO: six requests in six places: ex.1–2 p.67 (track 89)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.130, before the listening: bowling (mime it) and cloakroom (a place where you leave your coat). Then the picture: «Where is the boy?» (at a shop) · «Why do you think he is speaking to the woman?» (he wants to buy something).
  2. In pairs, describe the second picture before anything is played — twenty seconds.
  3. ex.1 — read the six conversations and say which two the pictures show. Key: A – conversation 6 · B – conversation 4
  4. TB p.130, and this sorting is what makes the stage work: first put the twelve phrases into two columns before anybody completes anything. Questions: Can you tell me, Could I, Could you, Do you mind if I, Is it OK if I. Replies: I'd like to, I'm afraid not, I'm sorry but, No problem, Of course, Sure, That's fine.
  5. Play track 89 for ex.2 — listen and check. Key: 1 I'd like to · 2 I'm sorry, but · 3 Could you · 4 No problem · 5 Is it OK if I · 6 I'm afraid not · 7 Do you mind if I · 8 That's fine · 9 Can you tell me · 10 Sure · 11 Could I · 12 Of course
  6. One line of pronunciation that is worth more than a rule: with a polite request the pitch starts high and the intonation rises at the end. Say Could you help me? flat, then rising, and let them hear which one sounds rude.
  7. In pairs, act out two of the six conversations. Two minutes, standing if there is room.
📺 On screen: track 89 in the whiteboard app (Backup with no sound: read the six conversations aloud yourself in two voices — the audioscript is on TB p.296. For this task a live voice is fine, because the point is the phrases, not the accent)
👦 Students do: sort the phrases, listen, check, act out. Expect the sorting into questions and replies to be almost entirely correct — and expect them to still say the questions flat. Typical error: Do you mind if I… answered with yes when they mean no problem; flag it once, do not lecture

62–72′
Speaking · making requests
pairs
Why: Twelve fixed phrases sorted into questions and replies before anybody speaks. Politeness in English is not a tone of voice, it is a structure — and it has to be sorted before it can be said.

WHAT WE DO: controlled practice of the twelve phrases before the productive stage

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with request cards, 4 minutes: one side a situation, the other side a phrase. Quiz your partner. Coach if he's wrong. Trade cards. Find a new partner!
  2. Then Kagan RallyCoach in pairs: A reads a blunt sentence (Open the window. · Tell me the time. · I want a ticket.), B makes it polite in two different ways, then they swap. 4 minutes.
  3. Two minutes of feedback with your ears only: read out three of their politer versions and let the class vote for the politest.
📺 On screen: the twelve phrases on screen in two columns, questions and replies (Backup: the same two columns in chalk on the board, written during the previous stage — they stay up until 84′)
👦 Students do: practise with cards, then rebuild blunt sentences. Expect them to over-use Could you and never reach Is it OK if I — name that and ask for it specifically in the next stage

72–84′
🎯 Speaking out · where am I
pairs → class
Why: The productive outcome: a real request in a real place, built with a die so that nobody can choose the easy combination, and the class guesses the place from the language alone.

WHAT WE DO: the dice task on p.67: a real request in a real place, and the class guesses where

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.130 turns the practice into a game with one die per pair: they write six places numbered 1–6 and six requests numbered 1–6, throw twice — first throw is the place, second is the request — and build the conversation. The book's own example: at the zoo / feeding the lion — «Excuse me, do you mind if I give the lion some ice cream?» «Sure, but our lion only likes banana ice cream.»
  2. Give them the frame in one line on the board: greeting → request → reply → one more sentence. Four lines, no more.
  3. 6 minutes to build and rehearse. Circulate with the correction sheet and log errors silently — no correcting out loud during this stage.
  4. The other pairs listen and guess where the conversation is happening. That is what makes everybody listen to somebody else's dialogue.
  5. Kagan Showdown for the guessing: every pair writes its guess on a mini-whiteboard and everybody reveals at once, so nobody shouts the answer first.
  6. Close with the errors you logged: three sentences on the board, no names, thirty seconds to repair them.
📺 On screen: the two columns of phrases still on the board, plus a die per pair (Backup with no dice: two numbers written on scraps of paper and pulled out of a cup — the point is that they cannot choose the easy combination)
👦 Students do: throw, build, rehearse, perform, guess. Expect the dice to produce two genuinely funny combinations, and expect those to be the ones everybody remembers. Typical error under performance pressure: the intonation goes flat again — that is what the correction sheet is for

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can choose the option that paraphrases a short text and make and answer a polite request in a public place.
  • Subsidiary. Twelve uncountable nouns and the much/many/a lot of pattern (SB p.67); listening to six short conversations for their function; the rising intonation of a request.
  • Personal. In the reading stage, refuse to confirm any answer until the student reads out the line that proves it. That single habit is worth more than the six answers.
By the end, students can…
  • read a short notice and say what it is really telling you
  • choose between much, many and a lot of correctly
  • use twelve uncountable nouns without putting an ‑s on them
  • ask for something politely in a shop, a station or a museum, and answer a request

Structure
Could you …? · Could I …? · Is it OK if I …? · Do you mind if I …? · Can you tell me …? — and the replies: Of course. · Sure. · No problem. · That's fine. · I'm afraid not. · I'm sorry, but …
Form
uncountable nouns take no plural ‑s and no a/an; much in questions and negatives, a lot of in statements; many and a few only with countables.
Pron. watch
a polite request starts high and RISES at the end. Said flat, the same words sound like an order — which is exactly the mistake to hunt for.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
I have ___ homework — much, many or a lot of? → homework is uncountable, and this is a statement, so a lot of homework. Much lives in questions and negatives.
«The news are bad» — what's wrong? → news is one thing in English: the news is bad.
«Give me two tickets» — polite or not? → not, in English. Could I have two tickets, please? The words change, and so does the intonation: it rises.
The sign says «big towns» and the option says «cities». Same answer or different? → the same. The right option almost never repeats the words of the text.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
They choose the option with the same words in itThis is the whole task, so it gets a rule and a ritual: no answer is accepted without the line from the text read aloud. Ask Which words in the text say the same thing? every single time.
informations, advices, furnituresOne line on the board that stays up: these words have no plural. Then tap it instead of explaining. It will need tapping four or five times, and that is normal.
The requests come out flat and sound rudeDo not talk about politeness. Say the same sentence twice — flat, then rising — and ask which one they would answer. They will hear it instantly.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
