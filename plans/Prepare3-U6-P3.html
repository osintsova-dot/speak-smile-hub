🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name nine types of film and join two sentences with who, which or that — a film which makes you think — instead of starting a new sentence every time.
What is genuinely hard
The Russian kotoryy is one word for everything, so English asks a question Russian never asks: person or thing? And it offers what, which looks like the right translation and never is.
Typical error to expect
the film what I saw yesterday — what used as a relative pronoun, a direct carry-over from the Russian relative pronoun (kotoryy), which covers both which and that [high priority, A2/B1]
the boy which lives next door · the film who I saw — the relative pronoun chosen by habit rather than by person/thing [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary is nine labels, and labels are cheap — so the stage is short and goes straight into using them about films the class has actually seen. The reading is the source of the grammar: every example sentence on p.69 is lifted out of the blog they have just read, which means the relative pronoun arrives inside an argument about why films succeed rather than in a table. Discovery and practice are split by the productive middle of the lesson on purpose: the discovery is two minutes of thinking and the practice is twelve minutes of doing, and putting them together kills both. Pronunciation comes late and it is the light stage of the lesson — counting letters and syllables is a puzzle, and it lands after the hardest grammar of the block. The productive stage is the book's own task: design a cinema, which cannot be described without relative clauses.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“In Russian one word does the whole job — which . English has three, and choosing between them takes one question: person or thing? Today you learn nine kinds of film, and by the end you design a cinema and describe it — and you will need which in every second sentence.”

WHAT WE DO: nine types of film: ex.1–2 p.68 (track 90) plus a Quiz-Quiz-Trade mingle

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 — match the nine types to the images, in pairs, 3 minutes. Key: A comedy · B adventure film · C animated film · D action film · E drama · F science-fiction film · G thriller · H musical · I horror film
  2. Play track 90 — check and repeat chorally. Drill the word stress of animated, science-fiction and adventure, because all three are unrecognisable with the stress in the wrong place.

WHAT WE DO: guided discovery from the blog: ex.1–2 p.69

Grammar · practice · relative pronouns
pairs → class
Why: Controlled practice, then sentence-joining, then correcting six sentences — which is where the Russian what gets caught, because it never appears until they produce language themselves.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–5 p.69: gapped text, joining halves, then error correction

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 — books closed first: How are cinemas changing? Collect three ideas. Pre-teach bean bag and hot tub from TB p.134. Books open, complete the five gaps. Key: 1 who · 2 that · 3 which · 4 that · 5 that
  2. ex.4 — the IMAX photo first: What kind of cinema is this? (IMAX) · Have you ever been to one? Then match the six halves and join each pair with who or which. Key: 1 f — IMAX cinemas are popular with people who like 3-D films. · 2 c — The cinemas have special seats which move, and sometimes you'll feel wind or water during the film. · 3 d — It's not possible for children who are under 13 to go in alone. · 4 a — You need to wear special glasses which let you see in 3-D. · 5 e — Some IMAX cinemas have screens which are as tall as four double decker buses – about 16 metres. · 6 b — There's a 3-D film called Bugs! which shows you the world of rainforest insects.
  3. Kagan RallyCoach for the check: A says the joined sentence, B coaches, then swap.
  4. ex.5 — correct the six sentences. This is the heart of the stage. Key: 1 My favourite colour is blue so I bought a mobile phone which/that was blue. · 2 There are two windows which/that don't close very well. · 3 He told me about something interesting which/that happened in the film. · 4 I want to sell a TV which/that is two years old. · 5 It's a music concert which/that is going to be in Sao Paulo. · 6 There are some great books which/that can help you learn English.
  5. Kagan Showdown on the six corrections: everybody writes, everybody reveals at Boards up!
  6. Two questions to finish, and they are worth thirty seconds: Do you think all films will be 3-D in the future? What about 4-D or 5-D?
📺 On screen: the six halves of ex.4 on screen so they can be moved around (Backup: everything is printed on SB p.69 — cut the halves out of a photocopy or simply number them on the board)
👦 Students do: complete, join, coach, correct. Expect ex.5 items 3 and 5 to be the ones nobody sees. Typical error: what instead of which — the crossed-out word on the board catches it silently

Pronunciation · spelling and syllables
class → teams
Why: Letters are not sounds. Counting both numbers for sixteen words breaks the assumption that a long word must be long to say — and that assumption is what makes their reading aloud slow.

WHAT WE DO: letters against syllables: ex.6 p.69 (track 92)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.134, and it is the best thirty seconds of the lesson: write the English town name Loughborough on the board without saying it. «How many letters?» (12) · «How do you pronounce it?» — let them try — /ˈlʌfbrə/ · «How many syllables?» (2).
  2. Books open. Explain the task and play track 92, pausing after each word so they have time to think and write.
  3. Play it again to check. 🎯 Speaking out · design your cinema
groups of four
Why: The productive outcome: a cinema of their own described to the class, which cannot be described without relative clauses — seats which move, a room where you can…

WHAT WE DO: ex.7 p.69 with the task on SB p.137: design a cinema and present it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.134 ex.7 sends small groups to SB p.137 to design their own cinema; one person from each group then presents it to the class.
  2. Give them the frame on the board so the presentation has a shape: Our cinema has seats which … · a room where … · a screen that … · It is for people who … Four sentences, and every one of them needs a relative pronoun.
  3. 7 minutes to design and rehearse. Circulate with the correction sheet and log errors silently — nothing is corrected out loud during this stage.
  4. One person from each group presents. The class votes for the cinema they would actually go to, and has to say why in one sentence — which is one more relative clause each.
  5. Close the loop with the errors you logged: three sentences on the board, no names, thirty seconds to repair.
📺 On screen: the four-sentence frame on the board and SB p.137 open (Backup: the frame is dictated into notebooks — this stage needs no technology at all)
👦 Students do: design, rehearse, present, vote. Expect the frame to double the amount of language produced. Typical error under time pressure: the pronoun disappears and they list features instead — point at the frame, do not interrupt

WB Unit 12 (p.48–51)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can name nine types of film and join two sentences with who, which or that.
  • Subsidiary. Reading a blog for gist and detail (SB p.68 ex.3–4); counting sounds against letters in sixteen words (ex.6 p.69); correcting six sentences in which the relative pronoun is missing.
  • Personal. Say kotoryy once, in the lesson frame, and never again. It is named at 5–8′ precisely so that it can be dropped afterwards — the moment the translation comes back during discovery, the person/thing question stops being asked.
By the end, students can…
  • name nine types of film and say which they like and why
  • choose between who, which and that on the person/thing test
  • join two short sentences into one with a relative pronoun
  • say how many syllables a long English word really has

Structure
This is a question that filmmakers are always asking themselves. · IMAX cinemas are popular with people who like 3-D films. · The cinemas have special seats which move.
Form
who = a person · which = a thing · that = either. The pronoun replaces the subject, so the subject is not repeated: the film which I saw it.
Pron. watch
letters ≠ syllables: chocolate has 9 letters and 2 syllables, photography has 11 and 4, which has 5 and 1.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I know someone ___ won an Oscar» — is the information about a person or a thing? → a person, so who or that.
«The film what I saw yesterday» — what's wrong? → English has no relative what. Use which or that.
«There are two windows which don't close very well» — can we say who here? → no, windows are things.

Assumptions: SB p.68–69, syllabus lesson 36. They can say I like horror films and will produce five of the nine types unaided; animated film, science-fiction film and the comedy/drama pair need work. Relative clauses have been met passively in reading all year and never produced.

the film what I sawExpect it, and greet it without drama: write what on the board, cross it out once, write which / that under it, and leave it there for the rest of the lesson. Tap, do not explain.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
