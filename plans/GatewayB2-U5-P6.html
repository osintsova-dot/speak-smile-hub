🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Retrieve and re-organise the grammar of the whole first term — present tenses and present perfect (Unit 1), narrative tenses and used to (Unit 2), future forms (Unit 3), comparison, articles and ability (Unit 4), the two modal systems (Unit 5) — and the vocabulary fields that go with them, in five station-based tasks.
What is genuinely hard
Nothing here is new, and that is exactly the difficulty: the class believes it knows all of it, and recognition is not retrieval. The lesson has to force production without books, under a clock, in mixed teams — otherwise it becomes a comfortable reading-through of things everyone nods at.
Typical error to expect
The term's five recurring errors, which is what the stations are built around: present perfect vs past simple («I have seen him yesterday»), will for arrangements («I will meet my friend at six» for a fixed plan), double comparison («more happier»), missing articles («she is student»), and mustn't ≠ don't have to.
Why the lesson is built this way
Five stations, one per unit, eight minutes each, mixed teams of four that rotate — so every student works on every unit and the strong ones cannot sit out the units they are weak in. Each station has the same shape: retrieve, produce, check. The output is not a score: it is a personal revision page, three sheets that go home and come back for the mid-year paper.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
No new grammar today and none next lesson. Five stations, five units, eight minutes each — you rotate, you produce, you check. What you leave with is not a mark: it is three pages of your own, listing exactly what you can do and exactly what you can't. Those pages are what you revise from before the paper.

0–5′
Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting at the door, then a one-sentence round on what the term has actually added

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what was the last thing you bought? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — the term in one sentence each. On the board: Name one thing you can say in English now that you couldn't say in September. RallyRobin in pairs, then five answers aloud — and write the five on the board.
  3. Leave those five up all lesson. They are the evidence the class needs on a December morning.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Five stations, five units, eight minutes each. Books closed at every station — the book is the checker, not the source.
📺 On screen: the five answers written on the board. Backup: say the prompt aloud twice; nothing else is needed.
👦 Students do: one sentence each in pairs, then five students report.
board marker
5–13′
Set-up · the revision page, and the teams
desks
Why: Retrieval without a record is entertainment. The three-sheet page is the thing that survives the lesson and goes home.

WHAT WE DO: the personal artefact of the lesson is explained and started before any station opens

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Every student folds three sheets of A4 in half and writes five headings, one per unit: Unit 1 school · Unit 2 travel · Unit 3 the future · Unit 4 people · Unit 5 money.
  2. Explain the rule that makes the page useful: at every station you write TWO things — one sentence you produced correctly, and one thing you got wrong or could not retrieve. Both. Every station.
  3. Form five mixed teams of three or four, by ability, by you, in under a minute. Number the students 1–4 within each team — you will use the numbers.
  4. Show the room: five stations, one per corner and one in the middle, each with its task sheet and its answer key face down.
  5. The key is face down. You check only when the eight minutes are over.
📺 On screen: the five station names written on the board with their positions. Backup: nothing to fail — the stations are paper and the room is the display.
👦 Students do: make their revision page, take a number, and find their first station.
A4 sheets · five station sheets + keys
13–21′
Station 1 · Unit 1 — school, and the two present systems
station corners
Why: The first station sets the discipline for all five: closed books, timed, self-checked, two lines written.

WHAT WE DO: present simple / continuous, present habits with will, present perfect simple and continuous — produced, not reviewed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The sheet has three tasks, all book-closed: (1) six sentences with the verb in brackets — choose the present system and justify it; (2) five prompts for How long …? answered with present perfect continuous; (3) three sentences about a classmate's habits using will (he'll always sit at the back).
  2. One team-mate keeps time; at seven minutes the team turns the key over and checks.
  3. The rule of the station: the team's score is irrelevant. The only obligation is the two lines on each personal page.
  4. You circulate and answer nothing. If a team is stuck, ask which unit was this? What page? and move on.
  5. The error to hunt for on this station: «I have seen him yesterday.»
📺 On screen: the station sheet and the face-down key. Backup: if a sheet gets destroyed, the tasks can be read from SB p.16 (Unit 1 checkpoint) directly.
👦 Students do: produce sentences as a team without books, then check against the key and write two personal lines.
station sheet 1 + key
21–29′
Station 2 · Unit 2 — travel, and telling a story
station corners
Why: Narrative tenses only reveal themselves in a narrative. Sixty seconds of speaking exposes what a gap-fill hides.

WHAT WE DO: past simple, past continuous, past perfect and used to / would, produced in a connected narrative

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The sheet gives six picture prompts or six phrases from Unit 2 and one instruction: tell the story of the worst journey you have ever heard of. Sixty seconds each, and between you you must use all four past forms.
  2. Then a written task: five sentences to correct, all of them narrative-tense errors from real student work.
  3. Check against the key at seven minutes, then the two personal lines.
  4. The error to hunt for here: a story told entirely in the past simple, with nothing in the past perfect and nothing in the past continuous.
📺 On screen: station sheet 2 and its face-down key. Backup: the Unit 2 checkpoint page in the Student's Book carries the same forms.
👦 Students do: tell a timed story in turn, then correct five written sentences as a team.
station sheet 2 + key
29–37′
Station 3 · Unit 3 — the future, and the arrangements trap
station corners
Why: The future is the term's most systematically mis-produced area, and the diary forces the meaning-to-form decision every time.

WHAT WE DO: will, going to, present continuous for arrangements, present simple for timetables, future continuous

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Task 1: a diary page for next week (given on the sheet) — each student says four sentences about it, and the form must match the meaning: an arrangement, a plan, a prediction, a timetable.
  2. Task 2: eight sentences where the wrong future form has been used; the team corrects them and names the reason.
  3. Task 3, thirty seconds each: this time tomorrow, I'll be …
  4. Check, then the two personal lines.
  5. The error to hunt for: «I will meet my friend at six» for something already arranged.
📺 On screen: station sheet 3 and its key. Backup: SB p.42–43 (Unit 3 checkpoint) covers the same ground if a sheet is lost.
👦 Students do: produce four future forms each from a diary, then correct eight sentences with reasons.
station sheet 3 + key
37–45′
Station 4 · Unit 4 — people, comparison, articles, ability
station corners
Why: Three of the term's five recurring errors live on this station, which is why it carries three tasks instead of two.

WHAT WE DO: comparatives and superlatives, articles, ability across time, plus noun suffixes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Task 1 (spoken): compare three people the team all know — three sentences each, at least one superlative, at least one modifier (far, slightly, much).
  2. Task 2 (written): ten gaps for a/an/the/– in a short text.
  3. Task 3 (written, fast): eight base words to turn into nouns — aware · lazy · create · involve · psychology · free · improve · concentrate.
  4. Check, then the two personal lines.
  5. The two errors to hunt for here: «more happier» and «she is student».
📺 On screen: station sheet 4 and its key. Backup: SB p.54 has the same reference material.
👦 Students do: compare aloud, then complete ten article gaps and eight suffix items as a team.
station sheet 4 + key
45–53′
Station 5 · Unit 5 — money and the modals
station corners
Why: Closing the rotation on the freshest unit means the class ends the circuit with a success, which matters at the end of a long revision lesson.

WHAT WE DO: obligation, prohibition, advice, past modals and speculation, in the money field

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Task 1: five situations, each needing a different modal (a museum sign · a bank rule · a friend who overspent · a locked door · a stranger who left money).
  2. Task 2: six sentences to rewrite in the past — needn't have, should have, wasn't allowed to.
  3. Task 3, spoken: one photo on the sheet, three speculations each, one of them in the past.
  4. Check, then the two personal lines. This is the fifth page — every student now has ten lines about themselves.
  5. The error to hunt for: «you mustn't pay in cash» where the meaning is «you don't have to».
📺 On screen: station sheet 5 and its key. Backup: SB p.68 covers the same material.
👦 Students do: produce five modal situations, rewrite six past sentences and speculate about a photo.
station sheet 5 + key
53–63′
Vocabulary blitz · the twenty words nobody could retrieve
teams → whole class
Why: Sampling twenty words across five units in ten minutes finds the gaps that a unit-by-unit review never surfaces.

WHAT WE DO: the vocabulary half of the term, sampled fast and hard

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Numbered Heads Together: twenty definitions read aloud, one at a time, teams confer for eight seconds and you call a number.
  2. Four words from each unit: (1) undergraduate · do well · qualifications · attend. (2) sightseeing · get away · book in · package holiday. (3) departure · delay · in advance · board. (4) resourceful · a live wire · tactless · laziness. (5) afford · receipt · bargain · get by.
  3. Any word that fails in two teams goes on the board in red — those are the words for tonight's revision page.
  4. Last two minutes: everyone copies the red list onto their revision page. That list is more valuable than the ones they got right.
📺 On screen: the red list of failed words on the board. Backup: no equipment beyond a marker.
👦 Students do: confer in fours, answer when their number is called, then copy the failed words.
board · revision pages
63–78′
🎯 Production · the term in one text
desks
Why: A revision lesson that ends in recognition ends in nothing. Eight sentences, eight structures, in one connected personal text, is the proof for the student as much as for the teacher.

WHAT WE DO: everything retrieved today is used once, in a single connected piece of writing

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The task, on the board: Write eight sentences about your first term of Year 10. One with the present perfect. One with the past perfect. One with a future arrangement. One comparison. One article rule you used to get wrong. One with «had to». One with «should have». One speculation about next term.
  2. Twelve minutes, silent, individual. This is not a test and it is not marked — it is the proof that the retrieval worked.
  3. Rally Coach for the last three minutes: partners read each other's eight and tick the ones that clearly do what they were asked to do.
  4. Take three sentences for the board — one from a strong student, one from a mid student, one from a student who rarely volunteers. Choose them while circulating, and ask permission first.
📺 On screen: the eight requirements on the board. Backup: dictate them; eight lines takes two minutes.
👦 Students do: write eight sentences using eight different structures, then check a partner's against the list.
notebooks · board
78–86′
Feedback + the plan for the paper
whole class
Why: Naming the top three errors as a class turns individual failure into a shared task — and telling them exactly what the paper is removes most of the anxiety.

WHAT WE DO: the class sees what it collectively got wrong, and knows exactly what happens next

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board the five error patterns you saw most across the stations. Take a hands-up vote on each: whose page has this one?
  2. Rank them together — the three that most hands went up for are the three we drill next lesson.
  3. Say precisely what the mid-year paper is, because ambiguity is what makes December classes anxious: Next lesson is speaking — cards, pairs, no writing. The lesson after that is the paper: an official Cambridge-format paper, four parts, fifty-five minutes, and everything on it is from these five units.
  4. Nobody leaves without their three revision sheets in their bag. Check that at the door, physically.
📺 On screen: the five error patterns and the two-line plan for the next two lessons on the board. Backup: same two lines in the class chat.
👦 Students do: vote on their own errors and write the top three onto their revision page.
board · revision pages

Wrap-up + homework
desks → door
Why: Self-assessment against the aim they heard at the start, so the lesson closes the loop it opened. Homework comes from their own workbook, not from a photocopy.

WHAT WE DO: self-check against today's aim, then the homework said once and written on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Self-check (60 seconds): Look at your five pages. Which unit are you least sure of? Write the number on the exit slip — that is the station I run again next lesson.
  2. Homework: your own revision pages — add five sentences of your own to each. Plus any Unit 4 checkpoint exercises you left unfinished (Workbook p.38–39), checked against the book in a different colour.
  3. Bring the pages back next lesson. They are the only thing you need.
📺 On screen: the homework on the board in one line. Backup: same in the class chat.
👦 Students do: write a unit number on the slip and leave with three sheets in the bag.

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will have retrieved and reorganised the grammar and vocabulary of Units 1–5, and will have produced a personal revision record of what they can and cannot yet do.
  • Subsidiary. Working in mixed teams against a clock; self-checking against a key; writing a connected text to a structural specification.
  • Personal. Answer no grammar questions at the stations. Redirect to the key or to the unit page — the point of the lesson is that the class retrieves, not that you re-teach.
By the end, students can…
  • choose between the present systems, the narrative tenses and the future forms without the book
  • use comparison, articles and ability structures accurately in production
  • distinguish obligation from prohibition and speculate about the past
  • retrieve forty words from five different vocabulary fields
  • name their own three weakest areas and write them down

Form
no new form is presented today; the lesson samples the five systems of the term in production: present simple / continuous / perfect, narrative tenses with used to and would, five future forms, comparison and articles and ability, and the two modal systems.
Pron. watch
keep the weak forms honest in the spoken stations: /ˈhæftə/ · /ˈɔːtə/ · /wəz ˈeɪbl tə/ · /ˈmʌstəvbɪn/. A term of grammar said in strong forms sounds like a list; said in weak forms it sounds like English.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
Present perfect: «I have seen him yesterday» — is «yesterday» finished? — Yes. So which tense? — Past simple.
Future: It's in my diary and someone else knows — will or present continuous? — Present continuous.
Articles: «She is student» — one of many? — Yes. So? — a student.
ICQ for the stations: Books open or closed? When do you turn the key over? How many lines do you write on your page?

Assumptions: Students have completed Units 1–5 and have their books and notebooks. The station sheets and answer keys are prepared in advance — this lesson does not work if they are improvised in the room.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
A team turns the answer key over after two minutes.Keys go face down and one named student per team is the timekeeper. If a team checks early, they lose the right to the key at the next station and must self-check against the unit page — announce that rule once, at the start.
The strongest student does all five stations for the team.Numbered roles: each station names which number speaks first and which number writes. The number is on their badge or on the desk.
«We've done all this» — the class disengages.The books are closed at every station, which changes «we've done this» into «I can't retrieve this» within ninety seconds. Say nothing; let the first station make the point.
Nobody writes the two personal lines.Check pages at the rotation, physically, in ten seconds per team. A station is not left until both lines are on the page.
Preparing five station sheets is a lot of work.It is one hour once, and the sheets are reusable every year and for every group. Number them and keep them in the folder with the keys.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 19–25, 26–31 and 32–36 — the whole grammar-and-vocabulary block of the first term.

Half a year of language in one room. The revision is built around what the exam actually samples: tense choice in a narrative, future forms, comparison, articles, ability and the modal cluster. If a student can do those six things under time pressure, tasks 19–25 stop being frightening.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
