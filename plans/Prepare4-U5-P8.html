🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Pull back and use everything the first half of the year owns — the vocabulary of Units 1–10 and the grammar from the two present tenses to the present perfect — and then produce it once, alone, on paper, to a known scale.
What is genuinely hard
Not the individual items. What is hard about a half-year paper is the distance: Unit 1 was in September and has not been touched since October. The second hard thing is the arithmetic of it — a teenager who does not know what the paper is worth spends the first ten minutes managing fear instead of answering questions.
Typical error to expect
The four that come back on every paper of this half-year: the dated present perfect (yesterday I have bought), I know her since 2010, there is much traffic, and we was. All four are on or around the paper and all four are the clinic at the end.
Why the lesson is built this way
Three decisions, and all three are arguable, so here is the reasoning. First: the scale goes on the board before the papers are handed out, and the bottom of it is a retake rather than a two. When the rules are known in advance a paper stops being a threat and becomes a task. Second: the paper is the official Cambridge Term 1 Test, whole — seventy-nine self-checkable marks plus the email. It covers Units 1–7 and stops there; the publisher's Term 2 paper was rejected because it opens on Units 8–14 and is full of language nobody has seen. The gap is covered honestly elsewhere: Unit 8 sat in the block 4 test, Units 9–10 sat in the block 5 test LAST LESSON — and station 3 today re-airs them anyway. Nothing home-made is stapled onto a Cambridge paper. Third: the revision runs across both review spreads at once — Review 1 for Units 1–4 and Review 2 for Units 5–8, both already worked in class (lessons 14 and 25), with a third station built by hand for Units 9 and 10, which have no review page of their own. The email (part 11) has no key, so it is teacher-marked over the holidays and returned in January — the self-check covers the seventy-nine keyed marks.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Half a year. Ten units. Today we close it. First twenty-nine minutes: everything comes back — pages thirty and fifty-two, plus the two units the book forgot to give a review page to. Then a break, then one official Cambridge paper: seventy-nine marks plus one email, in forty-five minutes. The scale is on the board now, before you start, and it does not move: seventy-one is a five, forty-three is a three, and under that is not a two — it is a retake in January on your own sections. Then we mark it here, together, and you leave with three lines instead of a number.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts. The rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, the error lists collected at the door, and the bridge into the half-year

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — collect the tickets at the door. Everybody was told at the end of lesson 32 that their five-mistake list from the block test IS the ticket into today. Ask for it as they come in, by name, and look at it — thirty seconds each is enough to know what station 3 has to fix.
  3. Then one round on the board, fast: One thing you have never eaten. One thing you bought last week. One thing you've had for years. Three students, three sentences, no comment.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the lesson: Half a year. Ten units. Today we close it — and the first thing you get is the scale.
📺 On screen: the three prompts in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: hand over their lists and produce one sentence each. Expect about half the group to have the list and half to have lost it — do not spend a minute on that today, note it and move on. Typical error: a dated perfect in the second prompt, which goes straight onto the briefing board

Lesson frame · and the scale
board
Why: The one stage that decides how the other eighty-two minutes feel. The scale goes up before anything else happens, so that every student spends the revision knowing exactly what the paper is worth and exactly what happens if it goes badly.

WHAT WE DO: name the aim, then put the scale up before anything else happens

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Put the scale on the board without a word, before anybody asks:
    Marks out of 79Grade
    71–795 — excellent (90%+)
    59–704 — good (75%+)
    43–583 — pass (54%+)
    below 43not a 2 — a RETAKE in January, on your own weak sections only
  2. Say the bottom line out loud, once, calmly: Under forty-three is not a two. It is a retake in January — and only of the sections you personally failed. The paper is a to-do list, not a verdict.
  3. Name the email separately, once: Part eleven is an email. It has no key, so I mark it — you get it back in January with comments. It is not in your seventy-nine.
  4. Then the shape of the lesson, in twenty seconds: Three short revision stations. A break. Forty-five minutes of paper. Then we mark it here, in this room, together.
  5. Set the bar for the revision half: Every answer comes with the reason. «Past simple» is half an answer. «Past simple because of yesterday» is an answer.
📺 On screen: the scale written in marker on the board, deliberately in chalk and marker and not in anything that can be switched off — it has to stay visible for the next eighty-two minutes, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: sit down and read the scale before any explanation. Expect the first question to be about the bottom line, and that is good: the answer removes half the anxiety in the room in one sentence. Typical problem: somebody works out that they need exactly fifty-nine and announces it — let them, that is the habit you want

Revision station 1 · the words of the half-year
teams
Why: Retrieval practice rather than re-teaching, run across both review spreads at once so that Units 1–4 get pulled back from September. Words that have not been touched for two months are the ones the paper will catch.

WHAT WE DO: Review 1 and Review 2 vocabulary in one run: Units 1–8, orally and then in writing

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open at p.30. Review 1 Vocabulary ex.1 (opposites), oral, ninety seconds, teams on the buzzer. Key: opposite adjectives: 1 miserable–cheerful · 2 polite–rude · 3 confident–shy · 4 careless–careful · 5 friendly–unfriendly.
  2. The wordsearch words called as definitions, not searched — two minutes: the wordsearch: 14 words across age, looks, hair and clothes (teenage, elderly · pretty, good-looking · blonde, curly, straight, bald · casual, smart, unfashionable, trendy, second-hand, brand new).
  3. Now p.52. Review 2 Vocabulary ex.1 — the crime infographic pairs, called across the room: the crime? the criminal? Key: the crime infographic, word pairs into 10 gaps: burglary/burglars · pickpocketing/pickpockets · vandalism/vandals · shoplifters/shoplifting · hacker/hacking.
  4. Compounds fired as halves, all eight: compounds from two boxes: departure gate · boarding pass · public transport · traffic jam · check-in desk · power cut · green spaces · baggage hall.
  5. Written round, two minutes: Kagan Showdown — you say the word, the team writes it, boards up on command. Spelling counts. The traps: receipt · delicious · graffiti · pedestrian crossing · second-hand · boarding pass.
  6. Every misspelling goes into a «watch out» column on the board and stays there until the papers are handed out.
📺 On screen: SB p.30 and p.52 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed; you dictate the Showdown words from the list above and the «watch out» column goes on the board either way
👦 Students do: answer orally in teams, then write on mini-whiteboards. Expect the oral rounds to be nearly perfect and the written round to drop by a third — that gap is the whole reason for the station. Typical errors: grafitti, reciept and pedestrain

Revision station 2 · the grammar of the half-year
pairs → class
Why: Same logic for grammar — and thirty minutes before the paper it shows you, and them, exactly which form still breaks. Everything found here goes straight onto the briefing board.

WHAT WE DO: Review 1 and Review 2 grammar: every tense since September, and the reason each time

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. SB p.30 Review 1 Grammar ex.1 — the conversations, three minutes in pairs, our units' items first. Key: conversations, present and past forms: 1 spend / didn't have · 2 did you stop / didn't make · 4 Are you going / usually go / lost · 6 sent / didn't read / 'm training / Are you coming.
  2. Straight to SB p.52 Review 2 Grammar ex.1 (the nine quantifier choices), three minutes. Key: quantifier choices 1–9 (much crime, a lot of arguments, a little sightseeing, any, a few new friends, many, some amazing graffiti, a lot of traffic, a little vandalism) — no key printed in the book: agree answers as a class, you hold the veto.
  3. One modal minute, books closed: mustn't or don't have to? You ___ pay — it's free. You ___ touch it — it's dangerous. It is part seven of the paper.
  4. Kagan RallyCoach throughout: one pen between two, one writes and the other asks for the REASON. Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together.
  5. One minute of ear-training while the boards are out: I was walking… we were watching… — the weak /wəz/ and /wə/ said fast; then How long have you…? with the have almost gone.
📺 On screen: SB p.30 and p.52 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed; write the keys on the board one item at a time as the class agrees them
👦 Students do: solve in pairs with a spoken reason, then answer by number. Expect the quantifiers to be half wrong on the first pass — which is exactly what the briefing four minutes later is for. Typical errors: much people, we was, and don't must

WHAT WE DO: the two units with no review page, done orally in four minutes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Units 9–10 are the youngest and have no review page of their own (Review 3 covers Units 9–12 and half of it is unseen language) — so the third station is built by hand, entirely oral, entirely on what the block test checked last lesson.
  2. For your own reference, what these two units own: what the two units own: money and shopping words + pay/charge/cost · twelve food adjectives + look/taste/smell + adjective · present perfect (ever/never, been/gone) · present perfect vs past simple · How long? + for/since.
  3. the six dictation lines: 1 I've never worn them — take them back and keep the receipt. · 2 Have you ever paid too much? — Yes, I have. · 3 She's gone to the shops; she's been there since ten o'clock. · 4 The pineapple tastes sweet and juicy. · 5 Raw fish? I've tried it once — it was delicious. · 6 How long have you had this? — For two years.
  4. Kagan Showdown for the six: you dictate, they write, boards up on command, all at once.
  5. Thirty extra seconds on the one that splits the room — history says it is line 3 (gone… been… since).
📺 On screen: the six lines dictated by voice, answers on mini-whiteboards — voice and boards are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write six dictated lines on mini-whiteboards. Expect lines 1–2 to be clean two days after the block test and line 3 to need the extra thirty seconds. Typical error: she's been to the shops for someone still out

26–29′
Test briefing
board
Why: The errors from the stations get named and fixed while the test is still ahead — feedback is only useful when there is still time to use it.

WHAT WE DO: four errors named while there is still time to use them, and the rules of the paper

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Four lines on the board, taken from what you have just heard in the three stations, no names attached. In a normal group they will be: yesterday I have bought · I know her since 2010 · there is much traffic · we was watching.
  2. Thirty seconds each. Ask for the repair from the room, write it underneath, move on. Do not explain — they have all four already; they simply have not used some of them since October.
  3. Then the rules of the paper, in this order: Seventy-nine marks that check themselves, plus one email that I mark. Forty-five minutes. Twelve parts. The listening is part nine — I will stop everybody for it at minute twelve. If you cannot do something, leave it and come back.
  4. And the honesty line about coverage: Units 8–10 have no line on this paper. That gap is covered honestly elsewhere: Unit 8 sat in the block 4 test (lesson 24), Units 9–10 sat in the block 5 test LAST LESSON — and station 3 today re-airs them anyway. Say all of this to the class in one sentence; a class that is told why trusts the paper more.
  5. Point at the scale, which has been on the board for twenty minutes: Nothing there has changed and nothing will.
📺 On screen: the four error lines and the paper rules in chalk under the scale — the board is the medium here and all of it stays up during the paper except the four errors, which are wiped, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair four sentences aloud, then listen to the rules. Expect the room to go quiet at «seventy-nine marks» and to relax again at «you have already been graded on Units eight to ten». Typical problem: somebody asks whether the scale could move if everyone does badly — the answer is no, and saying it now is worth more than saying it later

31–76′
⚡ The half-year paper
solo · silence
Why: Exam conditions, familiar formats, nothing unseen, and a scale that has been on the board for half an hour. Every mark on the paper is Cambridge-made and everything on it has been done in this room since September.

WHAT WE DO: the official Cambridge Term 1 paper: 79 self-checkable marks plus the email, in forty-five minutes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Silent mode, palm down. Names on. Turn them over — go. Timer started and visible.
  2. Answer procedure questions only. Answer no content question at all, however small and however politely asked.
  3. At minute 12, stop everybody for the listening (part 9). The Martina interview, played twice with thirty seconds between the playings. Then straight back to silence. Doing it at minute 12 rather than at the end means the slow writers are not cut off in the middle of it.
  4. Call the time at 20 and at 38: Twenty minutes — you should be around part seven. · Seven minutes left — the email needs five of them.
  5. Pens down on the timer. Papers stay on the desk, face up. Nobody moves and nobody compares anything.
📺 On screen: the listening file PRE-TT-1S_ex.10.mp3 from the course folder, played twice at minute 12 (Backup: the full tapescript is printed inside PRE-TT-1S_AnswerKey.pdf — read it aloud twice at natural speed in two voices; the rest of the paper is on paper and cannot fail)
👦 Students do: work alone in silence for forty-five minutes. Expect parts 1–4 to be fast, part 8 (the cloze) to be the slow one, and part 12 to be quicker than they fear. Typical problem: the email left for last and started at minute 40 — the 38-minute call exists for exactly that

76–84′
Self-check + the three-line error log
solo → board
Why: They mark their own paper against the key and convert their own number on the scale. What leaves the room is not a mark but three written lines — and the holiday homework is built out of those three lines rather than out of a common list.

WHAT WE DO: they mark their own 79, convert on the scale, and write three lines; the emails go to you

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Keys out, one per desk, and read them aloud at the same time, part by part, so nobody gets lost. Ticks and crosses in their own hand, in a different colour if you have one.
  2. Totals added up by the student, then converted against the scale that has been on the board since minute five. Your number, your grade, your arithmetic. Nobody reads it out.
  3. The emails are NOT self-checked: Part eleven comes to me. January, with comments.
  4. The three-line error log, written in the exercise book before any paper leaves the desk: line 1 — my weakest SECTION by name (not «I did badly»); line 2 — one sentence I got wrong, written out CORRECTLY; line 3 — what I revise first in January, in five words.
  5. Walk the room while they write the three lines and read them over shoulders. That is your record of the half-year and it is worth more than the marks column.
  6. ⚠️ Collect the papers only AFTER the three lines are in the exercise book.
📺 On screen: the printed keys, one per desk, read aloud part by part (Backup: if the keys have not been photocopied, read all seventy-nine answers from the teacher's copy slowly, part by part, and have them tick in pencil; the scale is already on the board)
👦 Students do: mark their own paper, convert it, write three lines. Expect relief at the conversion — a raw number is frightening and a scale is not. Typical problem: two students comparing totals out loud; cut it once, lightly, and remind them that the number is private

84–88′
Error clinic · four errors
board
Why: Four errors, ninety seconds each, nobody named. This is the difference between a paper that produced a mark and a paper that produced a repair.

WHAT WE DO: four errors, a minute each, and nobody in this room is named

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The clinic rule, said before the first sentence goes up: Nobody in this room is named — these four sentences belong to all of us.
  2. The four, and all four live on or around the paper they have just marked:
    • Yesterday I have bought it — the block's own version of the paper's biggest trap: a past time phrase forces the past simple — Yesterday I BOUGHT it. The tense chooses itself once you spot the time word.
    • I know her since 2010 — unfinished past → present perfect: I've KNOWN her since 2010. And check the partner error: for + length, since + starting point.
    • There is much traffic — positives love A LOT OF — there's a lot of traffic. Much and many prefer negatives and questions (part 8 of the paper tested exactly this).
    • We was walking home — was/were by subject — WE WERE. The weak /wə/ in fast speech is why the ear misses it; the hand must not.
  3. After each one, one repair from the room out loud and one written into the exercise book. Two of the four should already be in most people's three lines — point that out, because it makes the log feel accurate rather than random.
  4. Close the clinic with the sentence that matters more than the four errors: Everything else that went wrong today is in your three lines and nowhere else. I am not going to say it in front of anybody.
📺 On screen: the four wrong sentences written on the board one at a time, with the repair underneath — chalk is the medium here on purpose, because each one is wiped before the next goes up, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair four errors aloud and copy the four corrections. Expect the quantifier one to produce the most «oh» in the room. Typical problem: a student announces that error two was theirs; thank them and do not build on it — the rule is that nobody is named, including by themselves

WHAT WE DO: the half-year closes on what they can do, and two lines of holiday homework

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

📺 On screen: the three “I can” lines written on the board and students point at them — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Does this one count for a grade?» → yes, and the scale is on the board before you start. Under forty-three is a retake in January on your own sections, not a two in the register. The point of the paper is to fix things, not to record them.
«Yesterday I have bought it.» What decides the tense? → the date: yesterday forces the past simple. A date in the sentence closes the perfect's gate.
«You mustn't come» and «you don't have to come» — same or different? → completely different. The first forbids, the second frees. It is on the paper, part seven.
ICQ before the paper: How many marks check themselves? (seventy-nine) How long? (forty-five minutes) How many times do you hear the listening? (twice) What do you do if you cannot do a question? (leave it, go on, come back) And the email? (I mark it — you get it back in January.)

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«Is this for a grade?» asked before you have finished saying helloIt is the first question of the lesson every single time, so answer it as the second thing you do rather than the tenth. The scale goes up in stage two, before the revision, precisely to take this question off the table.
The revision half runs over and eats the paperThe three stations are seven, seven and four minutes and the timings are tight on purpose. Run them on a visible timer and stop each one mid-sentence if you have to — an unfinished station costs nothing, an unmarked paper costs the lesson.
A student finishes the paper in twenty-five minutesCheck whether the email (part 11) is actually written. If it is, hand over the Term 1 Plus paper's adverb exercise as extension — it does not count towards the seventy-nine and they should be told that.
Somebody is below the threshold and knows it during the markingThis is exactly why the bottom line of the scale is a retake. Go to them, quietly, while the room is still marking: Three lines. That is all I want from you today. January, your sections only. Never let a student sit with the number alone.
The listening file will not playThe full tapescript of the Martina interview is printed inside PRE-TT-1S_AnswerKey.pdf. Read it aloud twice at natural speed, in two voices, exactly as the recording runs. Five marks are not worth losing the paper for.
«Why is there nothing about Units 8, 9 and 10 on the paper?»Somebody sharp will notice. Tell them the truth in one sentence: The Cambridge half-year paper stops at Unit seven; Units eight, nine and ten sat their own official tests in the block weeks — you have already been graded on them. A class that is told this trusts the whole paper more, not less.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the OGE — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: The whole written paper — the half-year paper has the same skeleton as the OGE: vocabulary and grammar in context, a listening, a reading, an email to a friend.

More useful than any single task: the exam habits themselves — a known scale before you start, the listening at a fixed moment, leave-and-return on hard items, and a personal error log instead of a bare score. Students who close the half-year this way sit real exams calmer.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the OGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
