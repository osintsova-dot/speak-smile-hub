🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use twelve cooking verbs (bake, barbecue, bite, boil, burn, freeze, fry, grill, roast, steam, stir, taste) precisely, and read five very short texts — note, sign, email, message — for their one point: Cambridge Reading Part 1.
What is genuinely hard
Russian uses one verb, готовить, where English uses eight, and the differences are not decorative: roast and bake are both in the oven but one takes oil and meat and the other takes flour and no fat; boil and steam differ by whether the food is IN the water or above it. The reading is hard for the opposite reason — the texts are twenty words long and the answer depends on one of them, so students who read fast lose the mark.
Typical error to expect
«cook the bread», «cook the meat in the oven» — Russian uses one verb (готовить) where English uses eight. Bake, roast, fry, grill, boil and steam are not synonyms, and the exam asks for the precise one.
The reading error is structural: the option that repeats a word from the text gets chosen. In question 1 the text says «interviewed last month» and option C says «interview a chef» — same word, wrong answer.
Why the lesson is built this way
The verbs arrive attached to three real dishes and three named cooks, so they are learned as part of a recipe rather than as a list: the class hears Adam, Melissa and Ravi, matches them to the food, and only then puts the twelve verbs on the twelve pictures. The two contrasted pairs (roast/bake, boil/steam) go on the board as a comparison because that is where the precision lives. The reading half then uses the shortest texts in the course, which is a gift: the whole text can be re-read in ten seconds, so the proof rule from lesson 1 becomes cheap to enforce. And every one of the five texts happens to contain a relative clause — which is tomorrow's grammar, met today without comment.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“In Russian there is one word for all of this. In English there are twelve, and the difference between two of them is whether the food touches the water. Today: how three teenagers actually cook — and five texts of twenty words each, where one word decides the answer.”

Move break · mime the verb
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer with every student answering at once

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. You say a verb from the wall, the whole class mimes it — all twelve, fast, no talking.
  2. Then reverse it: one student mimes, and instead of shouting out, the whole class writes the verb on their mini-whiteboard or a scrap of paper and holds it up on Boards up!
  3. Six rounds. Nobody watches; everybody answers. Spelling counts, and this is where freeze and barbecue fall over.
  4. Freeze and sit: Back to the recording. This time you only listen for one chef — yours.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4: the exam task, the word-matching trap named, and the highlighted words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Name the format: Five texts, one question each, three options. The question is always about what the text is FOR.
  2. Question 1 together, and make the trap public: the answer is B (help a new employee), proved by the final request «Could you show him round…?». Then show why C is a trap — the text and the option share the word interview, and share nothing else.

WHAT WE DO: a productive task that cannot be done with the word cook

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Talking points first, four minutes, Kagan RallyRobin in pairs: Why might working in a restaurant be stressful? What are the benefits of cooking your own food? Why do people like going out somewhere to eat? — pairs first, then the floor Give them the background as ammunition: kitchen work is one of the most stressful jobs there is, and the Teacher's Book gives the numbers: chefs work forty-eight to sixty hours a week, kitchens are loud, very hot and genuinely dangerous, and nobody knows what the next order will be. Ingredient prices push wages down and unpaid overtime up. And yet — most chefs say they love looking after guests and find the pressure of service energising rather than draining
  2. Then the task, on the board: Write the method for a dish you can actually make. Five steps, five different verbs from the wall, and you may not use the word cook.
  3. Five minutes, individually, imperatives only — Fry, Stir, Don't let it burn. The recipe register is the grammar practice here.
  4. Kagan Timed Pair Share, 90 seconds each way: read your method WITHOUT naming the dish; the partner guesses it.
  5. Four to the floor, same rule — the class guesses. Take the vote on the one they would most like to eat.
  6. Deferred correction: five errors from your monitoring on the board, unattributed, repaired by the class in the last minute. Expect burn, freeze and the missing -ed.
📺 On screen: the five errors written up anonymously at the end — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail

Structure
Barbecue the chicken pieces, or roast them in a hot oven for 40 minutes. · Don't let the onions burn. · Cook it slowly — don't boil it. · Steam some rice to serve with this dish.
Form
the imperative is the tense of every recipe in English: verb first, no subject (Fry the onion, Stir the stew) · negative imperative for warnings: don't let them burn · -ed is a separate syllable ONLY after t and d: roasted, tasted, but grilled and baked are one.
Pron. watch
the cooking verbs are short and the class will swallow the endings: roast /rəʊst/ against roasted /ˈrəʊstɪd/ — the -ed is a whole syllable after t and d, and only there · barbecue /ˈbɑːbɪkjuː/ has three syllables and the stress on the first · whose and who's sound identical, which is why the spelling has to be seen on the board · and the non-defining clause is heard before it is punctuated: read it on a LOWER pitch and the commas explain themselves
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Roast the potatoes» and «bake the bread» — both in the oven. What is different? → roasting usually uses oil and is for meat and vegetables; baking is bread and cakes, without added fat.
«Boil the rice» or «steam the rice» — in which one does the rice touch the water? → boil. Steam is above it.
The sign says «keep this area clear for orders which are ready to serve». Who is it talking to — customers or staff? → staff. How do you know? → nobody tells a customer where to put orders.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Everything is «cook»The wall is organised by HOW, not alphabetically, so the distinctions are visible from across the room. Every time a student says cook where a precise verb exists, point at the wall and say nothing — they will fix it themselves.
The listening is too fast for the ingredient taskIt is played three times in total across the lesson, and the third time each group only listens for ONE chef. That is the Teacher's Book design and it works — do not shorten it.
Reading Part 1 answered by word-matchingQuestion 1 is done together precisely so the trap is public: the text says interviewed last month, option C says interview a chef, and the words match while the meaning does not. Name it once and refer back to it for the other four.
Weaker students stall on text 4 (the sign)Signs have no sentence to hold on to. Give them the one question that unlocks it: who is allowed to read this — everybody, or only the people who work here?
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
