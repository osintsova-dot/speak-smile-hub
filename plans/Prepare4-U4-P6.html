🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Run the coursebook's own Review 2 spread (Units 5–8) end to end: crime and city vocabulary, be/do/have/make, compounds from two units, quantifiers, past simple with continuous, modals, the two futures — mixed, unlabelled, like a real paper.
What is genuinely hard
Nothing on the spread is new — that is exactly what makes it hard: no unit label tells you WHICH rule a gap is testing. The skill of the day is diagnosis: name the rule first, answer second. Plus the honest wrinkle: the book prints no keys for this spread, so flagged items get settled by argument and the teacher's call.
Typical error to expect
becouse · tomorow · alot · wich — the most frequent misspellings; a misspelt word scores nothing, in a class test and later in the exam
Plus the block's two classics resurfacing unlabelled: mustn't for don't have to, and will for an arrangement.
Why the lesson is built this way
The КТП names the format and this plan delivers it: the spread's tasks in TEAM format, a grammar auction, and reflection. Vocabulary first (three tasks, three units, fifteen minutes) because it warms the team machinery on winnable material. The Hudson story carries the past-tense gaps as a story, not an exercise. The auction sits on the transformations and the futures — the two places where committing to an answer and defending it IS the practice, and where the flagged keys get settled by the best argument. The exam-style cloze is done in quiet pairs near the end, when the room can hold silence. The lesson closes with one true personal sentence per unit — four units turned into speech — and a reflection list each student takes home. Where a key is flagged, the teacher checks the printed TB (p.96–97) after class and confirms next lesson.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“One page, four units, no labels. Nobody will tell you today whether a gap is about quantifiers, modals or tenses — you diagnose it yourselves, in teams, with money on the table. This is exactly what a real exam paper feels like — and after today it feels smaller.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts. The rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a mixed four-unit card round, and the bridge into the spread

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — four-unit warm shots. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with a mixed card set (crimes + city words + collocations + airport words), two trades each — the cards deliberately DON'T say which unit they are from.
  3. Sit them down on a clap: Nobody told you which unit each card was from — and you didn't need it. Hold that thought.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page fifty-two. One spread, four units, no labels.
📺 On screen: the mixed card set from the block reviews — the cards are the medium, so no backup is needed; if the set has gone missing, call definitions and pairs answer chorally
👦 Students do: trade mixed cards round the room. Expect Units 5–6 words to come back slower — say so kindly: three weeks is a long time, and that is today's point

The map of four units
board
Why: Review 2 mixes four units with no labels — exactly like a real paper. Naming the four territories first turns «a page of random tasks» into a map the class can navigate.

WHAT WE DO: name the four territories and the rule of the day

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Draw the map on the board — four boxes: U5 CRIME + past tenses · U6 CITY + amount words · U7 FRIENDS + must/should · U8 TRAVEL + futures.
  2. The rule of the day, said once and pointed at forever: Name the territory FIRST, answer second. Demo on one line of the spread: «We did ___ sightseeing» — territory? Amount words. NOW answer.
  3. Teams of 3–4 are formed here and keep their name and score all lesson.
  4. Honesty up front: This spread has no answer key in your book. Some answers we will argue out — the best argument wins, I referee, and I double-check the teacher's book for next lesson.
📺 On screen: the four-box map in chalk — it stays up all lesson, so the board is the medium and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: meet the map, form teams, hear the rule. Expect the no-key honesty to raise eyebrows — it also raises the quality of the arguing, which is the point

Move break

Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: 90 seconds: four corners, four units

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Four corners of the room get unit names: CRIME corner, CITY corner, FRIENDS corner, TRAVEL corner.
  2. I say a word — you walk to its corner. Burglary! Boarding pass! Make up! Rush hour! Have in common! Six words, fast.
  3. Last word: your favourite of the four topics — go stand in it. Look around. Now sit down.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, the four corners are the medium — nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: walk the words to their territories. Expect an argument about where make up lives — friends, not cosmetics; the argument is free retrieval

Grammar · the Hudson story
pairs → class
Why: A true story carries ten past-tense gaps: narrative context is what the tenses were learnt IN, so the review meets them where they live.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.53 ex.2: Flight 1549 — ten past-tense gaps in a true story + ex.1 quantifiers

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hook first, books closed: January 2009. A plane takes off from New York and hits a flock of birds. Both engines stop. What happens next? Take guesses — then: it landed ON the Hudson river; everyone survived; a photographer on the shore made the pictures the world saw.
  2. ex.2 in pairs: ten gaps, past simple or continuous — the old question: background or event? 5 minutes. Working key: Grammar ex.2 — Flight 1549 story (the Hudson landing: Capt. Sullenberger + photographer Steven Day), past simple/continuous, ten gaps (U5): 1 hit · 2 stopped · 3 was falling · 4 was working · 5 found · 6 was taking · 7 was moving · 8 saw · 9 were standing · 10 appeared
  3. Collect fast by numbers; make gap 3 (was falling) and gap 9 (were standing) justify themselves out loud.
  4. ex.1 (quantifiers, U6) as a quick team sweep — nine choices, 3 minutes, one flagged item argued and settled: Grammar ex.1 — quantifiers, nine items (U6). Working key: 1 much crime · 2 a lot of arguments · 3 a little sightseeing · 4 any (housework question) — ⚠️ item 4's wording is checked in class · 5 a few new friends · 6 many boarding passes · 7 some amazing graffiti · 8 a lot of traffic · 9 a little vandalism
📺 On screen: the story is in every book on p.53 — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: gasp at the story, then choose tenses with reasons. Expect «was working» (the photographer at his desk) to be the surprise background — exactly the U5 lesson, back from the dead. Typical error: the plane fell for gap 3 — background, not event

38–52′
Grammar auction
teams
Why: The transformations and future forms are sold, not assigned: bidding forces every team to COMMIT to an answer and argue for it — engagement does the drilling, and the flagged keys get settled by argument plus teacher's call.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.53 ex.3 transformations + ex.4 futures — sold to the highest bidder

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Auction rules in one minute: every team starts with £100; for each item you read the sentence and open the bidding at £10; the winning team answers — correct earns the pot, wrong loses the bid and the item re-opens. Bankrupt teams earn £10 per correct free answer.
  2. Lot 1–5: ex.3 — rewrite with the word given, 3–4 words (modals, U7): «It isn't necessary to take an umbrella» + HAVE… Working key: Grammar ex.3 — sentence transformations, 3–4 words with the word given (U7 modals). Working key: 1 You don't have to take · 2 Do you think I should split up with… · 3 They didn't have to print · 4 must (only) pick up — ⚠️ settle the exact wording with the class · 5 Should we come round after dinner? — item 4 is flagged: two defensible wordings; best argument wins, teacher referees.
  3. Lot 6–11: ex.4 — going to or present continuous (U8). Working key: Grammar ex.4 — be going to or present continuous (U8): 1 she's going to steal (prediction) · 2 We're getting together on Saturday (arrangement) · 3 I'm going away in the last two weeks of August · 4 you're going to get a fine · 5 My friends aren't coming round this evening · 6 Mum and Dad are going to find out… and there's going to be trouble
  4. Bank the scores on the map; the richest team names the rule that made them rich.
📺 On screen: the auction ledger in chalk: teams, budgets, pots — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: bid, commit, defend, bank. Expect careful small bids on the transformations (they smell the difficulty) and overconfident bids on the futures — both teach. Typical error: 3–4 words means COUNT the words — auctioneer counts aloud

WHAT WE DO: SB p.53 ex.5–6: real learner errors, four units mixed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.5 (choose) solo, 2 minutes, pair check. Working key: Grammar ex.5 (Corpus, choose): 1 going to ask · 2 should · 3 a little · 4 saw
  2. ex.6 (correct the mistake) as Kagan RallyCoach: A fixes 1–2 saying WHY, B coaches; swap for 3–4. Working key: Grammar ex.6 (Corpus, correct): 1 we must → had to (last week!) · 2 when we WERE TRAVELLING to Cambridge · 3 I think I'M going to visit · 4 much money → a lot of money
  3. Name the pattern out loud: All four sentences were written by real students. All four errors were on OUR spread today. This is why we review.
📺 On screen: the sentences are in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: choose, hunt, coach. Expect «we must go» → had to to be instant now (third meeting this block) — say so: that is measurable progress. Typical error: fixing the wrong word in ex.6 item 2 — the tense, not the place name

Exam-style cloze
solo → pairs, silent
Why: The book's own ✓-flagged task: one word per gap, decided from the words around it. Run in pairs as careful reading — the exam name never goes on the board.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.53 ex.7: «How I met my best friend» — one word per gap, quiet and careful

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Reset the room first: Last task of the spread — the quiet one. Silent mode. Palm down; visible timer, 6 minutes.
  2. ex.7 solo: the story of meeting a best friend, six gaps, ONE word each. The working rule from Unit 6, revived on the board: the words around the gap vote — count the votes.
  3. Pair check in a whisper: where you disagree, BOTH candidates go to the class vote.
  4. Settle together. Working key: Grammar ex.7 (exam ✓) — open cloze «How I met my best friend», one word per gap. Working key: 1 it (I find IT hard) · 2 When/As (the morning break started) · 3 herself (introduced HERSELF as Monica) · 4 lot (we had a LOT in common) · 5 few (a FEW minutes' walk) · 6 like/as (we sometimes argue LIKE everyone does) — ⚠️ items 1 and 6 are the ones to double-check against the printed TB — items 1 and 6 are the flagged pair: argue them, take the vote, referee, and mark them for the printed-TB check.
  5. Close the task: Six gaps, four units, no labels — and you just did it. Remember this feeling.
📺 On screen: a visible 6-minute timer (Backup: write the end time in the corner of the board and count the last minute aloud)
👦 Students do: work alone in silence, then whisper-check. Expect gap 4 (a LOT in common) to be caught by Unit 7 memory and gap 2 (When/As) to split the room — both answers defensible, which is a good conversation. Typical error: two words squeezed into one gap

🎯 Speaking out · four units, my life
solo → pairs → class
Why: The productive outcome of the whole block-of-blocks: one true personal sentence per unit territory — retrieval turned into speech, which is the only place vocabulary counts.

WHAT WE DO: one true personal sentence per unit territory — retrieval turned into speech

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Point at the map: Four territories. Give me ONE true sentence about YOUR life in each: a past story with was/were doing… a quantified fact about our town… a friendship sentence with a collocation or a phrasal verb… and a real plan with going to or the continuous.
  2. Three minutes solo: four sentences, notes allowed.
  3. Kagan Timed Pair Share, 90 seconds each way: tell your four; the listener names the territory of each sentence as it lands — that's U6! that's a plan — U8!
  4. Swap partners once (move one seat right) and repeat faster.
  5. Four volunteers give ONE sentence each to the class — one per territory, so the map closes covered.
  6. You log errors silently — they seed the next block's opening clinics.
📺 On screen: the four-box map stays in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write four true sentences, tell them, name territories. Expect the past-story sentence to be the richest (they have real ones) and the quantifier sentence to be the laziest (there is a lot of snow) — push one detail into it. Typical error: I'm go to visit — the skeleton, one last time

Reflection + homework
circle → door
Why: The review closes with each student naming what is solid and what needs one more look — that list, not a mark, is what they carry into the next block.

WHAT WE DO: the personal list, homework, block close

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Reflection on a slip, silent, 2 minutes: Two columns. SOLID: two things from these four units you own. ONE MORE LOOK: two things that need it. The slip goes home WITH the student — it is their revision list, not your data.
  2. Quick voice vote around the circle: each student reads ONE item from either column — no comments, just the round.
  3. Homework, books open: finish any open Unit 7–8 workbook pages (p.28–35) and re-read the Grammar reference p.142–145 with a pencil — tick the solid, circle the shaky, bring the circles next lesson.
  4. Close the block: Four units, one page, no labels — done. Next lesson: a new block, and your marked tests with your five-line fix list.
📺 On screen: the two reflection columns in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write the list, read one line, take it home. Expect ONE MORE LOOK to name quantifiers and the modal split most often — that matches the test data and feeds the next feedback lesson

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Units 5–6 material has faded over three weeksThat is the design, not the problem — spaced retrieval. Let teams struggle for thirty seconds before any hint; the struggle is what re-fixes it.
An ambiguous item stalls the class (the flagged keys)Time-box the argument: two minutes, best case each side, teacher's call, move on. Mark it on the board and confirm from the printed TB next lesson.
Auction teams bet everything on one item and go bankruptHouse rule: the minimum bid is £10 and a bankrupt team earns £10 per correct FREE answer — nobody sits out.
The quiet exam cloze collapses into chat after the auctionPhysical reset first (the move break sits right before the story), then silent mode, palm down, timer visible — the quiet stage is protected by structure, not by shushing.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 20–26 — the Review 2 open cloze («How I met my best friend», one word per gap) is the closest thing in the coursebook to the exam's grammar-in-text format.

A four-unit review is what the real exam paper feels like: nothing is labelled by unit, everything is mixed, and the winning move is naming WHICH rule a gap is testing before answering. Today's team format trains exactly that naming.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the OGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
