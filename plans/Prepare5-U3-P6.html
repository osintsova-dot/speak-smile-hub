🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Complete a gapped text by finding the pronoun or linker that points across each gap (B1 Reading Part 4), and a six-item lexical cloze by asking what each missing word must partner with (B1 Reading Part 5).
What is genuinely hard
Two different tasks with two different methods, and the temptation is to use the same one for both. Part 4 is decided by cohesion — a his, a This, a Meanwhile — and Part 5 by collocation, where all four options mean roughly the same thing and only one of them keeps company with the word next to it.
Typical error to expect
Choosing by topic in Part 4 — the sentence about films fits a text about films, and is still wrong, because nothing in it points back or forward.
Choosing by meaning in Part 5 — save, hold and carry all mean something like keep, but only keep goes with a secret.
Why the lesson is built this way
The papers come back at the start, so the lesson opens with three minutes of real feedback and then changes gear. Each task gets the same treatment: the method named, one item dissected in public, the rest done under a clock, then an autopsy in pairs. The two texts are a gift and it is worth saying so aloud — the gapped text is about a teenager who turned a hobby into films (Unit 5), and the cloze is about friendship (Unit 2), so both fields are already theirs. The productive stage has students building their own cloze item, which is the fastest way to understand what a collocation trap is.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Emily Hagins directed her first full-length film at twelve. Five sentences have been taken out of her story and hidden among eight. Then six gaps where every option means the same thing — and only one of them keeps the right company.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, marked papers back with specific feedback, and the switch into the exam page

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what's the best thing about today so far? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — the papers come back. Hand back both tests with section totals written on them. One specific positive sentence about the class (The health verbs were the strongest section — twenty-four out of twenty-five of you spelled breathe correctly) and one specific instruction for block 4.

  4. If the modal section caused trouble, put one item on the board and settle it in ninety seconds with the two boxes from lesson 3.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): change gear: Papers away. Page one hundred and twenty-four. Two exam tasks today, and both of them are about things you already know — films and friendship.

WHAT WE DO: name the two tasks and put up the two DIFFERENT methods

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

  2. Say the difference in one sentence, because it is the whole lesson: One task is about how sentences hold together. The other is about which words keep company.

Why: Part 4 · Emily's story

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: gist under a clock, then the timeline that makes the gaps solvable

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the scene in one sentence: «Young movie-maker» — Emily Hagins, born in Philadelphia and taken to Austin as a baby; called Movie Girl by her classmates at seven; inspired by the Lord of the Rings films after reading The Hobbit; wrote to the director Peter Jackson, who suggested a contact; started at eleven with short films and a documentary, helped by her father from the advertising world but directing everything herself; wrote and shot the full-length Pathogen at twelve; won a grant from the Austin Film Society as its youngest ever recipient; another full-length film by sixteen, then the crime drama Coin Heist.
  2. ex.1: four minutes, silent, read the whole text with the gaps ignored. One question: what has she done since the age of eleven?
  3. Kagan Think-Pair-Share, ninety seconds: build her timeline in pairs — baby, seven, eleven, twelve, sixteen. Put it on the board.
  4. That timeline is the tool: three of the five gaps are solved by knowing where in the chronology you are.
📺 On screen: the five-point timeline on the board, built by the class. Backup: the text is on the page and needs nothing else
👦 Students do: read against a clock, then build a timeline that will do half the work of the exam task

Part 4 · the task and the pointing words
class → individual → pairs
Why: Part 4 · the task and the pointing words

WHAT WE DO: ex.2–3: gap 1 dissected, then four gaps under a clock

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the exam tip aloud: Read the whole text first. Then read the options. Read the sentence directly BEFORE and directly AFTER each gap — the answer is almost always a pronoun, a linker or a repeated idea. At the end, read the whole text again and check it makes sense.
  2. ex.2 in public, slowly: gap 1 is F. The sentence before it says she was just a baby; the sentence after it says she watched the same films over and over. F mentions a very young age AND an interest in movies, so it joins the two Underline the pointing phrase on the board.
  3. ex.3 individually, six minutes, clock visible. Rule: write the pointing word in the margin next to every answer.
  4. Pairs compare — but they compare the POINTING WORDS first, not the letters. Where the pointing words agree, the letters always do.
  5. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together, and for each answer take the pointing word: C «In his reply» needs a letter to somebody · G «This was due to HIS job» needs the father · A weekends and holidays · H «ever since» plus the example after it.
  6. Then the distractor question: Sentence E is about asking for advice, and the text is about asking for advice. Why is it wrong? Because nothing in it points anywhere.
📺 On screen: the pointing words listed on the board next to the gap numbers — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: work alone with a margin note for every answer, then compare reasons before letters

Move break · point at the word
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that drills cohesion physically

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Eight short sentence pairs are stuck around the walls, each pair joined by one pointing word (this, they, so, however, meanwhile, his, there, that way).
  2. Everyone walks. At each pair they say ONE sentence to a neighbour: «They» points back to the twins.
  3. Ninety seconds of walking, then freeze and take three answers aloud.
  4. Sit them down with the transition: That's task one. Task two is completely different — now it's about which words live together.
📺 On screen: eight sentence pairs on the walls, printed or handwritten in advance — nothing to fail
👦 Students do: walk, read, and name the pointing word aloud to a neighbour eight times

Part 5 · what keeps company with what
class → pairs
Why: Part 5 · what keeps company with what

WHAT WE DO: the exam information, the method, and the first item dissected

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the exam information aloud: Reading Part 5 — a short text with six gaps and four options for each. It tests vocabulary: collocations, the preposition a word takes, and fixed expressions.
  2. Then the tip, which IS the method: Read the whole text first for meaning. Then read the sentence containing each gap carefully and ask what the missing word must go WITH. At the end, read the text again to check.
  3. ex.1: read the short text about friendship, two minutes, one question — why do we need different kinds of friends?
  4. ex.2 in public: item 1. Four options, and all four are nouns about people or relationships. Only friendship makes a compound noun with group. Say the rule aloud: Not what it means. What it goes WITH.
  5. Pairs, ninety seconds: find two more collocations in the text they already know (keep a secret, deal with a problem). Collect them on the board as pairs, not as single words.
📺 On screen: the partner question and the collected collocations on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: read for gist, then watch one item decided by collocation and hunt for two more

Part 5 · the task and the autopsy
individual → pairs → class
Why: Part 5 · the task and the autopsy

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 under a clock, then every rejected option explained

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 individually, five minutes, clock on the board. Rule: write the PARTNER word in the margin next to every answer.
  2. Then the autopsy in RallyCoach pairs, six minutes: for each of the six items, say why the other three options fail. Use the partner test on each one — hold a secret? save a secret? carry a secret?
  3. Check as a class, item by item, partner word first: friendship group · keep a secret · separate FROM · deal WITH · a number of · know for sure.
  4. Item 4 is the one to slow down for, because it is about prepositions rather than meaning: deal with, manage to, challenge somebody to, act on. Write all four on the board.
  5. Close with the count: Twenty-four options, six right. Every wrong one was a real word with the wrong friends.
📺 On screen: the six partner words listed on the board as the check proceeds — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: work alone under time with a margin note, then explain eighteen rejected options in pairs
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE tasks 26–31 (vocabulary in context). A cloze decided by collocation rather than meaning is the same item in both exams.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: after the class check, give three Russian-format items on the board for five minutes. Take the time from the build-your-own stage, which works with one item instead of two.

🎯 Speaking out · build your own cloze item
pairs → class
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · build your own cloze item

WHAT WE DO: students manufacture a collocation trap and test it on another pair

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. In pairs: write ONE sentence with a gap and four options, using vocabulary from Units 5 and 6. Rules on the board: one option is the correct collocation · the other three must MEAN roughly the same but not keep the right company.
  2. Six minutes. Circulate and push hard on the three wrong options — that is where the learning is. Feed them starters if they stall: make / do / take a decision, keep / hold / save a promise, deal / manage / cope with a problem.
  3. Swap with another pair. Answer their item, then explain why each of their three distractors fails.
  4. Take the three best items to the whole class as a Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade round and vote on the most convincing trap.
  5. Close the loop: You have just done the examiner's job. It is harder than it looks, and now you know what they are doing to you.
📺 On screen: the item recipe on the board while they write — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: manufacture an exam item with three working distractors, then solve another pair's and explain it

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Emily showed a strong interest in movies at a very young age.» Which word in it points at the sentence before? → «a very young age» — the text has just said she was a baby.
«keep a secret» — could I say hold a secret? → no. It means the same and it is still wrong. That is collocation.
In Part 5, do I choose the option that fits the meaning? → I choose the one that fits the WORDS next to it.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students choose gapped sentences by topicEvery accepted answer must come with a pointing word said aloud. If they cannot name it, the answer is provisional and stays in pencil.
The cloze is answered by translationThe partner question goes on the board and stays there: What must this word go WITH? In the check, ask for the partner before the letter.
Two tasks in one lesson feels like a marathonThe move break sits exactly between them, and the second task is shorter by design. Say the shape at the start so nobody is surprised.
«Is this the Russian exam?»One honest sentence — these two tasks are the closest match in the whole year — and move on. The detail lives in the note at the foot of this plan, for you.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 10–18 (the long text) and tasks 26–31 (the lexical cloze) — today's two tasks are the closest match to those two Russian sections in the entire year.

Reading Part 4 is a gapped text: five sentences removed, and every one recoverable from a pronoun or a linker in the sentence on either side. That is the same cohesion work the Russian reading section tests. Part 5 is a lexical cloze decided by collocation — keep a secret, know for sure, a number of — which is what tasks 26–31 are made of, item for item.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
