🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Close the group's own named gaps before the final four lessons: the class chooses from a menu built on the year's error logs, works its weakest systems in stations, and walks out knowing exactly what the mock will feel like.
What is genuinely hard
Honesty. A reserve lesson dies when it becomes «a bit of everything»: the hard part is naming ONE weakness per student and spending real minutes on it — retrieval of the year's systems on demand, in mixed order, with no fresh input to hide behind.
Typical error to expect
Katy said she likes reading · Cosmo said the board will be full — the tense left unshifted after a past reporting verb; Russian keeps the original tense, so the interference is systematic [A2/B1]
I watched a famous actor in the street / let's see TV — look (at), see and watch swapped; Russian «смотреть» covers all three [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The syllabus reserves this slot for weather days and group requests — so the plan is a MENU, not a script. The diagnostic opening turns error logs into a visible demand map on the board; the middle is two rounds of choice stations built from that map (the plan carries SIX ready stations covering the year's big systems — run the three the map demands); the teach-back stage makes students explain their own fixed weakness to another pair, because explaining is the deepest retrieval there is. The lesson ends with the mock briefing: next lesson loses its mystery today.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Today I teach what YOU order. Your error logs wrote today's menu. Pick your weakest thing, fix it, then teach it to somebody else — and I'll show you exactly what next week's mock looks like, so nothing about it is scary except the alarm clock.”

0–8′
Opening + demand map
desks → board
Why: Opening + demand map

WHAT WE DO: error logs open, the class writes today's menu

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Meet at the door as always — one word each, in English.
  2. Logs open, 2 minutes solo: find and underline YOUR one weakest line. A gap has a name and an example.
  3. Build the demand map: six station names on the board; each student chalks one tally mark where their gap lives.
  4. Read the map aloud: The class has spoken: today is passive, confusables and tenses. I work for you.
📺 On screen: the map builds itself in chalk tallies — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: name the gap, vote with chalk, watch the menu form. Expect two or three «everything is fine» — their logs answer them

Menu frame

Why: Menu frame

WHAT WE DO: how the rounds work, the bar for the day

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The mechanics in 90 seconds: two 18-minute rounds, three stations from the map, cap of a third of the class per station, switch or stay after round 1.
  2. Every station's exit ticket is the same: one rule said aloud + one example of your own.
  3. Set the bar: Twenty focused minutes beat two hours of «looking through notes». Prove it today.
📺 On screen: the round plan chalked beside the map — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: choose a first station, hear the exit ticket. Typical problem — friends choosing together over gaps: the log check at the station door settles it

12–30′
Stations · round 1
three stations
Why: Stations · round 1

WHAT WE DO: the three demanded systems, worked in short cycles

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Run the three demanded stations from the plan's menu (see the keys card): each has a 3-step cycle — rule recalled aloud → six-item volley (Kagan Showdown or card sort) → two own examples written.
  2. You rotate between stations, 6 minutes each: coach, don't lecture; the materials are the year's own card packs and mini-whiteboards.
  3. Kagan RallyCoach inside every station: the stronger partner coaches item by item, then roles swap.
  4. Exit tickets collected by voice as the round closes.
📺 On screen: card packs and whiteboards on the stations — paper and boards are the media, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: recall, volley, produce. Expect real focus — chosen work beats assigned work; the tickets prove the twenty minutes

Why: A stirrer that also recycles the target language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: 90 seconds: the year in gestures

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. The year in five gestures: quote-hands (reported) · IF-arms (conditional) · shield (passive) · three-finger trio (look/see/watch) · brick hands (collocations). I call, you show — fast.
  2. Now swap stations — or stay and go deeper. Your call.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: flash the year's systems in gesture — the demand map in muscle form — then re-choose

Why: Stations · round 2

WHAT WE DO: switch or deepen; the coach role opens

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Same three stations, same cycle — but round 2 raises the volley speed and swaps the card sets.
  2. Students whose round-1 ticket was clean may re-badge as COACHES at their station — coaching is the deeper retrieval.
  3. You spend the round at the map's biggest station; the smallest runs on RallyCoach autopilot.
  4. Close with tickets again: rule + own example — new example, not round one's.
📺 On screen: the same station materials — paper and boards, no backup needed
👦 Students do: deepen or switch, some step up as coaches. Expect the coach badge to be wanted — status for accuracy is the right economy

50–62′
Teach-back
pairs → fours
Why: Teach-back

WHAT WE DO: explain your fixed gap to another pair — with an example that lands

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pairs prepare 3 minutes: your gap, its rule, ONE example that makes it obvious, one trap to avoid. A mini-lesson of 90 seconds.
  2. Fours: pair teaches pair, then swap. Listeners' job: ask one honest question each.
  3. Two volunteer mini-lessons go public; the class applauds technique, you applaud accuracy.
  4. Name the mechanism once: if you can teach it, you own it. That's why we did this.
📺 On screen: mini-lessons by voice with notebook examples — no backup needed
👦 Students do: teach, question, swap. Expect the traps section to be the best part — students name traps teachers forget. Typical error: examples too clever to be clear — coach for boring and obvious

62–78′
🎯 Speaking carousel
two circles
Why: 🎯 Speaking carousel

WHAT WE DO: fluency kept warm: the year's topics, 90 seconds a face

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two circles, inner and outer, facing. 90 seconds per pairing, then the outer circle steps left. Kagan Timed Pair Share in rotation.
  2. Prompts from the year, one per rotation: a book you'd review in one line · a crime story from Unit 5 · your perfect place to live · a fact that made you say NO WAY · if you could take over any job for a day…
  3. The one rule: no silence — rescue phrases allowed (Well, let me think…).
  4. You orbit and enjoy it; log nothing — this stage keeps May's speaking muscles warm, that is all it owes.
📺 On screen: prompts called by voice between rotations — no backup needed
👦 Students do: talk to five faces in twelve minutes. Expect the carousel to be loud and happy — the year's topics are old friends now

Mock briefing
desks
Why: Mock briefing

WHAT WE DO: next lesson loses its mystery: papers shown, rules named

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Show the printed mock materials physically — reading and listening worksheets, the paired speaking cards: this is next lesson. Not a monster — paper.
  2. Name the shape: exam-format reading + listening in the first half, paired speaking in the second; timing on the visible timer; publisher materials, marked as a diagnosis.
  3. The line, once and calmly: a mock is a diagnosis, not a verdict. It points; it doesn't punish.

📺 On screen: the printed mock papers held up — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: see the papers, hear the shape, exhale. Expect the anxiety to drop visibly when the paper is physically boring — that is the trick of showing it

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«My weakest point is grammar» — is that a named gap? → no: WHICH grammar? A gap has a name and an example.
«If I had more time, I would revise the passive» — do I have the time? → today, yes: that is the point of the reserve.
«The mock is a diagnosis, not a verdict» — does a bad mock mark hurt the year grade? → no: it points, it doesn't punish.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«Everything is fine» students who won't name a gapThe log doesn't lie: open theirs together and read one line back to them. If it is genuinely clean — they staff the teach-back stage as coaches, which is its own stretch.
The demand map is lopsided (everyone votes one station)Cap stations at a third of the class and run the popular one in both rounds — the map serves the class, not the plan.
Reserve energy dips — no new content to carry itShort cycles (18 minutes max), visible timer, and the carousel guarantees a loud stretch before the briefing.
Mock anxiety surfaces earlyThe briefing is the antidote and it is TODAY, not next lesson: papers shown physically, timing named, the diagnosis-not-verdict line said once, calmly.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
