🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Say how you travelled and what happened — eleven ways of travelling and the past simple of regular and irregular verbs, including questions.
What is genuinely hard
Not the ‑ed ending: the irregular verbs, and the moment did appears. The two collapse into Did you went? and I goed at speaking speed. On top of that, one item in the vocabulary list refuses the preposition everything else takes: on foot.
Typical error to expect
I goed to Moscow / I maked two friends instead of went, made — the regular ‑ed ending pasted onto an irregular verb [high priority, A2/B1]. The coursebook itself flags I maked with its «common error» icon on SB p.21 ex.7.
Did you went? / I didn't went — the past kept twice, in did and in the main verb. After did the verb goes back to its base form [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The reading is placed before the grammar because it is the grammar: ex.2 on p.21 asks students to mine the same three texts for every past simple form, so the rule is discovered from a story they already care about rather than announced. Vocabulary comes first because the eleven phrases are what the story is made of, and they are drilled as whole phrases — the preposition is half the word here. Pronunciation sits between the reading and the grammar as a deliberate change of gear: two minutes of noticing, then movement, then fifteen minutes of hard thinking. Subject questions (Who lost his wallet?) come at the very end of the practice block because they contradict everything just learnt about did, and they only make sense once the normal pattern is automatic. During the speaking stage you say nothing about errors: you log them and open the next lesson with them.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Eleven ways to get there — and one story each. By the end of today you can tell me where you went last summer, how you got there and what went wrong. One rule: if you say I goed, the whole class hears it. Irregular verbs are not a rule you learn, they are words you know.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, one line of block 1 recalled and a five-verb past tense check on boards

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. The block 1 rule, in one question: Last time we said: find the meaning, not the word. Give me one example. Take two answers, no more.
  3. Then the switch: five verbs on the board — go · have · lose · put · be. Thirty seconds. Write the past of all five on your board. Reveal together on Boards up!
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the new unit: Books open, page twenty. New unit — holidays. Today: how you got there, and what went wrong.
📺 On screen: the five verbs written on the board before the lesson — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write five past forms on mini-whiteboards and reveal at once. Expect went and had from everybody, lost and put from about half. Typical error: putted — note it, do not explain yet, it comes back at 47′

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three words on the board and nothing else: WHERE · HOW · WHAT HAPPENED. That is the shape of every story today.
  2. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: eleven ways of travelling, and the past simple — which you already know, except for the verbs that refuse to take ‑ed.
  3. Set the bar: By the end of the lesson you can tell me about a real trip in four sentences. Not one word — four sentences.
  4. Name the one trap in advance so it costs nothing later: Everything today is «by» — by bike, by ship, by tram. One thing is not. Find it and you win the lesson.

Lead-in · first holidays

Why: Builds the situation and pre-teaches the one word that would otherwise block the reading. Curiosity first, language second — and the topic is one every teenager has an opinion on.

WHAT WE DO: open p.20: the photos of the eleven ways of travelling plus the About you questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open, page twenty. Look at the photos — eleven ways to get somewhere. Which one is the fastest? Which one would you never take?
  2. Pre-teach the one word that blocks the reading (TB p.37): campsite — an area where people can stay in tents for a holiday.
  3. Kagan Think-Pair-Share on the About you questions: Where do you like going on holiday? How often do you go? Do you always go to the same place? How do you usually get there? Twenty seconds to think, then the partner, then two pairs report.
  4. Ask the framing question of the unit before anybody reads: Have you ever been on holiday without your parents? Would you like to?
📺 On screen: the coursebook page on the screen so a photo can be pointed at for the whole class (Backup: work straight from the paper book and write campsite on the board)
👦 Students do: study the photos and answer in pairs. Expect strong opinions about the scooter and the coach. Typical problem: they answer in Russian — bring them back with the English-first rule

Vocabulary 1 · ways of travelling
pairs → class
Why: Meaning before form: eleven phrases are met on photographs, then drilled in a mingle so they are said out loud many times rather than read once. The preposition is the whole difficulty, so the phrase is learnt whole, never as a bare noun.

WHAT WE DO: eleven ways of travelling: ex.1–2 p.20 (track 18) plus a Quiz-Quiz-Trade mingle

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open, ex.1: match the eleven phrases to photos A–K, in pairs, 4 minutes.
  2. Play track 18 — check and repeat chorally. Key: A by helicopter · B by motorbike · C on foot · D by coach · E by boat · F by plane · G by tram · H by bike · I by ship · J by underground · K by scooter
  3. Ask the question from TB p.37 and let them find it: Which preposition is different? (on foot). Box it on the board.
  4. ex.2 — sort them into air, land and water. Key: Air: by helicopter, by plane (+ by jet) · Land: by bike, by coach, on foot, by motorbike, by scooter, by tram, by underground (+ by ambulance, by bus, by train, by lorry, by van) · Water: by boat, by ship (+ by ferry)
  5. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with picture/phrase cards, 4 minutes, at least five trades each: Quiz your partner. Coach if he's wrong. Trade cards. Find a new partner!
  6. Round it off: Boards up — how do you come to school? Full phrase.
📺 On screen: track 18 in the whiteboard app (Backup if the board is down: read the eleven phrases aloud yourself twice and have the class repeat chorally; the Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards are printed in advance and need no technology at all)
👦 Students do: match, sort and then mingle with cards. Expect 8–9 of 11 unaided; coach is regularly confused with a bus and tram with a train. Typical error: by foot — send them to the box on the board rather than correcting

Reading 1 · three first holidays
solo → pairs
Why: Gist first, then detail — the CELTA order. The text is also the mine the grammar is dug out of in the next block, so the reading is doing double duty.

WHAT WE DO: the three accounts on p.20 (Sophie, Fred, Chris): gist first, then six detail questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the gist task BEFORE they read: Three teenagers, three first holidays. Ninety seconds — where did each of them go, and how did they get there? ex.3.
  2. Key: Sophie went to Athens by plane and then to Milos by ship. · Fred went to London by tram and by coach. · Chris went to a campsite in a forest by bus and on foot.
  3. Then ex.4 — six questions, 5 minutes. The evidence rule of the year: No line from the text — no answer. Finger on the line.
  4. Key: 1 First holidays means people's first holidays without their parents. · 2 They went to the beach to have a swim. · 3 They got sunburnt. · 4 He lost his wallet. · 5 His friends, Jim and Simon, lent him the money. · 6 It was raining.
  5. Kagan RallyCoach to check: one partner answers and points to the line, the other checks and either praises or corrects.
  6. Fast finishers (TB p.38 ex.2): underline every past simple form in the three texts and count them — there are twenty-five.
📺 On screen: the three texts on the screen so one line can be pointed out to everybody at once (Backup: everybody works from the book and you keep a «who found the line» score on the board)
👦 Students do: read against the clock, then check in pairs with the evidence line. Expect question 3 (what happened while they were sleeping) to be answered from the photo rather than from the text — send them back to the line

Why: Eight words whose spelling lies. Ninety seconds of noticing here saves a term of mispronounced island and climb, and it is a reading skill as much as a speaking one.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5 p.20: eight words in which one letter is written but never said (track 20)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Start from a word they own, technique from TB p.37: TB p.37: start from a word they already have — autumn. Ask what season comes after summer, then show that the final n is written but never said. Then the eight words, in pairs, underlining the silent letter. More examples they usually know: often, talk, night.
  2. ex.5 in pairs: read the eight words aloud and underline the silent letter, 2 minutes.
  3. Play track 20 — check and repeat. Key: climb (b) · flight (gh) · guess (u) · half (l) · island (s) · knew (k) · two (w) · where (h)
  4. A ten-second duel to fix it: I say the word — you show me the silent letter with your finger in the air.
📺 On screen: track 20 in the whiteboard app, with the eight words large on the screen (Backup: the eight words in chalk on the board and your own voice instead of the recording — say each word twice, once slowly)
👦 Students do: read the words in pairs and underline. Expect island and half to surprise them. Typical error: they pronounce the b in climb — take it out chorally, three repetitions

WHAT WE DO: 90 seconds of movement and a switch of gear before the grammar block

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. I say a way of travelling — you show me: in the air, hands up. On land, hands down. In the water, hands flat. Eight items, fast: by tram, by ship, by helicopter, on foot, by boat, by plane, by scooter, by coach.
  2. Last one for a laugh: How did you come to school today? Show me!
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required

47–62′
Grammar · discovery
pairs → class
Why: Guided discovery, not presentation: the past simple is not new, so the useful work is sorting regular from irregular themselves and noticing that be behaves differently. What they extract, they remember.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.21: pull every past form out of the texts and sort regular from irregular

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two lines on the board, technique from TB p.38: Give me a regular past simple verb. (walked) — elicit the rule (base form + ‑ed). Now give me one that is NOT like this. (went)
  2. ex.1: nine sentences from the texts, underline the past forms and answer the three questions under them, 6 minutes in pairs.
  3. Key: Regular: closed, travelled, didn't want, dried · Irregular: did (they) go, was, Did (they) have, wasn't, went · The verb that never takes did: be
  4. The third question is the one worth naming out loud: Which verb never has «did» in questions and negatives? (be).
  5. ex.2 — go back to the texts and underline every remaining past form. Check against: got · went · were · got · wanted · went · lay · woke · were · was · arrived · put · was · lost · lent · stayed · had · was · was · was · put · knew · were · came out · had
  6. Build the irregular list on the board as a column students dictate to you, not as something you write for them.
📺 On screen: the two columns «regular | irregular» on the screen, filled in live from students' dictation (Backup: the same two columns in chalk, with students coming out to write their own examples)
👦 Students do: underline, sort and formulate. Expect was/were to be filed as regular by two or three of them — that is exactly the question that fixes it. Typical error: didn't want is missed as a past form because it looks like a present

Grammar · practice
solo → pairs → class
Why: Controlled practice with immediate peer correction, and the error hunt makes them justify the choice — which is what transfers to speaking. Subject questions come last because they are the counter-intuitive bit.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3, then the subject question (ex.4–5), then ex.6–7 against the clock

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 solo, 4 minutes. Kagan RallyCoach to check: Say WHY, not just the answer. Key: 1 Did you go · 2 didn't travel, was · 3 wanted, didn't have · 4 Did the plane arrive · 5 wasn't, enjoyed · 6 Were you
  2. Now the counter-intuitive part, TB p.38: write Paula likes Fred on the board and label subject – verb – object. Then the pair Who did Sophie invite? / Who lost his wallet? — where has the did gone?
  3. ex.4–5 in pairs, 4 minutes. Keys: a Paula · b Fred · 1 a · 2 past simple without «did» · 3 the object · 4 the subject · 1 b · 2 a
  4. ex.6 and ex.7 — the error hunt, 4 minutes. Kagan Showdown: the team writes the corrected sentence on a mini-whiteboard and on Showdown! everyone reveals at once. Keys: 1 did you eat · 2 took · 3 did you go camping · 4 booked · 5 did you go · 6 bought || 1 I made two new friends on my holiday. · 2 Sorry we couldn't meet yesterday. I went shopping and then cycling with my mum. · 3 It was a rainy day on Saturday, so I spent the day at home. · 4 I watched TV and played on the computer yesterday evening. · 5 It was a pity you left the party early last Friday.
  5. Item 1 of ex.7 carries the book's own «common error» icon — I maked. Say so: the publisher put it there because it is the most frequent mistake in the world.
📺 On screen: the ex.7 sentences one at a time on the screen so the whole class repairs the same one (Backup: write the five sentences on the board before the lesson and uncover them one by one with a sheet of paper)
👦 Students do: solve, explain in pairs and reveal the boards. Expect ex.3 to go well and ex.4 to cause a real argument. Typical error: they add did to the subject question — Who did lost his wallet?

🎯 Speaking out · about the photos
pairs, rotating
Why: The productive outcome: free pair speaking on the unit's topic, where the target is not accuracy but interaction — a question back, a reaction, a reason. Errors are logged silently; correcting now would kill the very thing being measured.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6 p.21: a pair discussion about the ways of travelling — with a reason and a question back

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the frame: TB p.38: what is being taught here is INTERACTION — listening to what your partner says, asking them a question back, reacting. A monologue with the partner silent next to you is not the task, however good the English is.
  2. The six prompts go up: 1 Do you like these different ways of travelling? Why? / Why not? 2 Do you think riding a bike is difficult? Why? / Why not? 3 Do you think going in a boat or a ship is enjoyable? Why? / Why not? 4 Now tell your partner which of these ways of travelling you like best.
  3. Kagan Timed RoundRobin in fours: 45 seconds each, no interruptions, and the listener has one job — to ask ONE question back when the 45 seconds end.
  4. Second round, new partners, prompts 5 and 6: 5 How do you like to travel when it's very hot? 6 Do you prefer going on holiday to the sea or in the mountains?
  5. Then ex.8: Ask your partner five questions about a real trip. Past simple only.
  6. Log errors silently on your correction sheet; do not correct aloud — the feedback slot is the start of the next lesson.
📺 On screen: the six prompts and a 45-second timer on the screen (Backup: prompts on the board and you time it on the wall clock, calling «stop» yourself)
👦 Students do: speak in turn against the clock, then ask one question back. Expect the second round to be visibly longer. Typical error: an answer with no reason in it — the listener's question fixes it without you

WB Unit 3 (p.12–15)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can name eleven ways of travelling and tell a short story in the past simple, asking and answering questions about it.
  • Subsidiary. Reading three short accounts for gist and for detail (SB p.20 ex.3–4); guided discovery of the past simple from the same texts (p.21 ex.1–2); eight words with silent letters (p.20 ex.5).
  • Personal. Do not give the rule until students have sorted regular from irregular themselves. During the speaking stage log tense errors silently — delayed feedback opens the next lesson.
By the end, students can…
  • say how they travelled, with the right preposition, including on foot
  • tell what happened in the past simple, regular and irregular
  • ask a past simple question and correct a wrong one
  • talk with a partner about ways of travelling and give a reason

Form

Pron. watch
silent letters: clim(b) · fli(gh)t · g(u)ess · ha(l)f · is(l)and · (k)new · t(w)o · (w)here. And the ‑ed ending is one sound, not a syllable: travelled /ˈtrævld/, closed /kləʊzd/.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Did you went to Moscow?» — what's wrong? → after did the verb goes back to its base form: Did you go to Moscow?
«Who lost his wallet?» — where is the did? → nowhere: the question asks about the subject, so the past simple stands on its own (TB p.38).
«I maked two friends» — the book itself marks this one as a common error. → made. Irregular verbs do not take ‑ed.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
by foot — said by almost everybody at least onceDo not correct it as a mistake, mark it as a curiosity: eleven phrases take by and exactly one takes on. Put on foot in a box of its own on the board and leave it there all lesson.
I goed / Did you went?One hand signal, agreed in advance: you tap the table once and the student says the sentence again. No explanation, no interruption of the story — three taps in a lesson do more than one lecture.
Fast readers finish the reading in ninety seconds and go quietThe TB task is ready: underline every past simple form in the three texts and count them. There are twenty-five (TB p.38 ex.2), so it is a real job, not a filler.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the OGE — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 20–26 (put the given word into the right grammatical form inside a text) — tense forms are the largest topic of that paper, 250 of the 666 items in the national task bank — and speaking task 2, where an answer in the wrong tense scores nothing.

The past simple of irregular verbs is exactly what those items are built on. Nothing extra has to be drilled: today's lesson does it, and the rule «the question was in the past, so the answer is in the past» is the same rule in both rooms.
