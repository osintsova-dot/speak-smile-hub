🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Every student in the room speaks English for at least four minutes today: about their summer, about what they want by May, and — in the last block — for a full minute on their own, comparing two photographs. The language served up for that is the language of talking about yourself and speculating: I spent most of the summer …, What I'd really like to be able to do by May is …, They look as if they're …, Both photos show …, but in the first one ….
What is genuinely hard
The first lesson back is not hard linguistically — it is hard socially. Sixteen-year-olds arrive with a summer of no English behind them, a fear of sounding stupid in front of people they will sit with all year, and, in an exam year, a low-level dread that somebody is about to test them. If the first thirty minutes do not fix all three, the year starts a month late.
Typical error to expect
«I have seen him yesterday» — a finished time expression kills the present perfect. Yesterday, last week, in 2019, when I was ten → past simple, always.
Expect it in the very first activity: I have been to Sochi in July. Do not run a grammar lesson on it today — write the corrected version on the board silently, next to the student's idea, and move on. It comes back properly in lesson 3.
Why the lesson is built this way
Three blocks, in this order and no other. People first (ice-breaker + a contract the class writes itself, not one you impose), then the map (the actual books opened, the ten units named, the papers shown, so the year stops being a fog), then the target (each student writes what they want by May, and it goes in an envelope that is opened in December). Only after all that does anybody perform: the oral baseline is last, when the room is warm, and it is done in pairs simultaneously so that nobody is on stage.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
Welcome back. Three things about this year. One: by May you will be able to hold a five-minute conversation with an adult stranger about something you care about — that is what B2 means, and it is closer than you think. Two: this course is ten units and five exam sections, and I am going to show you all of it in the next fifteen minutes so it stops being a mystery. Three: nothing today is marked. Not one thing. Today we build the map.

0–6′
Opening · at the door, in English
door → desks
Why: The very first English of the year should be spoken, easy and successful. A student who has already said one sentence at the door is far more likely to speak in the room.

WHAT WE DO: meet them individually, one question each, before anybody sits down

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand at the door. Every student answers one question before entering, and any answer in English counts: One sentence — the best thing you did this summer.
  2. If a student answers in Russian, smile, give them the English frame (I went to … / I spent two weeks …) and let them say it again. Nobody gets past the door in Russian, and nobody is embarrassed.
  3. When everyone is in, say the aim of the lesson out loud in one sentence: Today we build the map of the year and everybody speaks. Nothing is marked.
📺 On screen: nothing on screen — this stage is you and the doorway. Backup: none needed; if the corridor is busy, do the same round standing behind the chairs.
👦 Students do: one English sentence each, out loud, before sitting down.

6–16′
Ice-breaker · the summer line-up
open floor
Why: A line-up mixes the group physically in ninety seconds and does what no seating plan can: it breaks the summer cliques before the year sets. Reporting the partner rather than yourself also forces third-person -s on day one.

WHAT WE DO: a whole-class mingle that gets everyone talking to everyone, not to their best friend

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Line-Ups: Line up along that wall in order of how far you travelled this summer — furthest at this end, nearest at that end. You may only speak English to work out where you go. Give them ninety seconds and stay out of it.
  2. When the line is formed, fold it in half so the two ends face each other — that gives you random pairs who would not normally sit together.
  3. RallyRobin in those pairs, two minutes: Three things about your summer. Then swap.
  4. Fold the line again to make new pairs and repeat with a second prompt: One thing you are looking forward to this school year and one thing you are not.
  5. Take four or five reports to the whole class — but report your PARTNER, not yourself: Tell us one thing about the person you just talked to.
📺 On screen: the two prompts written on the board. Backup: say them aloud twice — this stage needs no equipment at all.
👦 Students do: line up without Russian, then talk in two different randomly formed pairs and report on somebody else.
board · space to stand
16–28′
The class contract — written by them
groups of four → whole class
Why: A rule the class wrote is a rule the class enforces. Twelve minutes here saves twelve interventions a month, and the whole activity is done in English, so it is also a language stage.

WHAT WE DO: the group writes the six rules of the room; you only hold the pen

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Fours. You have five minutes. Write the six rules that would make this room a good place to learn English. In English. Real rules, not jokes.
  2. Circulate and feed language, not content: We should …, Everybody has the right to …, Nobody should be laughed at for ….
  3. Numbered Heads Together: number the fours 1–4, call a number, that student reads one rule. Go round until the ideas run out.
  4. Write the agreed six on a poster as they are given, in the students' own words. Add the two non-negotiables yourself and say why in one sentence each: Mistakes out loud are how this room works and English first — you may start in Russian, but the idea only counts in English.
  5. Everyone signs it. Put it on the wall today, not next week.
📺 On screen: the poster being written in front of them. Backup: A3 paper and a marker are all this needs — no display technology at any point.
👦 Students do: write six rules in fours, contribute by number, sign the finished poster.

28–43′
The map of the year — books open, nothing hidden
desks
Why: Uncertainty about the shape of a year is a real source of exam anxiety in grade 10–11. Fifteen minutes of transparency now buys attention for eight months and costs nothing.

WHAT WE DO: walk them through the actual Student's Book and Workbook so the year stops being a fog

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books out. Open the contents page. Count the units. Ten. Read the ten titles round the class, one each.
  2. Put the four blocks of the year on the board while they watch: Term 1 · Units 1–5 — Make the grade (school and higher education) · A job well done (work) · Departure time (transport and travel) · Our strengths (personality) · Financial advice (money). Grammar: present and perfect tenses, gerunds and infinitives, past tenses and used to, future forms, comparatives, articles, the modal cluster.
  3. Term 2 · Units 6–10 — Get fit, stay fit! (health) · Rave reviews (film, fiction and music) · To the rescue! (natural disasters) · Tech talk (technology) · News just in! (the media). Grammar: conditionals and I wish, reported speech, the passive, relative clauses, question tags and indirect questions, mixed conditionals.
  4. Five Exam success sections — after Units 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 — the First for Schools task types, one at a time, with the strategy said out loud before the task. The end of the year — a full practice paper in February and again in May, plus the publisher's End of Year test. Everything is an official Macmillan or Cambridge paper; nothing on this course is home-made.
  5. Show them one unit in detail — turn to Unit 1, pages 6 to 17, and name the seven spreads: vocabulary, reading, grammar, more vocabulary, listening and more grammar, speaking, writing, and then two pages where you check yourself. Every unit in this book is the same shape. Once you know the shape, you always know where you are.
  6. Now the Workbook: contents on page 2, and the Exam trainer on pages 94 to 103 at the back. That section at the back is the whole exam explained, part by part. Your homework tonight starts there.
  7. Finish with the honest sentence about pace: One spread per forty-five minutes. Two spreads a lesson. If we are ever behind, it is because something was worth the extra time, not because we forgot.
📺 On screen: the four blocks of the year written on the board as you speak. Backup: if you prefer to project the contents page, fine — but the board version is the default and needs no equipment.
👦 Students do: count the units, read the titles aloud, find the Exam trainer in their own workbook, copy the four blocks into their notebooks.

  2. Look at the room on each one — that is your class profile, gathered in three minutes without a questionnaire.

46–60′
Targets · what you want by May, in your own words
desks → pairs
Why: A named, specific, written target is the strongest single predictor of effort across an exam year, and sealing it makes the December reopening an event rather than a scold.

WHAT WE DO: each student writes a personal target that will be reopened in December

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board three sentence stems and leave them up: By May I want to be able to … · The thing I find hardest in English is … · One habit I will start this week is …
  2. Four minutes, silent, individual writing. Insist on full sentences and on something specific: «be better at English» is not a target. «Understand a film without subtitles» is.
  3. Timed Pair Share, ninety seconds each way: read your three sentences to your partner, then the partner asks one question about them.
  4. Say what B2 actually is, once, in plain words: B2 means you can follow a discussion between two native speakers, argue a point of view with reasons, and write a clear text of about two hundred words. That is the level of this book, and it is where you will be in May.
  5. Collect the target cards, seal them in an envelope, write December on it and put it somewhere visible. We open this before the holidays.
📺 On screen: the three stems on the board. Backup: dictate them if the board is unusable.
👦 Students do: write three personal sentences, read them aloud to one partner, answer one question about them, hand the card in.

60–85′
🎯 Oral baseline · compare two photographs
pairs, simultaneously
Why: This is the only stage of the lesson with a productive outcome, and it is built so that twelve students produce twelve minutes of speech in ten minutes of clock. A whole-class round would give two speakers and ten spectators.

WHAT WE DO: the publisher's Diagnostic task 13 used as a first, unmarked oral sample from every student

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Frame it before you hand anything out: This is not a test of you. It is a photograph of where your English is in September, so that in May we can look at both photographs. Nobody sees it but me.
  2. Hand out the Diagnostic Test, exercise 13 — two photographs of people doing different activities, with three prompts (What are the people doing in each photo? How are they feeling? Which would you prefer, and why?).
  3. Give the useful language first, on the board, and drill each line once chorally: Both photos show … · In the first one … whereas in the second … · They look as if they're … · I'd much rather … because …
  4. Two minutes' silent planning, notes only — no full sentences allowed on the page.
  5. Timed Pair Share, and this is the point of the stage: everybody speaks at the same time. Ninety seconds A, ninety seconds B, then swap partners and do it again — the second telling is always the better one and it is the one you listen to.
  6. While they speak, circulate with a class list and put one of three marks against each name: fluent but inaccurate / accurate but halting / needs the frames. That is your grouping information for the whole first term.
  7. Finish with three volunteers doing thirty seconds each to the whole class, and applaud all three regardless of quality.
📺 On screen: the four useful-language frames on the board, kept up throughout. Backup: the photographs are on the printed diagnostic sheet, so nothing needs projecting; if the copier failed, use any two contrasting photographs from Student's Book pages 96 and 104 and keep the same prompts.
👦 Students do: plan in note form, then speak for ninety seconds twice, to two different partners, at the same time as everyone else.

Workbook p.102–103
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will have set a personal, specific language target for the year and will have spoken for two ninety-second turns comparing two photographs, using the language of comparison and speculation.
  • Subsidiary. Talking about the summer in past tenses; the classroom English of rules and agreements; orientation in the Student's Book and Workbook.
  • Personal. Say almost nothing between minutes 6 and 16. The measure of a good first lesson is how little of it is your voice.
By the end, students can…
  • say what they want from English by May, in one specific sentence
  • find any section of the Student's Book and the Exam trainer in the Workbook
  • compare two photographs for a minute using four fixed frames
  • speculate about people in a picture with look / seem / as if

Target language
Lexis
spend the summerlook forward tobe able toimproveget better attargetin the foregroundin the backgroundwhereasboth photoson the other handI'd rather
Structure
By May I want to be able to … · The thing I find hardest is … · Both photos show … but in the first one … · They look as if they're enjoying it. · I'd much rather do the first one, because …
Form
be able to after want / will, never can: «I want to can» is the error of the day · look + adjective but look like + noun and look as if + clause · would rather + bare infinitive · whereas joins two halves of one sentence and takes no full stop before it.
Pron. watch
two things only, and both are about being heard. Sentence stress in comparison: in the FIRST photo … in the SECOND photo … — the contrast is carried by the stressed word, and students who flatten it sound unclear even when the grammar is perfect. Fillers that do not sound like panic: well … let me think … so … said slowly, with a falling tone, buy thinking time; eeeeh does not.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
be able to: «I want to be able to watch films without subtitles» — can I do it now? — No. Do I want to do it later? — Yes. Can I say «I want to can»? — Never.
look as if: «They look as if they're cold» — am I sure? — No. Am I guessing from what I can see? — Yes.
would rather: «I'd rather do the first one» — is this about ability or preference? — Preference. What comes after «rather» — «to do» or «do»? — «do».
ICQ before the baseline: How long do you speak? Do you write full sentences in your notes or only words? Does your partner interrupt you?

Assumptions: Students have finished a B1+ course and can talk about their holidays, school and preferences with some hesitation. They have not seen this Student's Book before. Most have heard of the EGE but few could name what is actually on it, and several are not sitting it at all.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Two students already know each other well and do everything together.That is exactly what the line-up prevents: pairs are formed by folding the line, so the pairing is arithmetic, not social. Fold twice and they will not be together in either round.
Somebody says «my English is terrible» in the first ten minutes.Answer it publicly and briefly, once: Everybody in this room is here to move one level. Nobody is here because they are already good. Then move straight on — a long reassurance makes it a bigger subject, not a smaller one.
The contract fills with jokes («no homework»).Take the joke, write it on the poster, and ask the group to write the version of it that could actually be agreed («homework we can finish in twenty minutes»). Negotiating the joke is more useful than banning it.
The oral baseline turns into two people performing.It cannot, if you run it as Timed Pair Share: everybody speaks at the same time, twice, and only three volunteers perform at the very end. Do not be tempted to hear everybody one by one — it is twenty-five minutes of silence for eleven people.
A student refuses to speak at all.Give them the frames on a card and let them read the first two aloud. Reading aloud is not speaking, but it is a voice in the room on day one, and that is what today needs. Note the name and pair them deliberately next lesson.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 39–40 (the oral: the monologue and the picture comparison) and, in the background, the whole paper — because this is the lesson where the group finds out what the paper even is.

Nothing is tested today and nothing is graded. What happens today is that every student in the room says out loud, in English, what they want by May. In two years of teaching exam classes the single best predictor of a good result is not the starting level but whether the student can name the target. The picture-comparison task from the publisher's Diagnostic paper is used here as an oral baseline: it is the same shape as task 40 of the Russian paper, so a recording made today is a fair before-and-after.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
