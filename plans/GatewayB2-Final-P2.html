🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Mark the whole Reading and Use of English paper against the official key, work out where the marks went part by part, and leave with three named, fixable lines written in the student's own handwriting — plus the map that gives every part of the paper its Russian exam address.
What is genuinely hard
Marking is easy; diagnosis is not. A seventeen-year-old reads a wrong answer, thinks careless, and learns nothing. The whole lesson is built to stop that: every wrong item gets a reason from a list of four (I didn't know the word · I ran out of time · I misread the rubric · I knew it and wrote it wrong), and only then does it become a line in the error log. The second difficulty is emotional — a low score on a real Cambridge paper lands hard in May, and the fix is arithmetic said out loud: what the numbers actually mean at B2 in eleventh grade.
Typical error to expect
Right word, wrong spelling in listening Part 2. The ten answers are words taken from the recording unchanged — audience, confidence, strength — and a misspelling scores zero exactly as it does in the Russian paper. Write what you hear, do not improve it.
Why the lesson is built this way
Papers come back unmarked — that is the whole design. The class marks in three blocks, with the key read aloud, and after each block they count and write the number on the front page, so that the shape of the paper appears in front of them: strong here, thin there. Then eight minutes on the three procedural facts that cost marks without touching English, then the error log, then the EGE map built standing out of their own errors, and finally the essay they wrote at home, read against the four Cambridge criteria. Every minute of the lesson has the student's own paper in it.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Your paper is coming back with no marks on it at all. You are going to mark it, because the person who marks a paper learns from it and the person who reads a mark does not. After every block you write your own number on the front. At the end you will have three lines — three things, not thirty — that you can actually fix before September. That page is the most useful thing you will take out of this year.”

Why: Predicting before checking turns marking into a test of self-knowledge, which is the skill this lesson is actually building.

WHAT WE DO: scripts returned unmarked, essays collected, and the one rule about numbers

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Essays in at the door — Workbook p.99, the timed one. Stack them face down on your desk; they are used at the end of the lesson, not now.
  2. Hand the scripts back face down with the answer sheet on top, and let them sit unopened for thirty seconds while you say the rule: Nothing on these papers is marked. You will mark them. And your number is yours — nobody asks, nobody announces.
  3. Kagan Timed Pair Share, thirty seconds each way, papers still closed: Which part do you think went worst, and why? The guess matters — in twenty minutes they will find out whether it was right.
  4. Red pens out. Then, and only then, papers open.

Why: Marking · Parts 1, 2 and 3

WHAT WE DO: the first twenty-four items marked against the key, coded, and counted

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the key for Part 1 at dictation pace, twice: 1 C, 2 D, 3 B, 4 B, 5 A, 6 C, 7 C, 8 D. They mark and code as you go.
  2. Part 2 needs the variants read out, because the key accepts more than one answer in four of the eight: 9 not or nothing · 10 of · 11 if, when or whenever · 12 them, these or there · 13 such · 14 that or which · 15 by · 16 the. Say the rule: if it is not in that list, it is a zero.
  3. Part 3, spelling counts: assistant · celebration · sensible · uncomplicated · pleased · handful · burning · Fortunately. Stop for ten seconds on uncomplicated — a prefix AND a suffix in one item, and the class will have written complicated.
  4. Kagan RallyCoach, four minutes: in pairs, take turns explaining ONE wrong item each to your partner — what the answer is and why. The coach may only ask why?
  5. Block score on the front page: Parts 1–3, out of 24. Written by the student, not by you.
📺 On screen: the key is read aloud, never displayed, so that everybody marks at the same pace and nobody runs ahead to their weak part
👦 Students do: mark twenty-four items, code every wrong one, explain one to a partner, and write their own block score

Marking · Parts 4 and 5
individual → teams of four
Why: Marking · Parts 4 and 5

WHAT WE DO: the transformations and the novel extract — the two parts where the reasons for errors differ most

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Part 4 first, and read each answer as a whole sentence, not as a fragment: 25 if she knew where his · 26 wish I could go diving · 27 insisted on speaking · 28 did not or didn't turn up · 29 was such a great holiday · 30 a waste of time.
  2. Then the counting rule, because it decides more marks than the grammar does: Two to five words, including the word given. Count your words now — anybody with six has lost the mark even if the English is perfect.
  3. Part 5: 31 D, 32 A, 33 A, 34 B, 35 C, 36 A. Then Kagan Numbered Heads Together on two of them: teams find the line in the text that proves 33 and 35, and you call a number.
  4. Name the trap of Part 5 out loud: two of the six questions ask what the writer is DOING (describing, explaining, admitting), not what he says. Students who read for facts lose exactly those two.
  5. Block score on the front page: Parts 4–5, out of 12.
📺 On screen: nothing on display — the key spoken, the proof lines found in the students' own copies of the text
👦 Students do: mark twelve items, count the words in every Part 4 answer, and find the proof line for two Part 5 questions

Why: A stirrer that also recycles language. Seventeen-year-olds who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, and in an examination week that is more true, not less.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that makes every corrected item leave the page and be spoken as a sentence

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Stand Up–Hand Up–Pair Up: everybody stands with their script, hand in the air, and finds a partner.
  2. The task each time: read ONE item you got wrong as a complete correct sentence, then listen to your partner's. Not the answer alone — the whole sentence.
  3. Three partners, forty seconds each, then freeze.
  4. One round to the floor: two sentences said aloud to the whole room, chosen by you from what you heard while walking.
  5. Sit down in teams of four for the second half — the room changes shape here, and it should.
📺 On screen: no display at all — voices, scripts and movement
👦 Students do: say three of their own corrections aloud as full sentences to three different partners

Why: Marking · Parts 6 and 7

WHAT WE DO: the last sixteen items, marked in teams, with the technique named for each part

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Part 6: 37 F, 38 B, 39 E, 40 G, 41 C, 42 A. Then two minutes on the technique, because Part 6 is the most learnable part of the paper: underline the reference word in every option — it, they, this, there, the other one — and match backwards.
  2. Take the option that nobody used (the extra one) and ask what made it attractive. Two minutes, teams, then one answer.
  3. Part 7: 43 A, 44 C, 45 A, 46 D, 47 C, 48 B, 49 C, 50 B, 51 D, 52 A. Ten marks, and in most groups the fastest ten on the paper — unless they were reached with four minutes left.
  4. Ask the honest question and count the hands: Who reached Part 7 with less than ten minutes left? Write the number on the board next to the number from the end of last lesson. That is the timing problem, in public, without naming anybody.
  5. Final block score, then the total on the front page, out of 52. Nobody says it aloud.
📺 On screen: the key spoken as before; the only thing written on the board is the count of hands for the timing question
👦 Students do: mark sixteen items in teams, name the technique for Part 6, and write their own total out of 52

The three facts that cost marks without touching English
whole class
Why: The three facts that cost marks without touching English

WHAT WE DO: capitals, the missing transfer time and the eighty-five-second arithmetic, taken from their own scripts

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Fact one, and check it on their own sheets as you say it: Parts 2, 3 and 4 are written IN CAPITAL LETTERS. Hands up who did not. Nobody lost a mark for it today because I marked generously; in the hall they would.
  2. Fact two: this paper has no transfer time. Ask who was still copying answers in the last two minutes and let the room see the hands.
  3. Fact three, on the board: 52 marks ÷ 75 minutes = 85 seconds a mark. Then the honest distribution: Parts 1–4 in thirty minutes, Parts 5–7 in forty, five to check. Compare it with what actually happened.
  4. Kagan Showdown, two minutes, mini-whiteboards: How many minutes for Part 2? For Part 7? What do you do when you cannot do question 19? All boards up together.
  5. Close it in one sentence: Not one word of this is English. All of it is marks.
📺 On screen: the arithmetic written on the board and left up for the rest of the lesson
👦 Students do: check their own sheets against three procedural facts and answer three timing questions on mini-whiteboards

🎯 The error log · three lines, in your handwriting
individual → pairs
Why: 🎯 The error log · three lines, in your handwriting

WHAT WE DO: every K and W item turned into three specific, fixable lines on one page

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Clean page, and the heading they will actually find again: What I fix before September.
  2. Step one, four minutes, individually: go through the script and list ONLY the items coded K (didn't know it) and W (knew it, wrote it wrong). Ignore T and R — those are the timing problem and it is already on the board.
  3. Step two, three minutes: group them. Almost every list collapses into three families — a grammar line (prepositions, the passive, reported speech), a vocabulary line (collocations with make/do/take), and a procedure line (capitals, word count, rubric).
  4. Step three, three minutes: write THREE sentences, each with a concrete action. Not improve my vocabulary but learn the twelve prepositions after verbs from Unit 8 — Workbook p.99, ten minutes a week.
  5. Step four, two minutes: read your three lines to a partner. If your partner cannot picture you doing it on a Tuesday, rewrite it.
  6. Photograph the page before it leaves the room. It is the revision plan for the summer and it took twelve minutes to build.
📺 On screen: the three-family framework written on the board as an example only — the content of every log is the student's own paper
👦 Students do: list their own K and W items, group them, and write three concrete actions checked by a partner
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE tasks 19–31 (grammar, word formation and lexis). Every item in a Russian written paper is produced, not chosen, and the K and W items on these scripts are the same knowledge in Cambridge clothing. A log built from a real paper is worth more than any list of topics, because the student has evidence that they lost the mark.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: when the three lines are written, spend four minutes putting two of them into Russian-exam form — a gapped sentence with the verb in capitals — and let the pairs solve each other's. Take the time from the EGE map stage, which works with eight cards instead of eleven.

The EGE map · built standing, out of their own errors
standing, whole class
Why: The exam link is honest here and only here: the students have just marked the evidence, so the map attaches to something real instead of being an announcement.

WHAT WE DO: every part of the Cambridge paper given its Russian exam address, by the students, not by you

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Eleven cards on the board, one per line of the map, face down. Each card names a part of the paper.
  2. Kagan Stand Up–Hand Up–Pair Up: a pair takes a card, decides which Russian task it corresponds to and why, and writes the number on it. Ninety seconds per card, two rounds.
  3. Then the map is assembled on the board in front of them: Part 1 → tasks 32–38 · Part 2 → 19–25 · Part 3 → 26–31 · Parts 5, 6 and 7 → 10–18 · Listening → 1–9 · the essay → task 38.
  4. Say the honest line about Part 4: This one has no Russian equivalent at all. It is pure grammar and it feeds everything else. Students trust a map that admits a gap.
  5. Photograph the board and put it in the class chat tonight. For the students who are sitting the Russian exam it is a summer revision plan; for the others it costs four minutes and explains what the year was for.

The essay you wrote at home, against the real criteria
pairs
Why: The essay you wrote at home, against the real criteria

WHAT WE DO: the timed Workbook essay read against the four Cambridge criteria and the printed model

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand the essays back. Four criteria on the board, in the exam's own words: Content — did you do what the task asked? Communicative achievement — is the style right? Organisation — is the order logical? Language — is there range as well as accuracy?
  2. Two minutes, individually: read your own essay and answer only the first criterion. Did you write about BOTH given notes AND one idea of your own? Anybody who missed the third point has just found the most expensive mistake in Part 1.
  3. Two minutes, in pairs: swap and check Organisation only — introduction, one idea per paragraph, conclusion that agrees with the body.
  4. Two minutes: read one paragraph of the Cambridge model essay from the key aloud and let the class name two things it does that their own paragraph did not. Do not admire it — dissect it.
  5. Essays go home with the three-line log clipped to them.
📺 On screen: the four criteria written on the board in the exam's own words; the model essay is read aloud from the key, not displayed
👦 Students do: check their own essay against Content, a partner's against Organisation, and compare one paragraph with the model

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

Workbook p.100–101
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students have marked their own Reading and Use of English paper against the official key, produced a part-by-part score profile and written an error log of three fixable lines.
  • Subsidiary. Categorising an error by cause; reading a marking criterion and applying it to a real text; explaining a correction to a partner.
  • Personal. Do not announce anybody's score, ever, and stop the first student who asks another what they got. The number is between the student and the paper. What is public in this lesson is the shape of the errors, never the totals.
By the end, students can…
  • mark an exam paper accurately against an official key
  • say which part of the paper cost them the most marks, with the number
  • give a cause for a wrong answer rather than calling it careless
  • name three specific things to fix, in writing, before September

Structure
the language of talking about your own performance: I lost four marks in Part 2, and three of them were prepositions · I knew this one and I wrote it in lower case · I ran out of time before Part 7
Form
the grammar of the items that were wrong — whatever those turn out to be. In most groups Part 2 produces prepositions and relative pronouns, Part 3 produces negative prefixes and plurals, and Part 4 produces reported speech and the passive.
Pron. watch
when a corrected item is read aloud it is read as a whole sentence, never as a bare word — She asked if she knew where his phone was, not if-she-knew-where-his. Corrections that are never spoken in a sentence do not survive the summer.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
You wrote «SUCH» where the key says «such». Mark? → full mark. The rubric asks for capitals in Parts 2, 3 and 4 — capitals are never wrong there.
The key gives «not/nothing» for question 9. You wrote «no». Mark? → zero. The key lists every accepted answer, and a near miss is a miss.
Your Part 4 answer is «if she knew where his phone». Mark? → zero: six words where the maximum is five, and the extra word is already printed in the sentence.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students compare scores out loud and somebody's afternoon is ruinedSay the rule in the first minute, before the papers are given out: Your number is yours. Nobody asks anybody else, and nobody announces one. I will stop this once and then I will stop being polite about it. Then hold it.
Marking becomes an argument about whether an answer «basically» countsThe key lists every accepted variant, so the rule is short: if it is not in the key, it is a zero, and there is no discussion. It feels harsh in a classroom and it is exactly what the real marker does.
«I was just careless» — the answer that ends all learningThat is why every wrong item gets a code, not a shrug: K (didn't know it), T (ran out of time), R (misread the rubric) or W (knew it, wrote it wrong). Four letters, written next to the item. The error log at 58–70 is built only from the K and W items.
A weak paper produces a student who stops trying in the last two lessonsGive the arithmetic out loud, to the whole room, and mean it: «these are marks on a Cambridge B2 paper sat by eleventh-graders in May, months before anybody would enter for it; the useful number is not the score, it is which part is thin». Then show one profile — yours, or an anonymous one — where a low total hides two strong parts.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Everything. Each part of the Cambridge paper gets its Russian address on the board today: Part 1 next to tasks 32–38, Part 2 next to 19–25, Part 3 next to 26–31, Parts 5–7 next to 10–18, and the essay next to task 38.

The review lesson is where the year's exam work is finally made explicit, because now there is evidence on the desk to attach it to. Students who are not sitting the Russian exam lose nothing by hearing the map; those who are get something they can revise from over the summer, built out of their own marked paper rather than out of somebody's list. The map is built by the students, from their own errors, standing up — a lecture on the same content would be forgotten by Friday.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
