🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Why (for the teacher): The Real-world speaking and the writing of this unit are the same task in two channels: agreeing on a time when both people are busy. In the video Sam turns Maria down THREE times and still meets her at 2:30 — that is the model. The mission constraint (“at least two failed attempts”) comes straight from a TB tip. The writing continues the same line: an invitation, a refusal with a reason, a second invitation, an acceptance — plus the apostrophe subskill, which is checked right there, in somebody else's text.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Hardest skill in this language: saying no to a friend without losing them. Watch Sam. Maria wants a bike ride. Saturday morning? Dentist. Saturday afternoon? Barbecue for his exam results. And Sunday morning SHE plays tennis. Three doors shut in ninety seconds — and they still meet at the park at half past two. Today your pair does the same, twice: once out loud, once in writing. Two failed attempts minimum, or it's too easy. Find the window.”
The gist, if you need to restate it: the hardest thing in this language is saying no to a friend without losing them. In the video Sam turns Maria down three times and they still meet at 2:30. Today every pair does the same, twice: out loud first, then in writing. At least two failed attempts — otherwise it is too easy.

WHAT WE DO: the mission: find a shared window in spite of two busy weeks — out loud and in writing

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Sam says no three times. And they still meet at half past two.
  2. Your pair does it twice: out loud, then in writing. Two failed attempts minimum — no instant yes.
  3. 🏆 The bar for the product (judges' rubric): Fluency — pauses ≤3 sec (1–3) · Accuracy — clean forms, no “will to go” and no “we meeting at 8” (1–3) · Richness — details, times, places, numbers (1–3). 9/9 = gold run, +5.
📺 On screen: the mission slide + the three photos from p.103 (the bike ride · the barbecue · the tennis) (Backup: draw three icons on the board and label them: bike ride · barbecue · tennis)
👦 Students do: they take on the constraint. Expect: “can we just say yes?” — no, and that is what makes the task interesting

🎯 GATE · say no politely
pairs · standing

WHAT WE DO: 60 seconds: refuse five times in a row in different ways and offer another time every time

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. I invite. You refuse — politely — and offer another time. Different words every round. Five rounds, sixty seconds.
  2. Your five invitations: Let's go for a pizza tonight! · How about the skate park tomorrow? · Are you free on Saturday morning? · Do you fancy coming to a concert? · Why don't we go for a bike ride on Sunday?
  3. The score stays private: how many DIFFERENT ways of refusing they managed. Keep the number. Rematch at the end.
📺 On screen: timer · the slide with the invitations
👦 Students do: they refuse standing, in pairs, against the clock. Expect: 1–2 ways for most of them (“sorry, I can't” and that's it) — exactly the gap this pair closes

🔥 Challenge · find the window
pairs → crews

WHAT WE DO: Skills boost ex.5: their own week diary → a dialogue with two failed attempts → the run and the swap of parts

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. THINK: Make a diary of one week. Write activities in it. Leave TWO days free — and put one morning and one afternoon activity in each busy day (TB tip).
  2. PREPARE: Choose one activity you BOTH want to do. Then build in two attempts that fail before you find the window (TB tip).
  3. The refusal formula on the board: Sorry + I can't + reason + another time.
  4. PRACTISE: Run it twice, then swap parts — you must be able to play BOTH sides (TB tip). Kagan RallyRobin on the Key phrases before the run.
  5. The constraint: Fewer than five Key phrases — the run doesn't count.
📺 On screen: SB p.103 Skills boost · the Key phrase cards · timer
👦 Students do: they build the diaries and rehearse. Expect: the two failed attempts make the dialogue natural — without the constraint the conversation collapses into three lines

WHAT WE DO: pp.104–105: three chats, the phrase table, apostrophes — and the draft of their own

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 warm-up on the emojis (key, TB: 1 camping · 2 bike · 3 Bye! · 4 happy · 5 a party · 6 sad). TB Culture note: emojis were invented in Japan in the 1990s, they have been in the Oxford Dictionary since 2013, and there are around 3000 of them now.
  2. ex.2–3 quickly (key, TB ex.3: 1 He's leaving on Friday around six. · 2 It's her sister's birthday and they're having a party. · 3 He's meeting her at 11 o'clock. · 4 They're meeting in the park. · 5 He's going to his grandparents' house for lunch.). TB support for anyone who gets lost: write out the three names — Ian, Jake, Emma — with camping/biking next to them and tick as they find things.
  3. ex.4 the phrase table (key, TB: Inviting — Do you fancy coming? Would you like to come? · Yes — I'd love to. Great! Sounds fun! · No — Sorry, I can't. Sorry, I'm busy …). TB routine: you invite a student, they answer and invite the next one — a chain round the class. That is Kagan RoundRobin: the chain goes strictly round the circle, everyone has to both answer and invite, so both halves of the phrase table pass through every mouth.
  4. The apostrophes subskill: ex.6 the three types in someone else's text (key, TB: a my sister's birthday · b my grandparents' house · c I'm, I'd, We're, can't, I'm free…) → ex.7 putting them in (key, TB: 1 It's, I'm · 2 I'd, I've · 3 can't, We're, Paul's · 4 parents', they're). ex.7 runs in pairs as Kagan RallyCoach: the first puts the apostrophes in out loud and says why they go there (contraction? possession? one person or several?), the second is the coach — not writing for them, but demanding the reason and catching stray apostrophes. On the next sentence they swap roles.
  5. The position rule done with hands (ABCD note in the TB): singular without -s → 's · a name in -s → both Jess's and Jess' work · a plural in -s → the apostrophe only · a plural without -s (children, men) → 's.
📺 On screen: SB pp.104–105 (Backup: print the three chats on sheets and have students circle the apostrophes in pencil; write the position rule on the board in four lines)
👦 Students do: they work through other people's chats and the apostrophe rule. Expect: “parents'” is the hardest one; work it out on fingers

🚀 Bonus level · not in the book
pairs

WHAT WE DO: a level for our students: a refusal that does NOT offend + the US/UK switch

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Weak refusal: 'No, I can't.' Full stop. That's how friendships end.
  2. Three levels of refusal on the board: 1 No, sorry, I can't. · 2 Sorry, I can't — I'm going to the dentist. · 3 Poor timing! I'm at the dentist Saturday morning. Are you free in the afternoon?
  3. Level three every time = richness points.
  4. The US/UK switch as a game: I say it American, you say it British. 'How about Saturday morning?' — 'How about ON Saturday morning?' 'Let's meet at two thirty.' — 'Let's meet at half past two.' 'The parking lot.' — 'The car park.' 'Vacation.' — 'Holiday.'
  5. Pick ONE variety and stay in it — that's what a real speaker does (TB tip).
📺 On screen: the slide with the three levels of refusal + the US/UK cards (Backup: two columns on the board, US | UK, and students translate in turn)
👦 Students do: they lift the refusal to level three. Expect: the US/UK game works as a warm-up and takes the tension out before the hand-in

PRODUCT · the arrangement
pairs → stage

WHAT WE DO: PERFORM: the dialogue in front of the class + peer review; then the draft of their own chat

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. PERFORM. Five Key phrases minimum, two failed attempts, one final arrangement with a place and a time.
  2. TB tip: with a large or a less confident class, perform in small groups of two pairs rather than in front of everyone.
  3. Peer review, ex.6 p.103: What did they decide to do and when? How many Key phrases did you count? Count them out loud.
  4. Extra TB questions after the performances: Which other Key phrases could they use? What reasons did they give for not meeting? How do YOU usually make arrangements with friends — phone, message, at school?
  5. Straight after the stage — the draft of the chat (ex.8 p.105) in seven steps: ask about plans → “not sure” → invite → refuse with a reason → invite again → accept → where and when. Check against CHECK: going to and present continuous · the inviting and replying phrases · apostrophes.
📺 On screen: timer · judges' cards · the rubric · SB p.105 Skills boost
👦 Students do: they perform, count each other's phrases and write the draft. Expect: the class counting the Key phrases is the best motivator; after the stage the draft goes fast

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, pairs can make arrangements out loud using at least five Key phrases (with two refusals and a reason) and write an invitation exchange with correct apostrophes.
  • Subsidiary. ex.2–3 the video and the Key phrases; ex.4 completing the dialogue; the Skills boost THINK→PREPARE→PRACTISE→PERFORM; the US/UK note and Phrasebook p.125; pp.104–105 the emojis, three chats, the table of invitation and reply phrases, the apostrophes subskill (ex.6–7), their own chat in ex.8 and the peer review in ex.9.
  • Personal. Drill the refusal phrases to automaticity — they are what unblocks the conversation. Don't lecture on apostrophes: students first find all three types in someone else's text, then fix their own.
By the end, students can…
  • ask about someone's plans in five different ways
  • say no politely, give a reason and offer another time
  • agree on a place and a time and confirm it
  • write an invitation exchange with correct apostrophes

Form
after fancy — the -ing form (Do you fancy COMING?); after would like — the infinitive with to (Would you like TO come?). Apostrophes: contraction (I'm, can't, we're, I'd) and possession (my sister's birthday, my grandparents' house).
Pron. watch
the intonation of a refusal: “No, sorry, I'm …” falls and stays soft; sympathy “Poor you!” — falling; “Awesome!” — rising. Plus US/UK: two thirty (US) / half past two (UK)
Concept checks (CCQs) · Assumptions
“Do you fancy to come?” — what is wrong? → fancy needs -ing: Do you fancy coming?
“Its my sisters birthday” — how many apostrophes are missing? → two: It's my sister's birthday.
“my parents' anniversary” — how many parents? → two: a plural in -s, the apostrophe after the s (ABCD note in the TB).

Assumptions: SB pp.103–105, syllabus pair 67. Present continuous and going to have been in use since Pair 5; what is new are the refusal phrases and the social strategy of “no, but”.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The dialogue ends at the first “yes”The mission constraint (a TB tip): at least two failed attempts, then the arrangement. The judges count the attempts.
The refusal sounds rudeThe refusal formula on the board: sorry + I can't + a reason + a counter-offer. Plus an intonation drill from the video, with pauses.
Apostrophes get sprinkled everywhere “just in case”The TB rule, done with hands: ask first — is this a contraction or a possessive? If it is possessive — is the noun plural or singular?
Classroom management — signals
«Eyes up in 3-2-1» — count down calmly → all eyes on you at «1». No shouting over noise, ever.
Visible timer — timer on screen = we work; timer stops = pens/hands down. The timer is the boss, not you.
Volume dial — arm up/down = louder/quieter; «radio whisper» for rehearsals.
«Boards up!» — mini-whiteboard rounds: everyone commits, all boards up together — nobody hides, nobody shouts first.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, class answers with two and freezes — instant reset mid-game.
