🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Control the -ed/-ing adjective switch (bored/boring, annoyed/annoying, tired/tiring…) — -ed for the person, -ing for the cause — and write a magazine article of about 100 words with a question hook, vivid language and an opinion.
What is genuinely hard
Russian has one word where English has two: «скучный фильм» and «мне скучно» both arrive as boring — so I am boring writes itself. Plus the two irregulars: stressed pairs with stressFUL, scared with scarY. In the writing, the hard part is the hook: starting with a question instead of «I want to tell you about…».
Typical error to expect
I am boring for «мне скучно» — the single most-quoted adjective error of Russian learners: -ed is how the PERSON feels, -ing is what the thing IS. Plus the two irregulars of the set: stressed → stressful (not stressing!), scared → scary (not scaring!) [high priority, A2/B1]
An opinion with no reason attached — I think reality shows are bad full stop — scores as no opinion at all in the speaking [national exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
The block's loud-half/quiet-half shape: the -ed/-ing rule is discovered from ONE cinema picture (the story is confusing → she is confused), stress-tested on the two famous traps, personalised through the book's own p.123 pairwork, and walked in the split-walls break. The quiet half is model-first: the Sunday article is mined for its three crafts (question hook · adverb+adjective · opinion frame), the beginnings market teaches what an opening promises, and then everyone writes ~100 words IN CLASS — because an article finished at home is an article written by somebody else. Tuesday's perfect and Monday's feelings are compulsory ingredients: the block's language earns its keep the same week. Peer review checks the walls before the paint.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“A boring person and a bored person — which one is YOU at school assemblies? English splits every feeling into two words: one for you, one for the thing that did it to you. Mix them up and you insult yourself. Today you master the switch — and then write an article about your favourite day that a real magazine page is waiting for.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a three-sentence error clinic from lesson 2, and the bridge into the switch

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: the notice, the model, the craft box

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 — the notice, counted: Three questions. Every one needs an answer. That's the law of articles. The notice: ARTICLES WANTED! — Everyone has a favourite day of the week. What day do you like best? What do you do on this day? How do you feel? Tell us what you think! → What's YOUR favourite day?
  2. ex.2 — read the model and take its notes: model notes: best day Sunday · activities: gets up late, looks online for project ideas, goes climbing, dinner with relatives, family film · feelings: excited, full of energy, relaxed.
  3. ex.3 — hunt the box phrases in the model. Key: box phrases found in the model: «Are you one of those people who looks forward to weekends?» · «I absolutely love Sundays» · «I feel excited and full of energy» · «really exciting» · «In my view, it's a perfectly relaxing end…». Extra craft: adverbs instead of very — absolutely, really, perfectly.
  4. Mine it for the block's grammar: Find me the present perfect. ('My dad and I have just joined a climbing centre.') Why perfect? … recent, and it matters NOW.
  5. Name the three crafts on the board: question hook · adverb + adjective (absolutely love, perfectly relaxing) · opinion frame (In my view…).
📺 On screen: the model is on SB p.79 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed

Why: Three notices, three openings, six stray sentences: matching them teaches what an opening PROMISES and what a body must deliver — structure learned by handling it, not by hearing about it.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–5: three notices, three openings, six stray sentences

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4 — match notices 1–3 to beginnings A–C. Pairs, 3 minutes. Key: notices to beginnings: 1 A · 2 C · 3 B.
  2. Ask the why: How did you know B belongs to the car-journey notice? … the hook PROMISES the topic.
  3. ex.5 — six stray sentences, two per article. Key: sentences to articles (two each): a 2 · b 3 · c 1 · d 3 · e 1 · f 2 — mine them for craft: adverb+adj (incredibly grateful, absolutely exhausted), time frames (by the weekend, as soon as), opinion frames (I would say that, For me).
  4. Choose your brief: notice from ex.1 (favourite day) or any of ex.4's three (Bored! · An amazing person · Your week). Thirty seconds of silent deciding; hands show the split.
📺 On screen: the notices are on SB p.79 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed

🎯 Writing out · my article
solo, silent mode
Why: The productive outcome in writing: a real 100-word article with a title and a question hook, built on a model, a plan and a checklist. Started AND finished in class.

WHAT WE DO: about 100 words: title, question hook, every question answered, opinion close

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The checklist goes up before the first word: title · first sentence ends in ? · ALL the notice questions answered · one -ed AND one -ing · one adverb+adjective · opinion line (In my view… / I would say that…) · ~100 words.
  2. Eighteen minutes of silent writing. You walk, read over shoulders, point at the checklist — no talking, no spelling service: Circle it, carry on, we fix it in review.
  3. Time calls in chalk: 10 · 5 · 2 minutes.
  4. Fast finishers: add ONE more concrete detail with a reason — or upgrade two flat adjectives with adverbs (absolutely, incredibly, perfectly).
  5. Two minutes before the end: Read your own last sentence. Is it an opinion — or just a stop?
📺 On screen: the checklist in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed; nothing else goes up while the room writes
👦 Students do: write to the end in silence. Expect 80–110 words from most; expect the opinion close to be the weak wall — the last-sentence call exists for it. Typical error: the hook question answered immediately with one word — the body must do the answering

70–80′
Peer review + polish
pairs
Why: A checklist swap turns every student into an editor: they catch each other's missing answers and flat hooks faster than they catch their own — both sides learn.

WHAT WE DO: checklist swap: walls first, paint second

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap notebooks. Round 1 — the walls: does the first sentence end in a question mark? tick each notice question you can FIND an answer to; a missing tick goes back to the author now, and one sentence gets added on the spot.
  2. Round 2 — the paint: Kagan RallyCoach: find one -ed, one -ing, one opinion frame; circle one spelling you doubt (embarrassed and disappointed are the usual suspects).
  3. Authors take their work back: three minutes of repair.
  4. The reviewer writes one honest compliment line under the article — a real one, about content.
📺 On screen: the two review rounds numbered in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: check walls, check paint, repair, compliment. Expect at least a third of articles to gain a hook or an answer — that is the stage paying for itself. Typical problem: reviews that only hunt spelling — the rounds are ordered against exactly that

Structure
The film was boring, so I was bored. · My little brother is really annoying. · Are you one of those people who…? · I absolutely love Sundays. · In my view, it's a perfectly relaxing end to the day.
Form
-ed = how the person FEELS · -ing = the thing that CAUSES the feeling · stressed→stressful, scared→scary (no -ing forms) · article shape: title → question hook → answers to every question → opinion close.
Pron. watch
the endings do not add a syllable: bored /bɔːd/, tired /taɪəd/ — but -ing does: BOring, TIring · annoying /əˈnɔɪɪŋ/ — two i-sounds meet · the hook question rises: Are you one of those people ↗ who…
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«The lesson was boring» — who felt something? → the students (they were bored); boring describes the LESSON, the cause.
«I am boring» — what did I just say about myself? → that I CAUSE boredom in others. Is that what you meant? → usually not: I am bored.
«My week is stressing» — does that word exist? → the pair is irregular: the week is stressFUL, I am stressed.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«I am boring» survives the discovery stageIt will — the reflex is deep. The split-walls break and the peer-review hunt are the second and third passes; by the article, casualties are rare. Never mock it: «you just called yourself dull» once, kindly, is enough.
stressing and scaring get inventedThe two irregulars are named at discovery and put on the wall as a pair card: stressful · scary. One choral repetition each; then point silently.
Hooks collapse into «I want to tell you about…»The box gives four ready hook shapes; the rule is mechanical — your first sentence ends in a question mark. Peer review round 1 checks exactly that before anything else.
Fast writers finish at 60 wordsThe bar is names-and-reasons: not «I like Sunday» but WHAT you do, WHO with, WHY it matters. One example pushed deep beats three listed.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
