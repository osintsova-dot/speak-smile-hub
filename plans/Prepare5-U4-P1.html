🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use fifteen words for music and the music industry (gig, lyrics, sound technician, concert hall, live, music channel…) and choose between the present perfect and the past simple by asking one question: is the time finished, or does it reach now? Plus the three adverbs that live with the perfect — just, already, yet.
What is genuinely hard
Not the words — the tense choice. Russian has one past tense where English has two systems, so the perfect leaks into sentences that name a finished time, and the past simple leaks into sentences that are still open. The adverbs make it harder before they make it easier: their POSITION is marked, and yet does not sit where already sits.
Typical error to expect
«Yesterday I have bought a new album» — a finished time takes the past simple, always. Russian has one past tense for both, so the perfect leaks into sentences that name a day, and it is the single commonest grammar error in the Russian exam's written work.
«Did you already decide?» and «I haven't seen the clip yesterday» — already and yet belong to the perfect, and yesterday does not. The Student's Book Corpus exercise plants both traps in the same six lines.
Why the lesson is built this way
The words arrive through sound, not through a list: five recordings and five photographs (a band rehearsing, an orchestra, a mixing desk, a festival crowd, a radio studio), so every word is attached to a place before it is attached to a definition. The reading is the best hook in the block — a seventeen-year-old who posted six seconds of a cover version and had ten thousand likes the next morning — and the grammar is then lifted straight out of that text, where both tenses are already sitting side by side. The productive stage is a two-minute personal answer that cannot be given without both tenses: how many artists have you seen live, and when did you last listen to a whole album?
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“He was fifteen, he taught himself the guitar from YouTube, and one six-second clip made him famous overnight. His first EP took thirty-seven minutes to reach number one. Today: the words the music industry actually uses — and the two tenses that let you say what has happened and what happened.”

Move break · people, places, things
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that spends all fifteen words at speed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands behind their chair. You call a word from the wall; the class points LEFT for a person, FORWARD for a place, RIGHT for a thing. No talking.
  2. Ten words at speed, then flip it: you describe (the person who makes sure the sound is right at a concert) and they call the word out.
  3. Last thirty seconds, in pairs: Name one gig or concert you would go to tomorrow if you could.
  4. Freeze and sit: Page forty-three. A boy who became famous in six seconds.
📺 On screen: the wall, still up — nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone moves on every word; nobody can hide behind a neighbour

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: predict from four numbers, then read to check

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Look at the photograph. Pairs, sixty seconds: How do you think he became famous? Take three guesses onto the board.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–5: multiple choice done the exam way, then the five highlighted words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Before they answer: the method on the board. 1 read the question · 2 underline the key words in ALL THREE options · 3 find the place in the text · 4 say why the other two are wrong.
  2. ex.4 individually, five minutes. Rule: next to every answer, write the line number where you found it.
  3. Pairs compare — line numbers first, letters second. Where the line numbers agree, the letters almost always do.
  4. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: 1 B, 2 C, 3 A, 4 B, 5 C. For each one, take the rejected options too — Why is A wrong in question two?
  5. ex.5: the five highlighted words matched to meanings (achieved, performs, checked, interacted, signed up with). Pairs, two minutes.
  6. Talking points if the clock allows: Have you ever uploaded a video to YouTube? Why / Why not? Why do people upload videos? In the future, do you think all stars will become famous through social media? — pairs first, then the floor

👦 Students do: work alone with a line number for every answer, then justify rejections out loud

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–4: two rules out of the text, then the three adverbs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books shut for ninety seconds. Two sentences from the text on the board: I've just become a Mendes fan. / The next day he checked his account. One question: In which one do I know exactly when?
  2. ex.1: the four examples matched to the two rules — 1 and 3 to the perfect, 2 and 4 to the past simple. Elicit the deciding question and write it above both columns: Is the time finished?
  3. ex.2 individually, three minutes: six items, the two tenses. Check by nomination, and for every answer the student says the reason in four words (finished time / link to now).
  4. ex.3–4: the three adverbs. Elicit the rules (already = at some time before now · just = a short time ago · yet = questions and negatives), then the POSITION rule, which is the part that gets marked: just and already between the auxiliary and the verb, yet at the end.
  5. Grammar reference is SB p.153 if anybody wants the whole picture in writing — say so, do not read it aloud.
📺 On screen: two columns on the board headed FINISHED and REACHES NOW, filled by the class — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail

Why: 🎯 Speaking out · have you ever, and when exactly

WHAT WE DO: ex.5–7: the Corpus errors, the adverb drill, then a personal exchange

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.5 (Corpus) in RallyCoach pairs, four minutes: six real learner sentences, six mistakes. One pen, one paper, alternate roles. These are the errors of actual Cambridge candidates — say that aloud, it changes how they treat them.
  2. Check fast and hard: I have just seen · Yesterday I bought · has joined · Have you already decided · I didn't see · I haven't seen … yet.
  3. ex.6, three minutes: six sentences needing the perfect AND the adverb in the right place. Nominate, and make the student say where the adverb goes before they say the sentence.
  4. 🎯 ex.7 as Kagan Timed Pair Share, two minutes each way: How many artists have you seen live? When did you last listen to a whole album? Have you listened to anyone new this month?
  5. Rule that makes it a grammar task and not just chat: Every «have you ever» answer needs a WHEN sentence after it. Model it once: I've been to two gigs. I went to the last one in June.
  6. Collect three exchanges to the floor and correct only the tense choice — nothing else, today.
📺 On screen: the model exchange on the board (I've been to… / I went to… ) — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: correct six real exam errors in pairs, then speak for four minutes with a rule that forces both tenses

Structure
I've just become a Mendes fan. · Mendes is young, but he's already achieved so much. · The next day he checked his account. · He practised every day. · They haven't announced the judges yet.
Form
present perfect (have/has + past participle) for a past event with a link to now, and with just, already and yet · past simple for finished actions in finished time · position: just and already between auxiliary and verb, yet at the END.
Pron. watch
live has TWO pronunciations and the unit needs both: /laɪv/ for a live concert, /lɪv/ for where you live — the class will merge them unless you separate them out loud · REcord (the noun) against reCORD (the verb), the classic stress pair · musician /mjuˈzɪʃn/ with a /ʃ/, not a /z/ · lyrics /ˈlɪrɪks/ · and the perfect auxiliary is weak in fast speech: /aɪv ˈdʌn ɪt/, not /aɪ hæv/
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I've seen them live» — do I know WHEN? → no, and that is the point. The moment does not matter; the experience reaches now.
«I saw them live last June» — is June finished? → yes. Finished time, past simple. No choice at all.
«They haven't announced it ___.» Where does yet go? → at the end. Say the pair aloud twice: I've already done it / I haven't done it yet.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The class answers every tense item with the perfectOne question on the board, in these words, and every disputed item gets it: Is the time finished? If a finished time word is in the sentence (yesterday, last week, in 2014), the perfect is impossible. Say IMPOSSIBLE, not wrong.
just / already / yet go in the wrong placeThree fixed sentence frames on the board, left up all lesson: I've JUST seen it. / I've ALREADY seen it. / I haven't seen it YET. Students point at the frame instead of explaining.
live is read as /lɪv/Separate the two on the board with a picture and a sentence: I LIVE /lɪv/ in Norilsk · a LIVE /laɪv/ concert. Drill four times chorally, then catch it every time it comes back in the lesson.
Fast finishers in the vocabulary setThe Teacher's Book task: do exercise 3 with the box COVERED. Then write one sentence per column of the wall for the class check.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

Present perfect against past simple is the single most productive grammar item in the Russian paper: a text will name a year and demand a past simple two lines after it demands a perfect. The signal is always the same one the Student's Book teaches today — is there a link to now, or is the time finished? Today's reading, a teenager who became famous from a six-second video, is also the exam's favourite genre: one person, one chronology, five questions.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
