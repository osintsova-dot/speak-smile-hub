🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Sit the publisher's official End-of-year paper — the whole twenty-unit year in twelve short sections — and leave with a number that means something in September.
What is genuinely hard
The paper is a fair one and the class knows its shape from the Term tests, but section 6 is genuinely hard: eight sentences rewritten so they mean the same, drawing on the passive, reported speech, have something done, reported questions reversed and a modal passive. That is five separate structures in eight items and it is the section where the year is actually measured. The second difficulty is the clock: twelve sections plus a hundred-word letter do not fit comfortably into forty-five minutes, whatever the syllabus says.
Typical error to expect
Leaving section 6 until last because it looks hard. It carries eight marks like every other section and it needs a clear head, so it belongs in the first half of the written block, not the last.
Under-writing the letter. «About 100 words» with three questions to answer means roughly thirty words per question plus an opening and a closing. Sixty words cannot answer three questions and it is the commonest way this section is lost.
Why the lesson is built this way
This paper measures the COURSE, not the exam — that job was done in lesson 69 with the real Cambridge paper, and the two are deliberately different animals. So the day is built around the course's own content: the paired speaking section is done in the FIRST half, before the silence, because it needs none and because the last lesson of a year should start with people talking; the express review before it is twenty minutes aimed precisely at section 6, where the marks are; then the silent paper with a named priority order for what gets carried if the clock beats us. The last four minutes are not administrative — they are the end of a year and they are planned as such.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Last lesson. We start by talking, then you sit the paper, and this one covers everything since September. It is the course test, not the Cambridge one — twelve short sections, the shape you already know. Twenty minutes of review first, aimed at the one section where the marks actually are, and then it is yours.”

WHAT WE DO: the self-chosen revision collected and the last lesson opened properly

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — collect the notebooks from last lesson's self-chosen revision. Look at each for five seconds and check one thing only: did they choose from their own log? Say one sentence to anyone who did.
  2. 1 minute — Kagan RallyRobin: in pairs, thirty seconds each way — one thing you can do in English now that you could not do in September. Not a grammar point; something you can DO.
  3. Take three of those to the floor and write them on the board. They stay there through the paper, which is deliberate.
  4. Bridge (30 seconds): Last lesson. We start by talking, then you sit the paper. Twenty minutes of review first, and it is aimed at exactly one section.

Why: Naming the target of the review and the purpose of the paper in the same three minutes turns a test day from a verdict into a piece of work.

WHAT WE DO: name the shape of the day and the one section that matters most

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the day on the board in four lines: twenty minutes of review · ten minutes of paired speaking · four minutes of briefing · the paper.
  2. Say what this paper is and is not, in one sentence, because they sat a Cambridge paper eight days ago: This one is the course test — twelve short sections, the shape you know from the Term tests, and it covers everything since September.
  3. Say which section the review is aimed at and why, plainly: Grammar 6 — eight sentences rewritten so they mean the same: the passive, reported speech, have something done, reported questions turned back into direct ones, the active from the passive, and a modal passive. This is the hardest section on the paper and the most representative of the year.
  4. Say the true thing about what today decides, in one sentence: This produces a profile that tells us where to start in September. It does not decide anything about next year.
  5. Set the bar for the review: Twenty minutes. Ask for anything you want. It is the last chance and nobody is going to think less of you.

Why: Express review · the five structures of section 6

WHAT WE DO: twelve minutes aimed at the hardest eight marks on the paper

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Five headings on the board, one per structure: passive · reported statement · have something done · reported question reversed · modal passive.
  2. One example each, given by a student and written up by them. If nobody can supply one, give it — but ask first, because most of the room can.
  3. Then eight rapid items, one per structure plus three mixed, run as Kagan Showdown: you read the first sentence and the target opening, everybody writes the rewrite, everybody reveals together.
  4. Use real ones — they are on the paper's own pattern: My parents gave me a bike → I ______ · «The weather will be sunny,» he said → He said ______ · Maria wants somebody to cut her hair → Maria wants to ______ · You mustn't leave rubbish → Rubbish ______
  5. Two minutes on whichever of the five the Showdown exposed. Not more — this is triage, not teaching.
  6. Say the strategy line and write it on the board: In section six, read the SECOND sentence first. The words that are already there tell you which structure is wanted.
📺 On screen: the five headings with a student example under each — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: supply five examples themselves, then produce eight rewrites and reveal them simultaneously
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE tasks 19–25 (grammar in a text). The passive, reported speech and modal passives are three named lines of the Russian codifier, and section 6 makes a candidate produce all three from a prompt rather than recognise them.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: run two of the eight Showdown items in Russian-exam form instead — a gapped sentence with the verb in capitals — and say that is what the same knowledge looks like in the other paper. Costs nothing.

WHAT WE DO: eight minutes of rapid coverage across the other seven sections

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Seven quick rounds, about a minute each, and do not linger on any: phrasal verbs (come out, break up, put out, end up, catch up with) · personality adjectives (loyal, sociable, easygoing, talented) · prepositions and conjunctions (despite, instead of, because of, besides) · verb forms (the second conditional, the past continuous, the past perfect) · comparatives (as…as, the irregulars) · articles and relative pronouns · the long anagram section, which is a SPELLING section and should be named as one.
  2. Run it as Kagan RallyCoach in pairs: you call the field, the pairs produce three examples between them in forty seconds, one nominated pair reads theirs out.
  3. The point of this stage is coverage and confidence, not depth. Nobody learns anything new in the last twenty minutes before a paper; what they get is the reminder that they already know it.
  4. Last thirty seconds: Anything you want to ask before we stop reviewing? Answer whatever comes, briefly, and then stop reviewing.

🎯 The speaking section, in pairs
pairs
Why: 🎯 The speaking section, in pairs

WHAT WE DO: section 12 done before the silence, because it needs none

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Papers out, section 12 only, everything else face down.
  2. Part A first, three minutes, silent and individual: match the five question halves. It is the marked half and it should be said. Answers are checked at the end, not now.
  3. 🎯 Part B, six minutes, Kagan Timed Pair Share: ask and answer all five with a partner. Everybody talks at once. You sample three pairs and note what you hear for the profile you will write.
  4. The rule from the whole term, on the board one last time: every answer has two parts — the answer, then a reason or an example.
  5. Do not cut it short. This is the last spoken English of the course and the noise is the right way to spend six minutes of it.
  6. Section twelve is finished. Papers back to page one, face down.

Test briefing
desks
Why: Test briefing

WHAT WE DO: four minutes that decide the marks

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Papers face down. Take the board down in front of them — the five headings, the seven fields, all of it. Leave the three student-named achievements from the opening.
  2. Say the shape and the end times: listening first, then sections 1 to 8, then reading, then writing. Seventy-four objective marks plus a hundred-word letter and a paired conversation. Sections 1 to 8 are the bulk and take about thirty minutes; the listening needs eight with two plays; the reading takes seven; the writing takes twelve to fifteen honestly. If the clock beats you, the priority order is: listening first (it is the only section that cannot be moved), then grammar and vocabulary, then reading, and the WRITING is what gets carried — set it as a three-day deadline by email and mark it separately. Do not cut the paper in half in the room; cut it in advance and say so.
  3. Say the priority order out loud, because a class that knows the plan does not panic when the clock moves: if we run short, the writing is carried and comes to me by email within three days. Nothing else moves.
  4. Two content warnings, one line each: section 6 is the hard one and should be done while your head is clear, not last; and the letter answers THREE questions in about a hundred words, which is roughly thirty words each.
  5. Rules: silence, pens only, read the rubric of every section, check if you finish early, nobody leaves.
  6. Then the sentence that ends the briefing: Twelve short sections, the same shape as the Term tests, and you have done that shape twice. Go.

The written paper
desks, individual
Why: The written paper

WHAT WE DO: forty-four minutes: listening, then sections 1–8, then reading, then the letter

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Listening, section 9, first — about eight minutes. Ninety seconds to read the five statements and underline the key words, then the audio TWICE with ninety seconds of silence between the plays. Say that you are playing it twice.
  2. Sections 1 to 8 — about twenty-four minutes. Start together, end time on the board. Halfway call at twelve minutes, said once and quietly. Circulate slowly, say nothing about any answer, and intervene only on a misread rubric.
  3. Reading, section 10 — about six minutes. One line before they start, and it is technique rather than help: read the whole article first, then find the paragraph for each question. Question 5 asks what Tina WOULD say, which is inference, and it is worth naming.
  4. Writing, section 11 — the rest. Two minutes of notes, one per question, before a sentence is written. Then write. Two-minute call at the end: count the words and write the number.
  5. If the clock has beaten you, stop the writing where it is, collect it, and say the carry-over rule again out loud — three days, by email. Do not improvise a different plan in front of them.
  6. Pens down. That is the last paper of the year. Say it, and then wait three seconds before anything else happens.

The last four minutes of the course
desks → door
Why: A year that ends with papers being collected ends administratively. Four planned minutes turn it into a finish, and the class remembers the last four minutes far longer than the forty-four before them.

WHAT WE DO: papers in, and a year finished properly rather than administratively

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Papers in, face down, one pile, by row. No discussion of answers — and this time say what happens instead: You get a section-by-section profile, not a total, in the first week of June.
  2. Point at the three achievements still on the board from the opening, ninety minutes ago, and read them out. They were written by the class, they are true, and they are the correct last thing to be looking at.
  3. One offer, said once and not chased: the second complete Cambridge paper is available for the summer or for a retake in September, with its key. Anyone who wants it asks for it.
  4. Then one round of the room, one sentence each, and it is the whole ceremony: One thing you are going to do in English this summer. Anything counts — a series, a song, a book, twenty minutes a week on the trainer.
  5. Say the homework, briefly and honestly, because there almost is none: nothing compulsory. If the writing section was carried, it is due by email in three days.
  6. Then say goodbye properly. It is the last lesson of a twenty-unit year and the two seconds of silence before they leave are worth protecting.

The Cambridge paper measures the EXAM: real rubrics, real distractors, real answer sheets, real timing, and a score that means the same thing in any country. Using the exam paper for the rehearsal and the course paper for the final report is the correct way round, and the second Cambridge paper stays untouched as the summer set.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
