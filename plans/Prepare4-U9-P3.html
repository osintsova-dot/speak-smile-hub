🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name and define twelve jobs (architect, babysitter, builder, coach, firefighter, hairdresser, journalist, lawyer, pharmacist, politician, presenter, vet) with the right article, and read a real magazine article for detail well enough to argue about it.
What is genuinely hard
Half the words look like Russian and are pronounced nothing like it (architect with a /k/, journalist with the stress at the front). The article is the other trap: students give a job title and stop, where the exam and the conversation both want what the person DOES.
Typical error to expect
«He work as engineer» — the article disappears in Russian and so it disappears here: it is a vet, an architect, every time. Expect also «journaLIST» with English stress moved onto the last syllable.
Why the lesson is built this way
First of Unit 18's three lessons: vocabulary and reading only, with the grammar deliberately left unnamed. The TB's alphabet warmer surfaces what the class already owns before the new twelve arrive labelled (photos), then described (six speakers), then defined (the six the recording skipped). The Takeover Challenge article is genuine teenage territory — a fifteen-year-old running a school — and it is quietly stuffed with second conditionals which the class will NOTICE today and be taught next lesson. The productive stage is a pitch: which job would you take over for a day, and can you defend it against three real questions.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Fifty thousand British teenagers spend one day doing an adult's job. Sophie, fifteen, ran her own school. Alexandra met the prime minister. Today: twelve jobs in English — and the one YOU would take over tomorrow.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a 90-second recall of the biographies, and the bridge into jobs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — the biographies come back into the room. Kagan RallyRobin, 90 seconds: Tell your partner who you wrote about last lesson — and what job they do or did.
  3. Harvest four or five jobs onto the board in English, whatever comes — singer, teacher, engineer, doctor. Leave them there; the new twelve will join them.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the unit: Books open, page one hundred and two. Unit eighteen — The world of work. Twelve jobs, and then three teenagers who did one for a day.
📺 On screen: the harvested jobs in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: recall their biography subject and name a job. Expect two or three Russian job words — write the English beside them without comment

Lesson frame

Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the aim, open the job wall with its article rule

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The wall starts with two example entries written the right way: a vet — looks after animals and an architect — designs buildings. The article is in a different colour on purpose.

Warm-up · a job for every letter
teams of 3–4
Why: The TB's own warmer: teams race the alphabet for job words. It surfaces everything the class already owns in ninety seconds, so the new twelve land on a full shelf rather than an empty one.

WHAT WE DO: the TB warmer: the alphabet raced for job words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Teams of three or four, four minutes, one sheet each: a job for as many letters of the alphabet as possible. Any language of origin, but the word must be English.
  2. Hard stop, then teams swap sheets and check each other's — Cross out anything that isn't a job or isn't English.
  3. Scoring: one point per job, two if no other team has it. Read the unique ones aloud — they are usually the best moment of the stage.
  4. Leave the sheets on the desks; students add today's new words to them as they come.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice and sheets on the desks — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: race the alphabet, then audit a rival team. Expect X and Z to produce arguments; let them, it costs ten seconds and buys energy

Vocabulary · twelve jobs
solo → pairs
Why: Photographs plus two recordings: the job words arrive labelled, then arrive again described by the people who do them — meaning first, spelling second, articles checked every time (a lawyer, an architect).

WHAT WE DO: About you + ex.1 (photos, track 84) + ex.2 (six speakers, track 85)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. About you as a fast pair exchange: What jobs do the people you know do? What job might YOU want to do — and why? Two answers to the class, no more.
  2. ex.1: label the twelve photos from the box, three minutes solo, then compare. Track 84 confirms. Key: A architect · B builder · C vet · D politician · E hairdresser · F presenter · G journalist · H babysitter · I lawyer · J firefighter · K pharmacist · L coach.
  3. Pronunciation drill on the four traps, three choral repetitions each: architect /ˈɑːkɪtekt/ with a /k/, never «arhitekt» · lawyer /ˈlɔːjə/ · vet is one short syllable · musician /mjuːˈzɪʃn/ and politician /pɒləˈtɪʃn/ both end /ʃn/ · -er and -or are the SAME weak /ə/.
  4. ex.2, track 85: six people describe their work — which job is each one? One play, pairs compare, second play for the two that split the room. Key: the six speakers: 1 vet · 2 firefighter · 3 babysitter · 4 hairdresser · 5 coach · 6 builder.
  5. Every confirmed job goes onto the wall with its article and its what-they-do line, written by the student who answered.
📺 On screen: video 12 (About you) plus tracks 84 and 85 in the board app (Backup: the audioscripts are on TB p.276–277 — read the six descriptions aloud yourself, one at a time, and the task is unchanged; for the photos, the box on p.102 plus the pictures is enough on its own)
👦 Students do: label, listen, correct, build the wall. Expect politician and presenter to swap places in the photo task — the giveaway is the microphones versus the studio

WHAT WE DO: ex.3: the six jobs the recording skipped, then Quiz-Quiz-Trade

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 in pairs, three minutes: six definitions, six jobs — and the rule that makes it a task, not a lottery: Only the jobs the recording did NOT use. Key: 1 a lawyer · 2 an architect · 3 a politician · 4 a presenter · 5 a pharmacist · 6 a journalist — the six jobs the recording did NOT use.
  2. Answers given as full sentences with the article: A lawyer explains the law and gives advice.
  3. Card round: twelve cards, one per student (job on one side, what-they-do on the other). Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade, four minutes, maximum trades — read the definition, partner names the job with its article, coach, swap cards, move.
  4. Close with a five-item lightning volley from you: definitions read fast, whole class answers chorally.
📺 On screen: printed job cards — the cards in their hands are the medium; if the set has gone missing, read the definitions aloud and rows answer chorally instead
👦 Students do: define, trade, coach, answer at speed. Expect pharmacist to be the slowest word in the room — it earns an extra choral drill

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: gist from the photos, then six detail questions with evidence

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hook, books closed: Fifty thousand British teenagers spend one day doing an adult's job. A real job. One of them ran her own school. What could possibly go wrong?
  2. ex.1 — photos and introduction only, two minutes of fast reading to check the three jobs. Key: Sophie was a headteacher · Simon was a football coach · Alexandra spent the day with a politician.
  3. ex.2 in pairs, Kagan RallyCoach, eight minutes: six questions, and every answer read aloud from the line that proves it. Key: 1 every year · 2 both sides: young people get experience of a wide range of jobs, adults learn from young people's ideas · 3 the meetings — there were so many and some went on for ages · 4 training with professional players was exhausting · 5 it is a 24/7 job with no time even for lunch · 6 the jobs named in the article: actor, sports star, musician, TV presenter, sports coach, journalist, (head) teacher, politician, school chef, professional footballer, prime minister.
  4. Question 6 is the collector's item — the article names eleven jobs; teams race to find them all and add the new ones to the wall.
  5. Silently, while they work: underline the three second conditionals in your own copy. You will need them at the start of the next lesson, verbatim.
📺 On screen: SB p.103 in every book; track 86 is the recording of the text and is optional (Backup: the stage is designed for silent reading — run it that way and nothing is lost)
👦 Students do: read for gist, then hunt with evidence. Expect question 2 (who does the day help?) to get half an answer — the text says BOTH sides, and the second half is the interesting one

50–58′
Reading · word harvest + fresh ideas
solo → class
Why: Six highlighted expressions harvested by definition (a wide range, ages, fresh, unforgettable), then the Talking point that the text sets up: is the Takeover Challenge actually a good idea?

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 by definition, then the Talking point argued

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 solo, three minutes: six meanings, six highlighted expressions. Key: 1 a wide range · 2 chance · 3 ages · 4 fresh · 5 unforgettable · 6 prime minister.
  2. TB's sharper version if the group is quick: books closed, you read the definitions, the class supplies the expression from memory.
  3. Then the argument, five minutes, Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Is the Takeover Challenge a good idea? Why, or why not? What job would you like to take over for a day? — pairs first, then open the floor; every opinion carries a because
  4. Push for the adult side of it once: The article says adults learn from the teenagers. Do you believe that, or is it just something they say? That question always produces the best English of the stage.
📺 On screen: SB p.103 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: harvest, argue, justify. Expect for ages to be the word everyone adopts immediately — it is the most useful of the six

58–62′
Reset · guess my job
standing
Why: Ninety seconds of yes/no questioning that keeps every job word in play and hands the class the question forms it needs for the pitch stage.

WHAT WE DO: three minutes of yes/no questioning on their feet

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. One student takes a job card and stands at the front. The class asks yes/no questions only — the TB's own set works best: Do you work inside? Do you wear a uniform? Do you need a degree? Do you work at weekends?
  2. Maximum eight questions before a guess is compulsory. Three rounds, three different students, everyone standing until their team guesses right.
  3. Sit down. Last stage: not somebody else's job — YOURS, for one day.
📺 On screen: job cards in hands — the cards are the medium; if they are missing, whisper a job to the student at the front instead
👦 Students do: question, deduce, guess. Everyone asks — the rule is that no student may ask twice until every hand has been up once

WHAT WE DO: ex.4: five questions answered as a ranked list with reasons

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of three or four. The five questions of ex.4 go up as a task with a twist: for each question the group must AGREE on a top three, not a single answer.
  2. Six minutes, then one question per group reported to the class. Possible answers for your reference: possible answers — 1 a degree: lawyer, journalist, architect, politician, pharmacist, vet · 2 best paid: lawyer, vet, presenter · 3 mainly an office: lawyer, journalist, architect · 4 weekend work: firefighter, babysitter, coach, pharmacist, presenter · 5 their own opinions, with reasons.
  3. The reporting frame is on the board and is not optional: We think the three best-paid jobs are… because…
  4. One follow-up question from you per group — always Why is that one first and not second?
📺 On screen: the five questions and the reporting frame in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: rank, argue, report with reasons. Expect a fight about who is paid most; the language is the point, so let the fight run thirty seconds

72–86′
🎯 Speaking out · the takeover pitch
pairs → mingling
Why: The productive outcome: each student claims a job to take over for a day and defends the choice against real questions from the group. The whole vocabulary set has to be used, and the reasons do the language work.

WHAT WE DO: each student claims one job to take over for a day and defends it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two minutes of silent preparation, notes only: Which job would you take over for a day? Three reasons, and one thing you think would be difficult.
  2. Kagan Mix-Pair-Share, three rounds: pitch your job in thirty seconds, then the listener asks all three compulsory questions from the board — Why that job? What would be the hardest part? Would you do it every day? Swap, then find a new partner.
  3. Sit down and report a PARTNER, not yourself: Ilya would take over a vet's job because he's better with animals than with people. Six or seven reports.
  4. Silently note anyone who reaches for would in the last question — that is next lesson's opening quotation, and you will credit them by name.
📺 On screen: the three compulsory questions in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: pitch, interrogate, swap, report in the third person. Expect the third question to produce natural second conditionals — collect them, do not correct them

WB Unit 18 (p.72–75)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can name twelve jobs and define them (Who designs buildings? — An architect does), and have read a magazine article for detail with every answer justified from the text.
  • Subsidiary. Listening to six people describe their work (tracks 84–85); a six-word vocabulary harvest by definition; a defended opinion in the pitch stage.
  • Personal. The JOB WALL goes up with articles attached — write «a vet», never «vet». Do NOT name the second conditional today, even when it appears in the text three times; underline it silently and let lesson 4 harvest it.
By the end, students can…
  • name twelve jobs with the correct article
  • define a job by what the person does
  • find the answer to a question in a long text and read the proof aloud
  • argue for a job they would take over for a day

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«a vet» or «an vet»? → a vet. And «a architect» or «an architect»? → an. The sound decides, not the spelling.
«He works like a journalist» — better? → works AS a journalist. Like means he is similar to one; as means he IS one.
«24/7» — how many days off? → none at all. That is exactly why Alexandra was surprised.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Articles vanish: «I want to be lawyer»The wall carries the article in a different colour, and the correction is a raised finger, not a sentence — students self-repair within three attempts.
Students recognise the jobs but cannot define themEvery job on the wall gets a WHAT THEY DO line beside it, written by a student, not by you. The Quiz-Quiz-Trade round then makes them say those lines twenty times.
Second conditionals in the text derail the readingExpected, and it is fine: underline them, name nothing. If asked directly — Good eye. That machine has its own lesson, on Thursday. Today we're reading.
The pitch stage turns into one-word answersThe three questions are compulsory and printed on the board, and the listener asks all three before swapping. No question, no swap.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
