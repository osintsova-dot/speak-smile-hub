🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Describe a person's character with fifteen B2 adjectives (broad-minded, down-to-earth, resourceful, impetuous, laid-back, reserved, tactful, considerate, self-confident, untrustworthy, resilient …) and eight idioms (a live wire, a wallflower, a big mouth, a party animal), and read a 1,000-word blog for detail: four multiple-choice questions answered from the text, not from opinion.
What is genuinely hard
Not the meaning — the precision. A2 English has kind, nice, funny; B2 English has considerate, tactful, down-to-earth, and the three are not the same person. The second difficulty is grammatical: half these adjectives are compounds with a fixed hyphen and a fixed stress (broad-MINDed), and students who learn them as two words never say them fluently.
Typical error to expect
«creativeness», «lazyness», «inventer» — the stem changes when the suffix goes on (creativity, laziness, inventor). This is a spelling mark in tasks 26–31 and it is learned by writing, not by recognising.
«He is very sociable person» — a singular countable noun needs an article, and Russian does not have one to remind them. It comes back as a grammar point in Lesson 3; today just correct it in the air, in one word: a sociable person.
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary is not presented as a list — it arrives inside the Big Five personality quiz on page 44, so every adjective is read as a description of a real person before it is ever written down. The reading then does the opposite job: Susan Cain's blog argues that the quiet half of the class has been undervalued, which makes the introverts in the room the experts for once. Vocabulary → text → opinion, with a standing stirrer in the middle so nobody sits for more than twenty-five minutes.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
Today you learn to describe people the way adults do. Not «he is nice» — «he is down-to-earth, but he can be tactless». Fifteen adjectives, eight idioms, and then a text by a woman who wrote a whole book to say one thing: being quiet is not a problem to be fixed. By the end of the lesson each of you will describe one person in this room without naming them, and we will guess who it is.

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting at the door, then a one-sentence future-form round on last unit's language

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what is the best thing that happened to you this week? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — rewind, Unit 3. On the board: Say one thing about your plans for next summer — use a future form. Round the circle, one sentence each, no repeats. Correct only the future form; everything else waits.
  3. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Last unit you talked about what people DO. This unit is about what people ARE. Books at page 44.
📺 On screen: the rewind prompt written on the board. Backup: say it aloud twice — this stage needs no equipment at all.
👦 Students do: stand in a loose circle, one sentence each, about eight seconds per student.

5–14′
Lead-in · the Big Five, without the words yet
desks
Why: A personality quiz is the one context where a teenager wants the vocabulary before it is given — they have just described themselves and are short of words. That gap is the lesson.

WHAT WE DO: students take the personality quiz on SB p.149 before they meet a single new adjective

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books at page 44. Point at the five letters O-C-E-A-N down the side of the quiz: Psychologists say every personality fits five scales. Not five types — five scales. You are somewhere on each one.
  2. Send them to the quiz on p.149: read, tick, no dictionaries, three minutes, silence.
  3. RallyRobin in pairs: Which scale surprised you? One sentence each, take turns. Thirty seconds each way.
  4. Do NOT teach any adjective yet. If somebody asks what impetuous means, say hold it — page 44 in two minutes.
📺 On screen: the O-C-E-A-N letters copied onto the board while they read. Backup: the whole stage is book-only, so nothing breaks if the projector is off.
👦 Students do: read and tick the quiz individually, then one exchange in pairs.

14–30′
Vocabulary in context · fifteen adjectives
desks
Why: Fifteen adjectives is too many for a list and exactly right for three short texts. Concept checks are aimed at the three pairs that Russian collapses into one word (tactful/tactless, reserved/unfriendly, self-confident/big-headed).

WHAT WE DO: SB p.44 exercises 1 and 2 — the adjectives are lifted out of the quiz texts they have just read

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pre-teach exactly five, in a sentence, never in a translation: Dan's parents are very broad-minded. He dyed his hair green and it was not a problem for them. Do the same for down-to-earth, impetuous, reserved, resourceful.
  2. Exercise 1 — sections O, C and E only. Individually, four minutes, then pairs compare. Answers: a broad-minded · b down-to-earth · c resourceful · d impetuous · e laid-back · f outgoing · g reserved (sections O, C and E of the Big Five quiz only).
  3. Exercise 2 — sections A and N, same procedure. Answers: h tactful · i considerate · j selfish · k untrustworthy · l self-confident · m moody · n insecure · o resilient (sections A and N).
  4. Concept-check the three that always blur: If I am tactful, do I always say what I think? (no — I say it kindly). If I am tactless? (I say it and it hurts). If I am reserved, do I dislike people? (no — I just do not show much).
  5. Mixed ability: for a weaker group, write the three or four words needed for each text on the board and say that one word in each set is not used.
  6. Drill the stress in chorus, then in pairs — see the pronunciation line in the preparation section.
📺 On screen: the five model sentences on the board as you say them; leave them up for the whole stage. Backup: if you are projecting the page, a paper copy of p.44 on the desk works identically.
👦 Students do: read, complete, compare in pairs, answer the concept questions with one word, repeat the stress pattern twice.

30–36′
Stirrer · the synonym–antonym line
open floor
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.44 exercise 3 on the feet: students physically place themselves between two opposite adjectives

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. You call an adjective from exercise 3 (big-headed! narrow-minded! energetic!).
  2. Line-Ups: the left wall is SYNONYM, the right wall is ANTONYM. Students walk to a wall and, when you point, must produce the word: big-headed — the opposite of modest.
  3. Eight words, ninety seconds. Say aloud the one honest gap: there is no clean opposite for energetic — do not invent one.
  4. Answers to check against: synonym / antonym work: big-headed ↔ modest · cheerful ↔ moody · easy-going ≈ laid-back · humble ≈ modest · introverted ≈ reserved, ↔ outgoing · loyal ↔ untrustworthy · narrow-minded ↔ broad-minded · tactless ↔ tactful. There is no clean opposite for energetic — say so, do not invent one.
📺 On screen: the two wall labels (SYNONYM / ANTONYM) on paper, stuck up before the lesson. Backup: no screen is involved at all — this stage is power-cut-proof by design.
👦 Students do: stand, move, and say the pair aloud when pointed at — everyone moves on every word, so nobody watches.

36–44′
Idioms · eight ways to describe a person in three words
desks → pairs
Why: Idioms are the fastest route from B1 to B2 in speaking, and the profession task forces the class to justify a word choice, which is the same move the exam oral asks for.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.44 exercises 4 and 5 — the idioms, then the professions task

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 4, individually, three minutes. Answers: 1 a party animal · 2 a pain in the neck · 3 a great laugh · 4 a wallflower · 5 a big mouth · 6 a bright spark · 7 a live wire · 8 a social butterfly.
  2. Teach the picture behind two of them so they stick: a wallflower stands by the wall and does not dance. A live wire is an electric cable with the power on — you cannot ignore it.
  3. Exercise 5 · Timed Pair Share: each partner takes one profession (surgeon, teacher, footballer, vlogger) and gets sixty seconds to argue which three adjectives or idioms it needs, and why. Then swap.
  4. Feedback: take two professions only, and push for the reason, not the word: why resilient and not just strong?
📺 On screen: the four professions written on the board. Backup: none needed — the board is the only display.
👦 Students do: complete the idioms alone, then one minute of continuous speaking each — timed, so both partners really speak.

44–52′
🎯 Production · describe someone in this room
standing pairs
Why: The productive outcome of the lesson: the words come out of the mouth about real people, which is the only way they survive to next week.

WHAT WE DO: each student describes a classmate without naming them, using three of today's words; the partner guesses

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the rules in ten seconds: three adjectives or idioms from today, no names, nothing unkind. If it is not something you would say to their face, do not say it at all.
  2. Give sixty seconds of silent writing — three words and one reason each — then stand up.
  3. Mix-Pair-Share: mingle, freeze, nearest person is your partner, describe, guess, swap, mingle again. Three rounds, ninety seconds each.
  4. Collect two descriptions for the whole class and correct them on the board — but correct the ADJECTIVE choice, not the grammar: is he really impetuous, or just energetic?
📺 On screen: the three-word rule and the kindness rule on the board. Backup: this is a standing, book-closed stage — no equipment at all.
👦 Students do: write three words, then speak to three different partners, guessing each time.
board
52–60′
Collocation clinic · the word never travels alone
desks
Why: A B2 word is not learnt until its partners are learnt with it. Splitting this lesson off from the reading is what buys the time to do it properly.

WHAT WE DO: the new words put back into the phrases they actually live in

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board six of today's words bare: broad-minded, down-to-earth, resourceful, impetuous, laid-back, outgoing. Ask the question that matters: What word comes before it? What comes after?
  2. Students hunt the collocations in the unit pages — the word plus its verb, its preposition, its partner noun. Five minutes, in pairs, written as whole phrases.
  3. RallyCoach: one writes the phrase, the partner checks it against the page and says it aloud in a sentence.
  4. Board the three that Russian gets wrong and leave them up for the whole unit. A bare word on the word wall is a mistake waiting to happen.
📺 On screen: the six bare words, then the collocations added around them as they are found. Backup: none — board and books.
👦 Students do: hunt collocations in the unit pages and write them as whole phrases

60–68′
Word building · what the word does when it changes shape
desks
Why: Word formation is the cheapest section of the exam to prepare and the one most often left until spring. Met inside the vocabulary lesson, it costs ten minutes and no extra material.

WHAT WE DO: the same word as a noun, a verb and an adjective — the EGE format, met early

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Take four words from today's set and build the family on the board: noun, verb, adjective, adverb. Leave the gaps the class cannot fill and fill them together from the dictionary.
  2. Students copy the table and mark the stress — it moves, and that is half the pronunciation problem.
  3. Quick drill: you say the noun, the class says the adjective. Two rounds, fast, everybody answering at once.
  4. Say plainly what this is for: This is exactly tasks 25–29 of the EGE — the same word, a different shape.
📺 On screen: the word families being built, column by column. Backup: dictate the four words and build the table on the board.
👦 Students do: build four word families, mark the stress, drill the changes aloud

🎯 Find Someone Who · the words in real questions
open floor
Why: Kagan PIES: everybody speaks in every round, and the words leave the page in the same lesson they arrived on it.

WHAT WE DO: every student asks and answers with the new language, standing up

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand out the grid: eight lines, each one a question built from today's vocabulary about Unit 4 «Our strengths».
  2. Find Someone Who: stand up, ask one person one question, get a full answer with a reason, write the name, move on. Nobody signs twice on the same student.
  3. Eight minutes with a timer. You monitor for the collocation, not for the grammar — this is a vocabulary lesson.
  4. Sit down and report: three students tell the class what they found out about somebody else, in full sentences.
📺 On screen: the timer. Backup: if there is no printed grid, board the eight questions and let them copy — it costs three minutes and works the same.
👦 Students do: ask eight different people eight questions, then report about a partner

desks
Why: The lesson ends in production, so the vocabulary is met, used and personalised on the same day — the only combination that survives to the unit test.

WHAT WE DO: written production before the words go home

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Six sentences about your own life, six different words from today, every one of them a whole phrase. Ten minutes, silent.
  2. Circulate and correct one thing per student — the collocation, not the article.
  3. Timed Pair Share: read three of your sentences to a partner; the partner repeats back the best one.
  4. Collect the notebooks or photograph the page — this is the baseline you compare against in the revision lesson.
📺 On screen: nothing — this stage is books closed, notebooks open. Backup: none needed.
👦 Students do: write six true sentences with today's words, then read three to a partner

82–86′
Exit check · five words, no book
desks
Why: Thirty seconds of retrieval at the end of a vocabulary lesson predicts the test score better than any amount of re-reading.

WHAT WE DO: a thirty-second self-test that tells them what to revise tonight

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Say five definitions; students write the word. No book, no partner.
  2. Answers on the board. Anything wrong goes straight into the error log as the full phrase, not the single word.
  3. Show of hands: who got five? four? fewer? That number is what tonight's workbook page is for.
📺 On screen: the five answers, written up at the end. Backup: none.
👦 Students do: write five words from definitions and mark themselves honestly

Workbook p.30–31
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will be better able to describe people's character using B2 adjectives and idioms, in the context of personality types and introversion.
  • Subsidiary. Reading for gist against the clock and for detail with evidence (four multiple-choice items, SB p.45); guessing meaning from context; word stress in compound adjectives.
  • Personal. Talk less. This lesson has four pair stages — stand at the back during two of them and write down three errors instead of correcting them aloud.
By the end, students can…
  • describe a person with three precise adjectives instead of «nice»
  • use eight personality idioms and explain the picture behind them
  • find the answer to a multiple-choice question and quote the line that proves it
  • work out the meaning of an unknown expression from the sentence around it
  • argue whether a text is fair, with one example

Structure
He's very down-to-earth — he says what he thinks and he doesn't think he's important. · She can be a bit tactless, but she's completely loyal. · He's a real live wire.
Form
adjective + noun (a resourceful person) · be + adjective (she is reserved) · be + a/an + idiom (he's a wallflower). Compound adjectives keep the hyphen: broad-minded, down-to-earth, laid-back, self-confident.
Pron. watch
word stress is what makes these adjectives sound English and it is worth four minutes: broad-MINDed · down-to-EARTH · reSOURCEful · imPETuous · conSIDerate · unTRUSTworthy · self-CONFident. Compound adjectives keep a clear beat on BOTH parts, which is why laid-back and easy-going are so easy to flatten into mush
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
tactful: Do I say what I think? — Yes. Do I hurt people? — No.
reserved: Do I dislike people? — No. Do I show my feelings? — Not much.
impetuous: Do I think first? — No. Do I act fast? — Yes.
ICQ before the gist reading: How many minutes? How many questions? Pens up or down?

Assumptions: Students met basic character adjectives (kind, funny, shy, hard-working) at B1 and can read a 1,000-word text with a clear aim. They have not met compound adjectives as a class of word, and idioms of character are new.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Fifteen adjectives arrive at once and half the class stops recording them.Do not let them copy the list. The record they keep is the exercise itself plus the three sentences they write in the production stage — words tied to a person are remembered, lists are not.
«He is very sociable person» — the missing article.Correct it in the air with one word, no explanation: A sociable person. Articles are a full stage in Lesson 3; today it is a reflex, not a lecture.
The reading is long and weaker students translate it.The timer is the answer, plus the three-step procedure on the board: question first, then hunt. Say aloud that nobody is expected to understand every word.
The «describe a classmate» stage turns unkind.The rule is announced before pens move: nothing you would not say to their face. One reminder, then stop the activity for anyone who breaks it — that costs you thirty seconds and buys the rest of the year.
Introverted students go silent in the mingling stage.They have written their three words first, so they read rather than improvise — and today's text is literally an argument that their way of working is valuable. Name that out loud.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
