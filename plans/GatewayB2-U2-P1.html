🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use about forty items of work vocabulary — conditions and responsibilities (be responsible for, be in charge of, skilled, self-employed, internship), and working life, hours and pay (be made redundant, on flexitime, work overtime, on the minimum wage) — and answer questions about two texts in their own words.
What is genuinely hard
The vocabulary is full of near-synonyms that Russian collapses into one word: resign and retire, be sacked and be made redundant, part-time and flexitime, salary and wage. Students will use them interchangeably unless the difference is made explicit and then tested. The reading difficulty is different: exercise 3 forbids copying from the text, and copying is exactly what a tired B2 student does.
Typical error to expect
«I want to earn a good money», «he gets a big salary money» — money is uncountable; you EARN a salary and MAKE money, and «a money» does not exist.
«I want to be a self-employer», «he was sacked because the company closed» — the second is redundancy, not the sack, and the difference is the whole of exercise 4.
Why the lesson is built this way
The set is huge, so it arrives in two halves with a different job for each: the first half is met inside a text written by a flavourist (a man who invents the taste of yoghurt for a living), the second half is sorted into four functional columns — getting a job, losing one, hours, pay — because that is how it will be retrieved. The reading is two texts on one page and they are quietly the same story: Bolivian cooks who became mountaineers, and an economist who left finance to teach girls to make video games. Question 6 asks what the two have in common, which is where the lesson becomes a conversation rather than an exercise.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Lidia cooked for mountaineers for years. Then she and ten other women put on their traditional skirts, climbed 6,962 metres and stood on top of Aconcagua. Today: everything English says about work — and two texts about people who threw their job away and were right.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every student says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls the previous lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, the Unit 1 test returned quietly, one correction said aloud, and the bridge into work

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. One question each at the door, one full sentence back: One sentence — what did you do at the weekend? Any content counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 2 minutes — the test comes back. Hand back the Unit 1 papers face down, with the score written and nothing else said aloud. Look, do not compare. Two minutes.
  3. 1 minute — one correction each. Think-Pair-Share: find ONE thing you got wrong and tell your partner the right version. That is the whole post-mortem the test gets.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Page eighteen. Unit two — work. And the first person we meet invents the taste of yoghurt for a living.
📺 On screen: nothing on screen. Backup: if the papers are not marked yet, say so honestly and use the two minutes for a Quiz-Quiz-Trade on the Unit 1 word cards instead.
👦 Students do: read their own score, then say one correction aloud

8–14′
A–Z of jobs · six minutes of retrieval
desks in teams
Why: Retrieval of what they already know, at speed, before anything new is added — and the spelling requirement makes the game a test rather than a shout.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.18 ex.1a–1b: one job for every letter, books closed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. Two teams. Team A takes A, team B takes B, and so on round the alphabet. Five seconds per letter, and the job must be SPELLED correctly.
  2. Skip the impossible letters and move on fast — the point is speed, not completeness.
  3. Then ex.1b: Which of your jobs are connected with science or new technology? Feed in cloud engineer, security engineer, software developer if nobody has them.
📺 On screen: the alphabet written across the top of the board, with jobs added under the letters as they are called. Backup: if the board is small, run it orally and keep score on paper.
👦 Students do: call out and spell a job for their letter, then find the technology jobs

14–26′
Vocabulary 1 · the flavourist
desks
Why: A single coherent text is a better container for fifteen words than a list, and the -er/-ee pair is a word-formation point that pays in the exam.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.18 ex.2 (track 05): fifteen words met inside one man's description of his job

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pre-teach only the three the Teacher's Book names: deal with the public (have contact with customers), qualifications (exams you have passed), internship (work experience, often unpaid).
  2. What do you think a flavourist does? Take two guesses, then let the text answer it.
  3. Students complete the fifteen gaps alone, six minutes. Then play track 05 to check and to hear the stress.
  4. Board the -er / -ee pair and drill it: emPLOYer — employEE. TRAINer — trainEE. INterviewer — interviewEE. The stress moves and the person changes.
  5. Take three answers with the whole phrase, never the bare word: responsible FOR quality, IN CHARGE OF a team.
📺 On screen: SB p.18 on the board app if you have it. Backup for the audio: if track 05 will not play, read the fifteen answers aloud twice at natural speed — the key is in the plan below.
👦 Students do: complete fifteen gaps alone, self-check against the recording, drill the -er/-ee stress

26–35′
Vocabulary 2 · four columns
desks
Why: Sorting by function is what makes a large set retrievable, and the six near-synonym pairs are precisely where Russian interference lives.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.18 ex.3–4 (track 06): sort twenty expressions, then defend the differences

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pre-teach the four the Teacher's Book flags: be made redundant, on flexitime, be sacked, do shift work.
  2. Ex.3 in pairs, four minutes: sort the twenty expressions into the four columns. Play track 06 to confirm.
  3. Ex.4 is the important one — six pairs that Russian merges. Do resign/retire together, then pairs do the rest.
  4. Numbered Heads Together for the check: the team agrees, then the number you call explains the difference to the room. Key: resign is your decision, retire is age; redundant is the company's problem, sacked is yours; part-time is fewer hours, flexitime is your choice of hours.
  5. The four columns on the wall get filled in by the students who answered.
📺 On screen: track 06 and the four wall columns. Backup: read the four lists aloud from the plan below and let them tick.
👦 Students do: sort twenty expressions in pairs, then explain six differences for the team

🎯 In your own words · the exam discipline of the lesson
desks
Why: Paraphrasing is the sub-skill that both exams pay for, and it only develops when copying is explicitly forbidden and then checked by a peer.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.19 ex.3 (track 07): six questions, and not one answer may be copied

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the rule before they write: You may not copy a phrase from the text. Change the words.
  2. Do question 1 together, exactly as the Teacher's Book suggests: elicit synonyms for start, climb and peaks (begin, go up, mountains) BEFORE anyone writes.
  3. Eight minutes alone. Give the line references to the students who need them (see Problems, below).
  4. RallyCoach: A reads answers 1–3, B checks for copied phrases and nothing else; then swap for 4–6.
  5. Whole-class check on questions 4 and 6 only — the two that require inference. Question 6 (what the two women have in common) is the one worth three minutes of discussion.
  6. Optional: play track 07 under a second silent reading for the weaker half.
📺 On screen: track 07 available for a supported second reading. Backup: if the audio fails, the students simply reread — the recording is support, not the task.
👦 Students do: answer six questions in their own words, then check a partner's answers for copied phrases

57–60′
Move break · would you take this job?
open floor
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Sixteen-year-olds who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: three minutes standing, and every step is a decision

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two walls: YES, I'D TAKE IT · NO, NEVER. Read a job with conditions attached, students walk.
  2. Six items: night shifts, very well paid · outdoors, minimum wage · high-pressure, big promotion after a year · manual work with friendly colleagues · a six-month unpaid internship at a famous company · working from home, no colleagues at all.
  3. One student in each wall gives a one-sentence reason after each item.
📺 On screen: two wall labels. Backup: hands up left / right if the room is fixed.
👦 Students do: walk to a wall six times and justify one choice
two A4 wall labels
60–65′
Collocation clinic · the word never travels alone
desks
Why: A B2 word is not learnt until its partners are learnt with it. Splitting this lesson off from the reading is what buys the time to do it properly.

WHAT WE DO: the new words put back into the phrases they actually live in

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board six of today's words bare: career, colleague, deal with the public, earn a salary, employee, employer. Ask the question that matters: What word comes before it? What comes after?
  2. Students hunt the collocations in the unit pages — the word plus its verb, its preposition, its partner noun. Five minutes, in pairs, written as whole phrases.
  3. RallyCoach: one writes the phrase, the partner checks it against the page and says it aloud in a sentence.
  4. Board the three that Russian gets wrong and leave them up for the whole unit. A bare word on the word wall is a mistake waiting to happen.
📺 On screen: the six bare words, then the collocations added around them as they are found. Backup: none — board and books.
👦 Students do: hunt collocations in the unit pages and write them as whole phrases
Student's Book, notebooks, board
65–71′
Word building · what the word does when it changes shape
desks
Why: Word formation is the cheapest section of the exam to prepare and the one most often left until spring. Met inside the vocabulary lesson, it costs ten minutes and no extra material.

WHAT WE DO: the same word as a noun, a verb and an adjective — the EGE format, met early

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Take four words from today's set and build the family on the board: noun, verb, adjective, adverb. Leave the gaps the class cannot fill and fill them together from the dictionary.
  2. Students copy the table and mark the stress — it moves, and that is half the pronunciation problem.
  3. Quick drill: you say the noun, the class says the adjective. Two rounds, fast, everybody answering at once.
  4. Say plainly what this is for: This is exactly tasks 25–29 of the EGE — the same word, a different shape.
📺 On screen: the word families being built, column by column. Backup: dictate the four words and build the table on the board.
👦 Students do: build four word families, mark the stress, drill the changes aloud
Student's Book, dictionaries or phones, board
71–77′
🎯 Find Someone Who · the words in real questions
open floor
Why: Kagan PIES: everybody speaks in every round, and the words leave the page in the same lesson they arrived on it.

WHAT WE DO: every student asks and answers with the new language, standing up

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand out the grid: eight lines, each one a question built from today's vocabulary about Unit 2 «A job well done».
  2. Find Someone Who: stand up, ask one person one question, get a full answer with a reason, write the name, move on. Nobody signs twice on the same student.
  3. Eight minutes with a timer. You monitor for the collocation, not for the grammar — this is a vocabulary lesson.
  4. Sit down and report: three students tell the class what they found out about somebody else, in full sentences.
📺 On screen: the timer. Backup: if there is no printed grid, board the eight questions and let them copy — it costs three minutes and works the same.
👦 Students do: ask eight different people eight questions, then report about a partner

🎯 Six sentences that are true about you
desks
Why: The lesson ends in production, so the vocabulary is met, used and personalised on the same day — the only combination that survives to the unit test.

WHAT WE DO: written production before the words go home

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Six sentences about your own life, six different words from today, every one of them a whole phrase. Ten minutes, silent.
  2. Circulate and correct one thing per student — the collocation, not the article.
  3. Timed Pair Share: read three of your sentences to a partner; the partner repeats back the best one.
  4. Collect the notebooks or photograph the page — this is the baseline you compare against in the revision lesson.
📺 On screen: nothing — this stage is books closed, notebooks open. Backup: none needed.

Exit check · five words, no book
desks
Why: Thirty seconds of retrieval at the end of a vocabulary lesson predicts the test score better than any amount of re-reading.

WHAT WE DO: a thirty-second self-test that tells them what to revise tonight

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Say five definitions; students write the word. No book, no partner.
  2. Answers on the board. Anything wrong goes straight into the error log as the full phrase, not the single word.
  3. Show of hands: who got five? four? fewer? That number is what tonight's workbook page is for.
📺 On screen: the five answers, written up at the end. Backup: none.
👦 Students do: write five words from definitions and mark themselves honestly

Structure
I'm responsible for the day-to-day running of the shop. · She's in charge of a team of ten. · He was made redundant when the factory closed. · I'd like a job where I can work outdoors and deal with the public.
Form
collocations and prepositions: responsible for · in charge of · apply for a job · on flexitime · on the minimum wage · earn a salary (never «earn a money») · be made redundant (passive, always).
Pron. watch
the -er / -ee pair moves the stress and changes the person: employer vs employee, trainer vs trainee, interviewer vs interviewee · the past-perfect contraction is one syllable: I'd been /aɪd bɪn/ · linking in the negotiating phrases: «I agree_up to_a point», «What_about_you?»
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«She resigned.» Was it her decision? → yes. «She was made redundant.» Her decision? → no, and she did nothing wrong.
«He was sacked.» Did he do something wrong? → yes, usually.
«On flexitime» — does she work fewer hours? → not necessarily; she chooses WHEN she works.
ICQ before reading exercise 3: can you copy the sentence from the text? → no. What do you do instead? → say the same idea with different words.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Forty new items in one lesson is too many to holdIt is, and you do not pretend otherwise: the aim is recognition today and production of about twelve. Name the twelve you will test (they are the ones in the workbook) and let the rest sit in the book.
Answers to exercise 3 are copied out of the textRefuse the first copied answer publicly and kindly, then do it together: elicit synonyms for start, climb and peaks before they write anything. The Teacher's Book asks for exactly this.
Weak students cannot find where the answers areGive the line references the Teacher's Book supplies: question 1 from «One day …», 2 from «They learned to …», 3 from «She then decided …», 5 from «She aims to …». It is support, not cheating.
Fast finishers in the readingThe Teacher's Book task: rewrite five phrases from the first two paragraphs in different words (a radical move, all over the world, look after, prepared to climb, exactly).
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 10–18 (reading) and tasks 26–31 (word formation) — work and career is one of the fixed topics of the Russian paper.

Question 3 on page 19 asks for answers IN YOUR OWN WORDS, which is the single most useful discipline for the Russian reading section: an answer copied word for word out of the text is usually the wrong answer, because the exam paraphrases. The vocabulary itself is exam money: qualifications, be responsible for, be in charge of, earn a salary, be made redundant, self-employed. Half of them are word families — employ, employer, employee, unemployed, employment — which is exactly what tasks 26–31 test.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
