🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Talk about five pictures for two minutes with a partner — saying what you can see, giving a reason for every opinion, and asking the other person what they think — and then say the same content alone, from a plan.
What is genuinely hard
Two opposite things. With a partner: not going silent while the other person talks, because bringing your partner in earns marks. Alone: keeping going for ninety seconds with nobody to react to, which is a completely different kind of difficulty and the reason this lesson exists.
Typical error to expect
A one-word reply («Karate.», «Yes.») does not count as an answer at all: an answer needs a verb in it [national exam report]
«I don't know» / «I haven't got a favourite» and full stop — a bare negative is marked as an unanswered point; it needs development: …but I like chess [exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
This is the one lesson of the block where the exam format IS the topic, and it is also the one lesson of the year where the two exams genuinely differ — so it is framed from minute five as «one skill, two wrappings», and the difference is stated honestly rather than smoothed over. The vocabulary of the five pictures is fixed before anybody has to perform, because a candidate with no word for what is in the picture cannot be assessed on anything else. Two real candidates are heard doing the task before the class attempts it: hearing that neither of them is fluent is the single most reassuring thing in the lesson. The distractor-equivalent stage here is the short-answer clinic at 64′, which is where the actual teaching happens — a short answer is not wrong, it is empty, and there are exactly four ways to fill it. The last fourteen minutes do the Russian version live: same five pictures, one student, a plan on the board, ninety seconds.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Today you do a Cambridge speaking task — and then the Russian version of the same thing. And for the first time this year they are genuinely not the same: in one of them you have a partner, and in the other you talk to a computer in an empty room. Everything else transfers. One warning: a short answer is not a wrong answer. It is an empty one, and it scores nothing.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, three minutes of collective feedback on the block test, and the bridge into the exam lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — test feedback, not a post-mortem. Hand the papers back face down. On the board, the three errors that cost the class the most marks, no names. Turn your paper over. Find one of these three in your own work. That is your line for today.
  3. Nothing else about the test today: anyone who wants to argue a mark sees you afterwards.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page one hundred and thirty-four. Last exam lesson of the year — and the only one where the two exams are really different.
📺 On screen: the three most expensive errors written in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: take their papers back and find one of the three in their own work. Expect relief that no score is read out. Typical problem: somebody wants to compare marks out loud — one line, «see me after», and move on

Two exams, one skill
board
Why: The map of the lesson, drawn before anything else happens. Today is the one lesson of the year where the two exams really are different, and saying so at minute five is what makes the rest of the lesson honest.

WHAT WE DO: the map of the lesson, and the one honest difference

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Draw two boxes on the board. In the left one: five pictures · you and a partner · an examiner in the room · 5–6 minutes. In the right one: a plan on a screen · you alone · a computer · 90 seconds.
  2. One of these is British. One is Russian. This time you can tell which is which — and that is the point of today.
  3. Say the honest thing, in full, once: ⚠️ The honest part. Speaking Part 2 has no analogue in the Russian exam. There the speaking paper is a monologue: a plan appears on the screen, the student speaks 10–12 sentences to a computer, and there is no partner and no examiner in the room at all (speaking task 3, seven marks). So we keep the pair discussion because it is live, useful language and because talking to a person is the point of learning to talk. And then we do the same content in the other wrapper: the same five places, but one student, a plan, and ninety seconds. What transfers is everything except the partner — the adjectives, the reasons, the «why» after every opinion.
  4. Then say what does transfer, because it is almost everything: the adjectives, the reason after every opinion, and never answering in one word.
  5. The plan of the lesson in three lines on the board: what is in the pictures → two real candidates → your turn → the Russian version.
📺 On screen: the two boxes and the three-line plan in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: compare the two boxes and see, for the first time this year, that they are not the same task. Expect one student to ask which one they will take — answer factually, in one sentence, and move on. Typical problem: anxiety about the monologue; the answer is that they will do one today and it lasts ninety seconds

9–20′
Speaking Part 2 · format
class → pairs
Why: The five pictures get their vocabulary before anyone has to perform. A candidate who has no word for what is in the picture cannot be assessed on anything else.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.134–135: the exam information and tips, and the vocabulary of the five pictures

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The format in two lines, from the box: «There is a conversation with your partner and the examiner about some pictures. You need to answer the examiner's questions and talk to your partner as well.» Five pictures of places you can visit; the real part lasts five to six minutes.
  2. The four strategies, one line each, straight from the book's Exam tips box: say what you can see — let the examiner hear how much vocabulary you have, and use adjectives instead of yes and no · try to make correct sentences and questions, but do not stop for small mistakes · let your partner speak too, and ask them questions — you get marks for that · try to relax and enjoy it
  3. ex.1: books open at p.135. They are all places you can visit. Work with a partner and say what each one is. Key: The five pictures are a museum, a park, a castle, a cinema and a shopping centre..
  4. Then the five detail questions, so that nobody runs out of words later: TB p.259, five questions to ask about the pictures before anything is played, so that students have the words when their own turn comes: What are they looking at in the museum? (maybe Greek vases) What are they doing in the park? (chatting and reading) What are the people looking at? (a castle) Why are they wearing glasses in the cinema? (it is a 3D film) Which shop has special low prices? (Bags and Accessories).
  5. And the strategy that is worth more than the other four put together: TB p.259: you do not have to talk about the pictures in the order they are printed. Start with the picture you can say the most about — it means you begin well and you feel confident, and the examiner hears your best language first.
📺 On screen: the five pictures from SB p.135 on the screen, enlarged (Backup: they are printed on SB p.135 in colour — students work from the book in pairs and you write the five place words on the board)
👦 Students do: name the five places, then find details in them. Expect castle and shopping centre to be known and the detail questions to produce almost nothing at first. Typical problem: they answer the detail questions in one word — which is exactly the habit today is about

Listening · two candidates
class
Why: Two real candidates doing the task is worth more than any description of it. They hear the length, the turn-taking and the fact that neither of them is fluent — which is the most reassuring thing in the lesson.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2–3 p.134 (track 161): two real students doing this task

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set it up in one line: Two students, the same five pictures, the same examiner. Listen to how long they talk and how often they stop.
  2. ex.2: play track 161. Number the pictures in the order they talk about them. Key: They speak about the shopping centre, the museum, the park, the castle and the cinema, in that order..
  3. Ask the question that makes ex.2 worth doing: Is that the order they are printed in? (no) So why did they start with the shopping centre? — because it is the one they could say most about.
  4. ex.3: play track 161 again. Fill the table — who likes what, tick and cross. Key: Girl: likes shopping centres, museums, parks and cinemas. Boy: likes parks, castles and cinemas; does not like shopping centres and museums..
  5. Kagan Showdown to check the table: boards up, all together, one row at a time.
📺 On screen: track 161 in the board app with the empty table on the screen (Backup: the audioscript is on TB p.308 — read the two candidates' parts yourself, twice, using two different voices; the table is printed in the students' own book)
👦 Students do: listen twice and fill a two-column table. Expect them to notice that the candidates hesitate and repeat themselves, which is the most useful thing in the stage. Typical error: they fill in what they think rather than what the speakers said

32–40′
Adjectives and bringing your partner in
class → pairs
Why: Two things earn marks in this part and neither is grammar: the adjectives you use about the pictures, and the questions you ask your partner. Both are collected from the recording rather than handed down.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 p.134: what adjectives the speakers used, and how they passed the turn

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4: What adjective did they use about each place? Replay fragments of track 161 if needed. Key: shopping centre — boring · museum — interesting · park — fun · cinema — expensive.
  2. Push it one step further than the book does: Give me a different adjective for each place. Any adjective, as long as it isn't «good» or «nice». Collect eight or nine on the board and leave them there.
  3. Now the second thing that earns marks, and it is not grammar: TB p.259: collect the phrases the two candidates use to bring each other in — What about you? · And you? · How about you? · Do you agree? — and put them on the board. In this part, asking your partner a question earns marks; silence while your partner talks does not.
  4. Kagan Timed Pair Share, 30 seconds each way, on one picture only: opinion, reason, then hand over with one of the four phrases. Twice, with different partners.
  5. Name the standard for the next stage: Every opinion has a reason. Every turn ends with a question to your partner.
📺 On screen: the adjectives and the four partner questions collected in chalk in two columns, with fragments of track 161 replayed on request (Backup: the four adjectives are the ones in the key above and the audioscript is on TB p.308 — read the line that contains each adjective yourself instead of replaying it; the collecting is done on the board either way)
👦 Students do: harvest adjectives from the recording, then practise passing the turn. Expect the hand-over phrases to feel artificial for about ninety seconds and then to stick. Typical error: they ask the question and then answer it themselves

42–54′
Exam conditions · your turn
pairs, timed
Why: The task run for real, with the examiner's exact words and a timer. A strategy that has not survived two minutes under a clock is not a strategy yet.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5 p.134: the pair discussion, run for real with the examiner's own words and a clock

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the interlocutor's instruction exactly as it is printed, including the repetition — this is what makes it feel like the real thing: TB p.259 gives the examiner's exact words so that the practice sounds like the real thing: Now, in this part of the test you are going to talk together. Here are some pictures that show different places to visit. Do you like these different places to visit? Say why or why not. I'll say that again. Do you like these different places to visit? Say why or why not. The person who says this is called the interlocutor, and the instruction is always repeated once.
  2. Say the timing before you start it: The pair talk together for a minimum of one minute and a maximum of two, then the examiner asks the «Do you think …?» prompts, then asks each candidate which place they like best. Phase 2 is up to two more minutes of follow-up questions.
  3. Run it: pairs talk about the five pictures, two minutes on the clock, visible timer. You walk the room and write down good phrases and errors, saying nothing.
  4. Then the examiner's follow-up, to each pair or to the class: Which of these places do you like visiting best? Say why.
  5. Repeat the whole task once in DIFFERENT pairs — TB p.259 says to, and it is the single highest-value repetition in the lesson, because the second run is always twice as long as the first.
  6. If you want new content for the second run rather than the same five pictures: The A2 Key Speaking bank in the course folder (speaking bank tasks + answer key + seventeen mp3 files) has more picture sets of exactly this shape. Use one of them for the second run in different pairs, so the format is repeated on new content rather than the same five pictures twice.
📺 On screen: the five pictures on the screen and a visible two-minute timer (Backup: the pictures are printed on SB p.135 — put the book open in the middle of each pair's desk, which is exactly how the real exam does it, and time it on your phone)
👦 Students do: talk in pairs for two minutes under a clock, twice. Expect the first run to last about fifty seconds and the second to fill the two minutes. Typical error: they talk about the pictures in printed order and run out on picture two — the «start with your best one» strategy is the fix

54–64′
Phase 2 · the follow-up questions
class → pairs
Why: The second half of the part, which students never expect: after the pictures come opinion questions to both candidates. Hearing the order they come in removes the surprise.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6–7 p.134 (track 162): the second half of the part, which nobody expects

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed: After the pictures, the examiner asks you both some questions about the same topic. Write down three questions you think they could ask. Give an example: Which places can you visit for free?
  2. Books open, ex.6: answer the book's «Do you think …?» prompts in pairs — museum boring, castle interesting, cinema expensive, shopping fun, park pleasant. Every answer needs a reason.
  3. ex.7: play track 162. Number the three questions in the order you hear them. Key: The order the follow-up questions are asked in: Do you like places that teach you about history? Why? → Do you prefer visiting places that are inside or outside? Why? → Do you prefer visiting places on your own or with other people? Why?.
  4. Kagan Numbered Heads Together to answer all three properly: each pair takes one question and produces an answer with a reason, and the number called reads it out.
📺 On screen: track 162 in the board app with the three follow-up questions on the screen (Backup: the three questions are printed on SB p.134 and the audioscript is on TB p.308 — read the examiner's part yourself and have the class order the questions from the book)
👦 Students do: predict the questions, then hear them. Expect their predictions to be close, which is worth pointing out — the questions are not mysterious. Typical error: they answer «inside» with no reason, which is exactly what the next stage is for

64–72′
Short answers and how to expand
pairs → class
Why: The single highest-value eight minutes of the lesson. A short answer is not wrong, it is empty — and the four ways of expanding it are learnable in one sitting.

WHAT WE DO: ex.8–9 p.134 (track 162): what the examiner does with a short answer, and the four ways out

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.8: play the last part of track 162 again. What happens when the candidate gives a very short answer? Key: What happens if a candidate gives a very short answer: the examiner asks Why? or Why not? and you have to expand it yourself. Ways to start the expansion, from TB p.259: Because … · I'm not sure but … · I think … · It seems to me that ….
  2. Write the four expansions on the board, big, and drill them: Because … · I'm not sure but … · I think … · It seems to me that …
  3. Then the drill that makes it automatic. You say a short answer, the class expands it out loud, all together: «Yes.» … «No.» … «Museums.» … «I don't know.» Four rounds, chorally, then four rounds individually.
  4. Kagan RallyCoach, 3 minutes: partner A gives a deliberately short answer, partner B says Why?, partner A expands it with one of the four. Swap.
  5. ex.9: pairs ask and answer the ex.6 questions again, and this time nobody is allowed to stop before the reason.
📺 On screen: the four expansions in chalk, large, in the middle of the board, with track 162 available for the last fragment (Backup: read the examiner's «Why?» exchange from the audioscript on TB p.308 yourself — the drill needs your voice, not a recording)
👦 Students do: hear the trap, then drill the way out of it. Expect this to be the stage they remember from the lesson. Typical error: I don't know expanded into I don't know, sorry — push for content: …but I think parks are better

🎯 OGE version · the monologue
solo → pairs
Why: The transfer, done rather than promised: the same five places, one student, a plan on the board and ninety seconds. What is different from the Cambridge task is felt, not explained.

WHAT WE DO: the same five places, one student, a plan on the board and ninety seconds

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Open the school trainer at the mock section on your own login and show ONE speaking task 3 screen, so they see what it actually looks like: a plan, a countdown, no examiner.
  2. Then write the plan on the board in the Russian exam's own shape: The plan for the monologue, written on the board in the shape the Russian exam uses: Talk about a place you like visiting. Say · what kind of place it is and where it is · how often you go there and who with · what you can do there · why you like it · and end with a sentence that closes the topic. Twelve sentences, two minutes to prepare, ninety seconds to speak.
  3. Name the two differences that matter, and nothing else: Nobody answers you. And you have to end it — a monologue that just stops loses a mark. Give them the ending sentence to steal: That's why I like going there.
  4. Two minutes of silent preparation, notes allowed, then Kagan Timed Pair Share: 90 seconds each way, partner listens with the five plan points in front of them and ticks the ones that were covered.
  5. Two volunteers do it to the whole class, standing, with the plan behind them.
  6. Close the comparison out loud: Same five places. Same adjectives. Same reasons. The only thing that changed is whether anybody answers you.
📺 On screen: the trainer's mock speaking screen open on the teacher's own login, shown once, and the five-point plan written in chalk beside it (Backup: the plan on the board and a phone timer are the whole task — the trainer screen is a demonstration, not the activity, and if it will not open, describe it in one sentence and run the monologue exactly as planned)
👦 Students do: prepare for two minutes, then speak alone for ninety seconds. Expect the first thirty seconds to be fluent and the last thirty to be a struggle — that is the actual finding of the lesson and it is worth naming kindly. Typical error: they stop at sixty seconds because they have run out of plan, not out of language

Structure
I like going to the museum because it's interesting. · I don't really like shopping centres — they're boring. · What about you? · I prefer visiting places that are outside, because …
Form
opinion + reason, every time: because … · I'm not sure but … · I think … · It seems to me that … Questions to the partner: What about you? · And you? · How about you? · Do you agree?
Pron. watch
the falling intonation of a finished opinion against the rising one of a question back to the partner. This is what makes a discussion sound like a discussion rather than two monologues taking turns.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Do you like these places?» Is «yes» an answer? → no. It is an empty answer and it scores nothing.
How long do the two of you talk together? → between one and two minutes. Less than one is too short.
Do you have to talk about the pictures in the printed order? → no. Start with the one you can say most about.
Your partner has stopped talking after four words. What do you do? → ask them a question. You get marks for that.
In the Russian version, who is in the room with you? → nobody. That is the whole difference.
In the Russian monologue, how many sentences? → ten to twelve, and it has to have an ending, not just stop.

One student in the pair says nothing for two minutesThis is the failure mode of the whole task and it is fixable with one phrase. Put the four partner questions on the board and make them a rule: If your partner stops, you ask. Silence costs you both marks, not just them.
The internet is down at 72′ and the mock will not openIt does not matter. The Russian monologue needs a plan and a clock, nothing else — write the five-point plan on the board in advance and time it on your phone. That is the whole task.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

The same skill is tested by: Speaking task 3 — a monologue from a plan, 10–12 sentences, seven marks.

This is the lesson where the exam format IS the topic, and it is also the one lesson of the year where the two exams genuinely differ. The Cambridge task is a conversation with a partner; the Russian task is a monologue delivered to a computer with nobody in the room. We keep the pair discussion because it teaches language, and we put the monologue next to it so the difference is experienced rather than described.
