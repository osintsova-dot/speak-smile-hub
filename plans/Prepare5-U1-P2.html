🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use compounds and phrases with any (anyone, anything, anywhere, any more, any longer, any better, any good) with the right verb around them, and write a short text of about 80 words that answers every question asked and adds one line of extra information.
What is genuinely hard
any changes meaning depending on the verb in front of it: «I'll eat anything» is generous, «I won't eat anything» is the opposite. Russian has no such switch. And in writing, the habitual failure is not grammar at all — it is answering two of the three questions and stopping.
Typical error to expect
«I can eat anything» vs «I can't eat anything» — the same word, two opposite meanings, decided by the verb. Students hear any and write «some kind of», which loses the sentence.
«We don't eat no special food» — one negative per sentence in English. The book's own Grammar ex.4 plants this trap, and the Teacher's Book asks you to name it out loud.
Why the lesson is built this way
Vocabulary first and fast, because it is needed inside the writing: three of the model texts use any-phrases in their best sentences (I couldn't wait any longer · I'm not telling anyone my secret store). Then model-first writing: the class mines three real competition entries for what makes them work — the questions answered, the determiners from yesterday, two tenses in one paragraph, and the extra information — before anyone writes a word. The text is started AND finished in class, because a checklist that goes home unused teaches nothing.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“A magazine wants eighty words about the last cool thing you bought. Three teenagers won with the texts on page thirteen. Today: why those three won — and how to write one that answers everything and still says something they didn't ask for.”

door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, three minutes of repairing yesterday's own errors, and the bridge into the writing lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what's the best thing about today so far? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — delayed feedback from lesson 1. Four sentences on the board, taken from yesterday's survey, two of them wrong: How much shops are there? · I haven't got many money. · There are plenty of websites. · She spends a lot of money on trainers.
  3. Pairs, 60 seconds: find the two that are wrong and repair them. Take the repairs from two different pairs, then rub the wrong versions off the board — visibly.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page twelve, bottom half. One small word today — any — and then you're writing eighty words for a magazine.
📺 On screen: the four sentences in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: spot, repair, say the corrected sentence aloud. The errors are theirs, which is exactly why nobody argues with the correction

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and put the writing checklist up before anything else

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the three-line checklist that will govern the second half and leave it up: 1 Answer EVERY question. 2 Add one thing they didn't ask. 3 Use more than one tense.
  2. Say the shape in 30 seconds: fifteen minutes on any, then three winning texts, then their own.
  3. Set the bar: Today's success is not «I finished». It's «my partner found all three things on the checklist in my text».

Vocabulary · compounds with any
class → individual
Why: Vocabulary · compounds with any

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: the rule discovered from three sentences, then eight items

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write ANY on the board. Sixty seconds of brainstorm: which word do we use for a person? (anyone) a thing? (anything) a place? (anywhere).
  2. ex.1: read the three examples aloud and let the class complete the rules. Then make the rule audible with a pair of sentences: I'll eat anything. I won't eat anything. Ask which one is a hungry person.
  3. ex.2 with books closed: what other phrases with any do you know? Collect any more, any longer, any better, any good on the board.
  4. ex.3 individually (EP). Teach the trick behind three of the items before they start: isn't any + adjective is a strengthened negative — Today's lunch isn't any better means not at all better.
  5. Check by nomination, one item each, no hands.
📺 On screen: the any-family on the board in three columns (people · things · places) — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: brainstorm, complete the rules, then work alone. Everyone answers at least once because the check is by nomination

Vocabulary · the any pairwork

Why: Vocabulary · the any pairwork

WHAT WE DO: the book's own information gap (p.135) run as Quiz-Quiz-Trade

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Direct them to SB page 135 (Extra activities, Unit 1). Demonstrate the first question with a strong student in open class, and give feedback on the ANSWER, not the question: full sentences, one detail added.
  2. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade: stand, ask, answer, swap, move on. Three partners minimum.
  3. Listen for the two predictable errors — «I didn't buy nothing» and «Is it good any?» — and write them (anonymously) on the board as you monitor.
  4. Sit. Repair the two boarded sentences as a class in 60 seconds.
📺 On screen: SB p.135 in every student's hands; the two error sentences appear on the board as they work — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: ask and answer standing, three partners each; then repair the class's own errors

Move break · anywhere, anyone, anything
standing circle
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that keeps the new words in the mouth

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Class in a circle. You name a category — a place you can't go · a person you can't see · a thing you can't buy — and throw a soft object.
  2. Whoever catches it makes a sentence with an any-word: I can't go anywhere without my phone. Then throws it on.
  3. Ninety seconds per category, no repeats allowed. If a sentence breaks, the class repairs it in one voice and the ball moves on.
  4. Finish standing and go straight into the writing half — they sit down at the reading of the competition advert.
📺 On screen: no technology in this stage — a ball and a circle, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: everyone speaks at least twice; the no-repeats rule forces listening to each other

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: the competition, then the three published entries

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: read the competition advert together. Ask the question that matters: What exactly do you have to write about? Get all three parts named and write them on the board as a numbered list — they become the marking scheme.
  2. ex.2: read the three texts (Maksim, Emily, JD), two minutes. Show of hands: which purchase is the coolest? Two students explain why, in English.
  3. Quick meaning check on the words the texts hand them for free: cool, stuff, customer service, a free case, saving up for.
  4. Pairs, one minute: which of the three texts answers the questions best? Do not settle it yet — that is the next stage.
📺 On screen: the three texts on screen if you have the digital book, otherwise the page. Backup: the printed page is the primary source here; no technology is required
👦 Students do: read, choose, argue. Fast finishers get the TB task: write two sentences about where they would buy those things

Mining the models
pairs → class
Why: Mining the models

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4: what the three texts actually do — vocabulary, determiners, tenses, extra information

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 with three different jobs, one per pair (Kagan-style division of labour): in Maksim's text underline the shopping vocabulary; in Emily's underline every determiner from page 12; in JD's find the two tenses.
  2. Report back in that order. Emily's determiners are the surprise — any longer, several, some, plenty of, anyone in nine lines, which is yesterday's grammar doing real work.
  3. ex.4: JD's text again. Which sentences answer the competition questions? Underline them. What is left over? That is the extra information — four sentences of it.
  4. Point at the Prepare to write box and read it aloud once: answer all the questions · add some extra information · use a range of tenses and structures. It is the checklist from the board, printed in the book.
📺 On screen: one model text enlarged on screen for the whole-class part of ex.4 if you have it. Backup: do it on the page and mark up the sentences on the board instead — same result
👦 Students do: each pair mines one text and teaches the class what they found. Everyone marks up all three texts in their own book
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE task 37 (the personal email). Answer every question, add extra information, keep the register informal — the three model texts do exactly what the exam email is marked for.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: read one real exam prompt aloud (three questions from a pen-friend) and have pairs plan an answer in note form for five minutes before the writing stage. Their own text then follows the same plan.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5: the plan — five questions answered in note form

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books at ex.5. The five prompts are on the page; put them on the board as well and add the rule: Notes only. If I see a full sentence in your plan, you're writing too early.
  2. Three minutes of silence for notes. Circulate and ask one question per student, quietly: What's your extra information going to be?
  3. Kagan Think-Pair-Share: 60 seconds each way, tell your partner your plan, and the partner asks ONE question that the plan does not answer. That question usually becomes the best line of the text.
📺 On screen: the five plan prompts on the board — no gadgets, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: make notes alone in silence, then talk them through with one partner and improve them

individual, silent
Why: 🎯 Writing out · about 80 words

WHAT WE DO: ex.6: the real text, written and finished in class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Silent mode, palm down: eighteen minutes, no talking, phones face down. The checklist is on the board and the Prepare to write box is open on the page.
  2. Give the target as a range, on the board: 75–90 words. Count them at the end and write the number.
  3. Circulate with a pen but do not rewrite: put a small dot in the margin of any line with an error and let the writer hunt it. Say it once at the start so nobody panics at the dots.
  4. At minute 14 call the two-minute warning and then the rule: Last two minutes are for checking spelling and the word count, not for new sentences.
📺 On screen: the visible timer and the three-line checklist — both on the board, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write about 80 words alone, in silence, and finish. This is the productive outcome of the lesson: nothing goes home unfinished

78–86′
Peer review + polish
pairs
Why: Peer review + polish

WHAT WE DO: a three-question checklist swap, then two minutes to repair

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Swap texts. The reader answers three questions IN WRITING at the bottom of the page: which sentence answers question three? which sentence is the extra information? how many tenses did you find?
  2. If any answer is «none», the reader draws an arrow to the place where it should go. Nobody corrects grammar in someone else's text — that is the teacher's job.
  3. Two minutes back with your own text: repair what your reader could not find.
  4. Collect the texts. Read out two anonymous best sentences and say exactly why they work: Listen to this one. She answers the question and then tells us something nobody asked.
📺 On screen: no technology in this stage — paper and pens, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: read someone else's text with a job to do, then repair their own. Both sides learn from the catch

Structure
I couldn't wait any longer, so I bought them myself. · I'm not telling anyone my secret store. · Is there anywhere near here where I can buy speakers? · This book isn't any good.
Form
any + one/thing/where for people, things, places; any + comparative or adjective (any better, any good, any longer) strengthens a negative — «not … any good» means not at all.
Pron. watch
the two words run together and the stress lands on the second part: any-THING, any-WHERE, any LONGer. Students who stress ANY sound like they are arguing
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I don't mind — any colour will do.» Is that one colour or all of them? → any of them, it doesn't matter which. Positive verb, positive meaning.
«I didn't buy anything.» How many things did I buy? → zero. Negative verb, any means nothing.
«Is this film any good?» Am I asking about quality or quantity? → quality. Any + adjective in a question means «at all».

Assumptions: SB p.12 (bottom)–13, syllabus lesson 5. Determiners were built yesterday from the same article, so any is already half-known — this lesson only adds the compounds. Past simple and present simple are secure from Prepare 4; the present continuous for now is the only tense that needs watching in the writing.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students write forty words and say they have finishedThe plan is the answer: four notes on paper before any sentence is written, and the word count is on the board as a target with a range (75–90). Anyone who finishes early gets the fast-finisher task, not permission to stop.
The extra information disappearsIt is the one thing the checklist checks first. In peer review the reader has to point at the extra sentence with a finger; if there isn't one, the writer adds it there and then.
Everything is written in the past simpleThe model does the teaching: JD's text has past simple, present simple and one present continuous in six lines. Ask the class to count the tenses before they write, and put «two tenses minimum» on the board.
A pair finishes peer review in thirty secondsThey were reading, not checking. Give the checklist as three questions with a written answer: which sentence answers question three? which sentence is extra? how many tenses did you find?
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
