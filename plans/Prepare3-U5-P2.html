🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Answer five multiple-choice questions on a long text by checking the options against the text rather than against memory, and agree or disagree with a statement and say why.
What is genuinely hard
A long text with five questions is the biggest reading load of the course so far. The options are paraphrases, so the word that is in the text is usually in the WRONG option.
Typical error to expect
They pick the option that repeats a word from the text. The correct option is almost always paraphrased; the wrong ones copy the wording [exam report]
«I don't know» / «I haven't got a favourite» and full stop — a bare negative is marked as an unanswered point; it needs development: …but I like chess [exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
The argument comes before the text, not after it: the lead-in question «is this a sport?» is the one the whole lesson runs on, and having taken a side they read to find out whether the text agrees with them. The reading strategy gets its own minutes before the questions are attempted, because without it they answer from what they remember. The listening is the place where the OGE «Not stated» idea is introduced — one sentence, inside a right/wrong task the book already provides, and then dropped. The last stage is deliberately an argument with a phrase box: the language aim is not «express an opinion» but «disagree without being rude».
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Some people earn a million pounds a year playing video games. Fourteen hours of practice a day, teams, fans, tournaments. Is that a sport? Take a side now — and by the end of the lesson you have to defend it in English against somebody who disagrees.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a fast recall of the four modal forms including the past, and the bridge into eSports

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. Six school situations on the board; the class calls the right form: wear a uniform · run in the corridor · bring your own pen · take photos in the exam · come on Saturday · call if you are ill.
  3. Then one past sentence each: Tell me one thing you had to do yesterday and one thing you didn't have to do.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the second spread: Books open, page fifty-six. Some people earn a million pounds a year playing video games. Is that a sport?
📺 On screen: the six situations in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: call the form, then produce one past sentence each. Expect didn't have to to be much rarer than had to. Typical problem — I hadn't to: write the correct form up and move on

Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Draw a line across the board: at one end IT IS A SPORT, at the other IT IS NOT A SPORT. Write four things along it: football · chess · eSports · fishing.
  2. Come and put a mark where you think each one goes. Three students, thirty seconds, no discussion yet.
  3. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: today you read a long text about exactly this — and at the end of the lesson you defend your position in English against somebody who disagrees with you.
  4. Set the bar: «I agree» is half an answer. The other half starts with 'because'.
📺 On screen: the line and the four items in chalk — the board is the medium here and it stays up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: place items on the line and immediately disagree with one another. Expect chess and fishing to be more controversial than eSports. Typical problem: they want to argue now — name the eight-minute slot at the end and move on

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.56: the photos, plus the background that makes the argument a real one

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

  3. Set the gist task before the reading: Read fast and check your ideas. Two minutes. Do not look at the questions yet.
  4. Warmer alternative if the group is flat (TB p.109): in groups, six crazy rules for your country with must and have to — it recycles yesterday and wakes them up.

👦 Students do: describe the photos and predict. Expect them to recognise a tournament immediately. Typical problem: they know more about eSports than the text does — remind them that the questions are about the TEXT

16–30′
Reading · eSports
solo → pairs → class
Why: A long text with five questions — the hardest reading format of the course so far. The strategy stage comes first because without it they answer from memory.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.56 (track 72): five 3-option questions on a long text

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The strategy first, from the book's own box: A2 Key box, TB p.109: read the whole text fast first to get the idea. Then read each question carefully and check whether the possible answers are positive or negative. Never leave a question blank — there are no marks lost for a wrong answer, so if you are unsure, work out which options are definitely wrong and guess between the two that are left.
  2. Do question 1 together, out loud, exactly as TB p.109 does it: Look at answer A. Does the text say eSports are fun? (yes) Does it say better to watch than play? (no — fun to watch AS WELL AS to play) Look at B. More and more people — is that the same as becoming more popular? (yes). So B.
  3. Then solo, 7 minutes, questions 2–5. Silence, no dictionaries.
  4. Kagan RallyCoach to check: the partner does not accept an answer without the line from the text.
  5. Key: 1 B · 2 A · 3 B · 4 C · 5 C
  6. Play track 72 while they read the text once more — hearing it at speed is free pronunciation practice and it settles the answers.
📺 On screen: the article on the screen so a line can be pointed at (Backup: it is printed on SB p.56 — everyone works from the paper book and the pair that finds the line first reads it out; if the audio is down, read the article aloud yourself at the end)
👦 Students do: read, answer alone, then justify to a partner. Expect 3–4 out of 5. Typical error: question 2 answered A (they don't have a lot of free time) because the text mentions 14 hours — but the question asks what is HARD, and the answer is that it is not easy to be a winner

Reading · your turn

Why: Talking about the text immediately after reading it is what turns comprehension into language; the questions are the book's own and they are genuinely open.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.56: four open questions about video games and eSports

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Do you play video games? Would you watch an eSports competition? Do you know any eSports stars? Is 'eSports' a good name for these competitions?
  2. Kagan RallyRobin in threes: one answer each, round the circle, two full rounds, no interrupting.
  3. Two comprehension questions from TB p.109 to keep the text alive: Is it more difficult to be an eSports star than a sports star? Which eSport would you be good at?
  4. Fast finishers, if a group finishes early: TB p.105: fast finishers classify the fifteen activities under Games, Team sports, Water sports, Racket sports, Winter sports, Others. TB p.109: fast finishers invent words beginning with e‑ and say what they could mean — eFriend, eWaste, e‑xcited.
📺 On screen: the four questions on the board — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: answer in turn with a reason. Expect question 4 (is eSports a good name?) to produce the best English. Typical error: a one-word answer — the RallyRobin rule makes it visible to the group before you have to say anything

Vocabulary · sports words and ‑er
pairs → class
Why: Six topic words and one small piece of word building. The ‑er suffix is worth its minutes because it is a spelling rule as much as a vocabulary point.

WHAT WE DO: Vocabulary ex.1–4 p.57: six topic words, then people made with ‑er

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: find the six words in the article and match them to definitions, in pairs, 4 minutes. Key: 1 f · 2 d · 3 b · 4 e · 5 a · 6 c.
  2. One collocation note that saves marks (TB p.110): take part is followed by in — take part in a competition.
  3. ex.2: find winner, player and footballer in the text. Key: winner — someone who wins · player — someone who plays · footballer — someone who plays football.
  4. The spelling rule, elicited not announced: SB p.57: add ‑er to some verbs and nouns to make a person. If the spelling is vowel + consonant + vowel, double the last letter before adding ‑er (win → winner). If the word already ends in e, add ‑r (dance → dancer).
  5. For students who need it, build the word letter by letter down the board: TB p.110, for spelling before ‑er: build the word one letter at a time down the board — s / sw / swi / swim / swimm / swimme / swimmer — so the doubling is seen rather than explained.
  6. ex.3 — twelve words to turn into people, solo, 4 minutes. Key: cleaner · climber · dancer · diver · golfer · photographer · runner · singer · skier · swimmer · teacher · worker.
  7. ex.4 as Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade: each card carries one word from ex.1 and one from ex.3, and the partner has to make a sentence with both.
📺 On screen: the twelve verbs on the screen, turned into people live as the class calls them (Backup: write the twelve verbs in a column on the board and let students come up and add the ending, doubling the letter where needed)
👦 Students do: match, derive the spelling rule, then build and use the people words. Expect swimmer and runner to be the two that need the rule. Typical error: photographer stressed on the first syllable

Why: Right/wrong statements with the wrong ones corrected — the sub-skill is holding a speaker's opinion apart from your own. This is also where Not stated gets its one sentence.

WHAT WE DO: Listening ex.1–2 p.57 (track 73): tick what you hear, then seven statements to judge and correct

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 first: read the word box and predict which are eSports, which are mind sports and which will come up. Then play track 73 once. Key: you hear all of them EXCEPT medal, prize and tournament.
  2. ex.2: read the seven statements BEFORE the second play. The statements are in the order of the recording, so if you have lost your place, jump to the next statement rather than trying to catch up.
  3. Play track 73 again. Correct the wrong ones in writing, not orally. Key: 1 ✓ · 2 ✗ — Dad doesn't think it's a good idea to have mind sports in the Olympics · 3 ✗ — Lily says you have to use your brain too · 4 ✓ · 5 ✗ — Lily says the Olympics are more famous · 6 ✓ · 7 ✗ — Dad disagrees.
  4. The one OGE sentence of the lesson, said once and not developed: In the Russian exam this task has a third option — «the text doesn't say». Look at statement 3: if Lily had said nothing about fitness, the answer would be neither right nor wrong. Then move straight on.
  5. Where marks go: Where marks go: statement 3 and statement 5 are both about what Lily SAYS, not about what is true. Students answer from their own opinion.
📺 On screen: track 73 in the board app, with the seven statements on the screen (Backup: read the audioscript from TB p.295 yourself, twice, at natural speed; the statements are printed in the students' own book)
👦 Students do: tick, then judge and correct. Expect statements 3 and 5 to be answered from their own opinion rather than from what Lily said. Typical error: they mark a statement wrong but cannot say what the speaker actually said

🎯 Speaking out · four statements
groups of 4
Why: Freer practice with a genuine disagreement built in. The phrase box gives them the tools to disagree without being rude, which is the actual language aim of the stage.

WHAT WE DO: Speaking ex.1 p.57: four statements about eSports, agreed or disagreed with and defended

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The four statements, one at a time on the board: 1 eSports are not sports because you don't have to be fit to do them. 2 It's a good idea to have eSports in the Olympics. 3 Thinking and using your brain is important in every sport. 4 Mind sports will be in the Olympics in ten years' time.
  2. The phrase box on the board, where it stays for the whole stage: I think / I don't think … · I agree / I don't agree … · I suppose so … · I see! · That's true. · I'm not sure. · Maybe
  3. Kagan Timed Pair Share first: 60 seconds each way on statement 1, then swap partners for statement 2.
  4. Then open it into fours for statements 3 and 4, with one rule: You must use a phrase from the box and then 'because'.
  5. Kagan Showdown to close: each group writes AGREE or DISAGREE for statement 4 on a mini-whiteboard, all boards up at once, and each group gives one reason.
  6. Log errors silently on your correction sheet; do not correct aloud — the feedback slot is the start of the next lesson.
📺 On screen: the four statements and the phrase box on the screen (Backup: both are printed on SB p.57 — copy the phrase box onto the board and read the statements out one at a time)
👦 Students do: argue in pairs, then in fours, then commit as a group. Expect statement 3 (thinking matters in every sport) to be the one everybody agrees with and nobody can justify. Typical error: I'm agree — note it, say nothing

Structure
I think eSports are a sport because you have to practise. · I don't agree — you don't have to be fit. · I suppose so. · I'm not sure. · That's true, but …
Form
verb or noun + ‑er to make a person; double the last letter after vowel + consonant + vowel (win → winner, swim → swimmer); add ‑r if the word already ends in e (dance → dancer).
Pron. watch
the ‑er ending is the weak /ə/ — ˈswimmer, not swimm-ER. Word stress: ˈtournament · ˈchampion · proˈfessional (second syllable).
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«eSports players usually play in teams and have fans.» Does the text say they are famous? → it says they are NOT well known in the way footballers are. Read to the end of the sentence.
«Are all eSports team sports?» → no. The text says players usually play in teams; usually is not always.
win → winner. Why two n? → vowel, consonant, vowel before the ending: double it.
«I agree.» Is that an answer? → not in this room. An opinion without a reason is half an answer.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The eSports argument eats the whole lessonTimebox it deliberately: 90 seconds in the lead-in, and then Hold that. You get eight minutes to argue at the end, in English. Naming the future slot is what buys the reading time.
They answer the reading questions from what they know about gamingThe evidence rule, enforced without exception: No line from the text — no point. Finger on the line. Two of the five questions can be answered plausibly from general knowledge and wrongly.
“I agree” and nothing elseThe phrase box on SB p.57 is the tool, not the decoration. Put three phrases on the board and make the rule: Phrase plus because. Every time.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
