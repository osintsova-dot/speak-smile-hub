🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name twelve things in a home and make predictions with will, won't, may and might, choosing the one that matches how sure you actually are.
What is genuinely hard
Russian marks the future with one form and adds a separate word for doubt. English puts the doubt inside the verb. So the hard part is not the form but the CHOICE — and the fact that after all three modals the verb is bare.
Typical error to expect
I will to go / He might to come — a to-infinitive after a modal. After will, may and might the verb has no to (SB p.47 rules 3) [A2/B1]
She wills come / He mays go — a third-person ‑s on a modal. Modals never take ‑s (SB p.47 rules 4) [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary is loaded first because the whole listening is made of it — twelve words at 14′ means the radio interview at 36′ is half understood before it starts. The «hands or technology» sorting task at 28′ looks like a filler and is not: it makes students produce the exact sentences the recording is about, which is the cheapest possible pre-listening. Grammar is split into two stages on purpose — will alone first, then may / might AGAINST it, because the pair is only meaningful as a contrast. Pronunciation gets four minutes and no more: if won't is not rounded the class hears want, and no amount of grammar rescues that.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two thousand and forty. Your house is warm before you get home and the walls change colour. By the end of today you can say what WILL happen, what MIGHT happen and what definitely WON'T — and the difference between those three is the whole lesson.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a fast recall of the kit list and one future plan each, then the bridge into Unit 8

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. The kit list against the clock: you name a situation, they call the object. It's raining. It's dark. You're lost. You cut your finger. You're hungry on the mountain. You want to sleep.
  3. Then one full sentence each, standing: Tell me one thing you are doing this weekend. With a time.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the new unit: Books open, page forty-six. Forget the mountains. It is the year twenty-forty and we are looking at your house.
📺 On screen: the six situations read out by you and the objects written up as they come — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: call the objects, then each say one future plan. Expect the kit words to come back fast. Typical problem — first-aid said without the kit

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three sentences on the board, one under the other: In 2040 we will live on Mars. · In 2040 we might live on Mars. · In 2040 we won't live on Mars.
  2. Same year, same planet. What is different? Take answers for 30 seconds — someone will say «how sure».
  3. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: English puts the certainty inside the verb. Russian adds a word next to it. Today you choose the verb that matches what you actually think.
  4. Set the bar: If you say WILL, I may ask you to prove it.
📺 On screen: the three model sentences in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: compare the three sentences and name the difference. Expect «how sure» within 30 seconds. Typical problem: they translate all three the same way — that is exactly the point of the stage

Lead-in · the year 2040
groups of 3
Why: Opinions before language: they have plenty to say about the future and no words to say it with yet. That gap is what makes the vocabulary stage land.

WHAT WE DO: the About you box p.46: describe seven everyday things as they will be in 2040

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. It is 2040. How old are you? What job do you have? Where do you live? Thirty seconds, no writing.
  2. Kagan Think-Pair-Share on the seven words in the box: books · cars · computers · homes · planes · smartphones · televisions. Each group takes three and describes them.
  3. Two questions to sharpen it (TB p.89): Which of these has changed the most already? Can you think of something completely new that we have in 2040?
  4. Take two groups only. Keep the ideas on the board — they come back at 78′.
📺 On screen: the seven words on the board, with space next to each for the class's ideas — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: imagine and describe in threes. Expect cars and homes to produce the most. Typical problem: they answer in the present — that is fine now, the grammar arrives at 47′

Vocabulary · home and appliances
solo → pairs → class
Why: Twelve words in one definition-matching task, then straight into a personalised question round — the words are used within three minutes of being met.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.46 (track 58): twelve definitions to twelve words, then two sorting questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed first (TB p.89): What rooms are there in a home? What is usually in the kitchen? Let them list what they already have.
  2. Books open — ex.1, solo, 5 minutes, then compare in pairs.
  3. Play track 58 — check and choral repetition. Key: 1 fridge · 2 washing machine · 3 air conditioning · 4 bookcase · 5 roof · 6 barbecue · 7 stairs · 8 drawer · 9 lights · 10 seat · 11 heating · 12 bin.
  4. Reverse it (TB p.89): students cover the definitions, look only at the words and say the definition from memory. 2 minutes in pairs.
  5. ex.2 in pairs: which need electricity, which are furniture, where in a home you find each. Key: 1 need electricity: air conditioning, fridge, heating, lights, washing machine · 2 furniture: bookcase, drawer, seat · 3 students' own answers.
  6. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with word cards, 3 minutes, five trades each.
📺 On screen: track 58 in the board app, with the twelve words on the screen (Backup if the board is down: read the twelve definitions aloud yourself and have the class call the word, then repeat chorally; the Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards are printed in advance and need no technology)
👦 Students do: match, check, reverse, sort, then mingle. Expect 9–10 out of 12 unaided. Typical errors: bin confused with drawer; air conDITIONing stressed on the first syllable

28–36′
Hands or technology
pairs
Why: A sorting task that is really a thinking task: it produces the exact sentences the listening is about, so the recording arrives already half-understood.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.46: eight everyday actions sorted into «with my hands» and «with technology»

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pre-teach two words in ten seconds by pointing at the real objects: TB p.89: pre-teach (light) switch and remote control by pointing at the real things in the classroom — it takes ten seconds and unblocks the whole of ex.3.
  2. Demonstrate with the TB p.89 question: How do you cook a pizza — hands or technology? And how do you EAT it?
  3. Pairs mark the eight actions H or T, then compare with another pair, 4 minutes. Possible answers: open your front door — H · turn on the computer — T (the switch) · turn on the lights — T (the switch) · close the garage door — H/T (hands or a remote control) · turn off the TV — T (switch or remote) · lock the car — H/T · close the windows — H
  4. The productive twist: for every T, they say what the technology IS — I turn on the lights with a switch. This is the sentence the recording is about, so make them say it out loud.
📺 On screen: the eight actions on the screen, sorted live into two columns as the class decides (Backup: the eight actions are printed in the students' own book — draw the two columns in chalk and write the actions in as they are called out)
👦 Students do: sort, compare and justify. Expect the garage door and the car to be argued over — both are right. Typical error: they answer H/T without naming the technology, which is the half that matters

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–5 p.46 (track 59): a radio interview — first the furniture, then the ORDER of the ideas

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Before you play (TB p.89): You are going to hear a professor talk about homes of the future. What questions do you want to ask him? Take three, write them on the board.
  2. Play track 59. ex.4 — which furniture is mentioned? Key: seat, bookcase, table, drawer (the furniture the professor talks about).
  3. ex.5 is the harder sub-skill: number five ideas in the order you hear them. Ask them to reorder BEFORE the second play, from memory (TB p.90) — then play and let them correct themselves. Key: a 2 · b 5 · c 4 · d 1 · e 3.
  4. Close it with the question that opens the grammar: Which of these do you NOT believe? Which do you not WANT to happen?
📺 On screen: track 59 in the board app, with the five statements on the screen (Backup: read the audioscript from TB p.292 yourself, twice, at natural speed; the five statements are printed in the students' own book)
👦 Students do: listen twice, catch the furniture, then order the ideas. Expect the ordering task to be genuinely hard — that is the point. Typical error: they order by what seems logical rather than by what was said

Move break
standing
Why: A stirrer that also forces one English sentence. Teenagers who have been sitting for forty minutes need to stand before the grammar block, or the grammar block dies.

WHAT WE DO: 90 seconds of movement and a switch of gear

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. That wall is YES, this wall is NO. In 2040 nobody will go to school. Go! Then: In 2040 nobody will drive a car.
  2. One sentence each time from a student on each wall — in English, and that is the price of standing there.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: move between walls and defend the choice in one sentence. Expect a genuine split on the school question. Typical error: Nobody will to go — do not correct it yet, it is the next stage's job

47–58′
Grammar · will
pairs → class
Why: Guided discovery from the interview they have just heard. Six short rules, each of which kills one specific Russian-speaker error.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 p.47: pull the six will rules out of the interview, then produce four forms

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two sentences on the board (TB p.90): Jason is meeting Susie at six o'clock tomorrow. / Homes will be very different in the future. Both are the future. Are they the same? Why not? (the first is a plan; the second is a prediction — we cannot be sure).
  2. ex.1 — read the three examples from the interview. Then ex.2 in pairs, 5 minutes: complete the six rules. Key: 1 in the future · 2 often use · 3 without · 4 do not use · 5 after · 6 don't use.
  3. Say the extra fact once (TB p.90) and move on: will is the most common way of talking about the future; shall survives mainly in suggestions (Shall we go?); the contraction 'll is used mostly after pronouns.
  4. ex.3 solo, 3 minutes, then Kagan RallyCoach. Key: 1 won't be · 2 will arrive · 3 will use · 4 Will people live.
  5. Ask the question the book asks: Do you agree with sentences 3 and 4?
📺 On screen: the will rules on the screen, completed live as the class calls them out (Backup: the rules box is printed in the students' own book — copy the six lines onto the board and fill them in with the class)
👦 Students do: derive the six rules, then produce four forms. Expect rules 3 and 4 (no to, no ‑s) to be the ones they have been breaking. Typical error: Will people lives in ex.3 item 4
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare3_Unit8_Grammar_Future_Predictions_A5
A5 grammar card — HOW SURE ARE YOU?: two levels of confidence — STRONG PREDICTION will/won't (subject + will + BASE VERB · won't · the question flip Will homes have stairs? with short answers) and POSSIBILITY may/might (There may be robots… · We might have…) — with the crucial rule AFTER WILL/WON'T/MAY/MIGHT → BASE VERB and the crossed-out will has / might to use, plus a SPEECH BANK (I think… · I don't think… · Maybe… · In my opinion…)
  • Glue the card in and highlight the crucial rule — the base verb after all four.
  • Colour the strong predictions in one colour and the possibilities in another.
  • Write four sentences about your own home in ten years, two of each strength.
  • Add a reason to each — a prediction without a reason does not count.
When: ~5 min at the start of the “Grammar · will” stagePrep: 1 × A5 per pupil. Scissors, glue sticks, pencils.

58–68′
Grammar · may and might
pairs → class
Why: Deliberately taught after will and against it: the whole point is the difference between «I think it will» and «it is possible», which Russian says with one word.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–6 p.47: the same six rules for may and might, and why they exist at all

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books CLOSED (TB p.90): write the two example sentences up without may and might and ask the class to fill them in from memory. Then books open to check.
  2. ex.5 in pairs, 4 minutes. Key: 1 possible · 2 often use · 3 without 'to' · 4 do not use · 5 after · 6 don't use.
  3. Ask for one example per rule — Give me a sentence with rule one. (I may study English at university.)
  4. ex.6 — word order, solo, 3 minutes, checked with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Sentence three — number ones, boards up! Key: 1 I may do · 2 might technology help · 3 may not be able · 4 might not want.
  5. Name the contrast explicitly once: Will = I think it happens. Might = it is one possibility. Won't = I think it does not happen.
📺 On screen: the may / might rules on the screen alongside the will rules from the previous stage, so the two boxes can be compared side by side (Backup: both rule boxes are in the students' own book — keep the will rules in chalk on the board and add the may/might column next to them)
👦 Students do: complete the rules from memory, then build sentences. Expect may not to be contracted by somebody — that is the one to name out loud. Typical error: might technology helps in ex.6 item 2

68–74′
Grammar · error hunt
teams
Why: Correcting other people's sentences is faster and safer than producing them, and the Showdown means every student answers every item instead of one student answering one.

WHAT WE DO: ex.7 p.47: five sentences that are wrong, corrected as a team against the clock

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Five sentences, 4 minutes. Kagan Showdown: the team agrees a correction, writes it on the mini-whiteboard, and on Showdown! every board goes up at once.
  2. Key: A few other friends will / may / might come to my house later. · I met a new friend and I think you may / might / will like her. · It won't rain this evening. · I'm not sure but the book may / might be on your kitchen table. · I'll / will meet you in the skatepark later.
  3. For every item, one team says WHY — not what. Say the rule number.
  4. Note that items 1, 2, 4 and 5 have more than one right answer, and say so: Two teams can both be right here. Convince me.
📺 On screen: the five sentences one at a time on the screen so the whole class repairs the same one (Backup: write the five sentences on the board before the lesson and uncover them one by one with a sheet of paper)
👦 Students do: correct as a team and reveal together. Expect 3–4 out of 5. Typical error: item 3 (It won't rain this evening) is corrected to It doesn't rain — the negative future is the weak spot
SB p.47 ex.7 · mini-whiteboards
74–78′
Pronunciation · will and won't
class
Why: Two sounds that decide the meaning: if won't is not rounded, the class hears want. Four minutes, drilled by feel rather than explained.

WHAT WE DO: ex.8 p.47 (track 60): four sentences, drilled by feel

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. Play track 60 and have them write the four sentences down. Books open to check, then listen and repeat. Track 60: Houses will be smaller. · I'll live in a big house. · People won't use door keys. · I won't walk anywhere.
  2. TB p.90: two things break. The rounded /w/ in will and won't — exaggerate the lip rounding when you drill it. And the dark /l/ in the contraction 'll — compare the two /l/ sounds in little: the second one is the sound in 'll, with the tongue curled further back.
  3. The minimal pair that makes it matter: I want to go / I won't go. Say one, the class shows one finger or two.
  4. Three choral repetitions of People won't use door keys. with exaggerated lip rounding, then individually round the room.
📺 On screen: track 60 in the board app (Backup: say the four sentences yourself twice — once with the /w/ exaggerated, once flat — and let the class tell you which is English)
👦 Students do: write from dictation, then repeat with rounded lips. Expect won't and want to be genuinely confused by two or three students. Typical error: 'll dropped entirely — I live in a big house for I'll live

Why: Freer practice with a built-in reason to choose between will and might: they have to say how sure they are, and disagree with each other.

WHAT WE DO: ex.9–11 p.47: their own About you ideas plus the book's seven predictions, argued in threes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

Structure
I think they'll be very different from today's homes. · You won't have to use your hands. · Will it work for the heating too? · Seats might not look very different. · You may have a table with a computer inside it.
Form
will / won't / may / may not / might / might not + bare infinitive. No ‑s in the third person; no auxiliary do in questions; the negative goes after the modal; may not and might not are never contracted.
Pron. watch
the rounded /w/ in will and won't, and the dark /l/ in the contraction 'll — compare the two /l/ sounds in little (TB p.90). Word stress: air conˈditioning · ˈwashing machine · ˈbookcase.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I'll live on Mars.» — am I sure? → yes, that is what I think will happen.
«I might live on Mars.» — am I sure? → no: it is one possibility out of several.
«He mays come» — what is wrong? → modals take no ‑s: he may come.
«I will to help you» — what is wrong? → no to after will: I will help you.
«Will won't rain» vs «It won't rain» — where does the negative go? → after will: won't.

Assumptions: SB p.46–47, syllabus lesson 24. Will has been met passively since Get Involved! A2 but never contrasted with may and might. Of the twelve home words they usually know fridge, lights and stairs; air conditioning, bin, drawer and bookcase are new or half-known.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
“Why can't I say I will to go?”One line on the board, no explanation: I will to go. Then three choral repetitions of the correct sentence. Explanation costs three minutes and teaches less than the crossing-out.
May and might are treated as different in meaningTB p.90's line, said once: at this level they mean the same. The difference is may is slightly more formal. Do not go further — it is not on the syllabus and it confuses them.
Everyone chooses will for everythingMake it cost something. In the final stage, a prediction with will may be challenged by the group: Are you SURE? Prove it. If they cannot, they must downgrade to might.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

will / may / might is one of the few places where Russian has one verb form and English has three degrees of certainty. Naming that difference out loud is worth more than any drill.
