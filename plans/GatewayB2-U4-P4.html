🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Listen to a four-minute podcast for specific information and answer six open questions in note form; then use articles (a/an, the, zero) by rule, and express ability across time: can / could for general ability, was able to / managed to / succeeded in for one successful occasion in the past, will be able to for the future, to be able to / being able to where a modal cannot go.
What is genuinely hard
Articles, because Russian does not have them: the is not «this», and the zero article in front of plurals and uncountables (Scientists say animals have complex brains) is invisible to a Russian ear. And could, because it looks like a translation of «мог» but cannot be used for one achieved event: yesterday I was able to buy the tickets, never yesterday I could buy them.
Typical error to expect
«The people are different», «I like the music» — a plural or uncountable noun used in general takes NO article. Russian speakers over-use the as soon as they have learned it exists.
«Yesterday I could buy the tickets» for one single successful occasion — English needs was able to / managed to / succeeded in. Could is general ability in the past, not one achieved event.
«He succeeded to win», «he managed in winning» — succeed in + -ing, manage + to + infinitive. The two patterns swap places in almost every group.
Why the lesson is built this way
Listening comes first, while the room is fresh, and it is genuinely hard: a real podcast about Leonardo's notebooks with numbers, names and one astonishing fact (he wrote backwards). The grammar then grows out of that same text world — the Newton passage for articles, the Belzoni passage for ability — so no sentence in the lesson is about nothing. The two grammar points are kept apart by a two-minute physical break, because articles and modals compete for the same attention.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
Leonardo da Vinci wrote to-do lists. Real ones: «find out why a fish in water is faster than a bird in the sky», «build glasses to see the Moon larger». You will listen to a podcast about them, and then we deal with the two things Russian speakers lose most marks on in English: the little words a and the, and how to say that you managed to do something once.

Why: Two listenings with two different tasks is what makes a hard recording learnable: gist-and-verify first, detail second. Notes before sentences protects the weaker half of the class.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.50 exercises 2 and 3, recording 20 — first true/false, then six open questions in note form

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pre-teach one word only: a masterpiece — the best work an artist ever makes.
  2. First listening — exercise 2 (six true/false). Read the statements aloud first; give them thirty seconds to predict. Play track 20 once, straight through. Answers: track 20, true or false: 1 F (the Mona Lisa is mentioned, not discussed) · 2 F (not mentioned) · 3 T (he wrote backwards — mirror script) · 4 F · 5 F · 6 T (more than seven thousand pages).
  3. Check in pairs before the class check, and make them say WHY a false one is false — that is where the listening actually happened.
  4. Second listening — exercise 3 (six open questions). Instruction, said before you press play: notes only. Not sentences. You will write the sentences afterwards with your partner. Play track 20 again.
  5. Pairs turn notes into full sentences, four minutes. Answers: 1 they were the things he wanted to do, investigate or learn · 2 any one of: calculate the size of Milan, describe the jaw of a crocodile, build glasses to see the Moon larger, find out why a fish is faster in water than a bird in the sky · 3 he could write from right to left · 4 because paper was expensive · 5 scientists at Stanford use his drawings of birds' wings · 6 he did not see art and science as separate.
  6. Only if the group struggled: play the last third a third time, with the audio script (TB p.123) in your hand so you can dictate the two lines they missed.
📺 On screen: recording — track 20 in the board app, volume checked before the lesson, and the track cued to zero. Backup: the full audio script is printed in the Teacher's Book (class audio script, TB p.123) — read it aloud yourself at normal speed if the sound fails; it takes four minutes and works.
👦 Students do: predict, listen twice with a different job each time, then rebuild the answers in pairs as full sentences.

30–34′
Stirrer · stand up if you can
open floor
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a two-minute physical break that pre-loads the ability structures the next stage will formalise

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. You call an ability; those who can do it stay standing, the rest sit: Stand up if you can swim. Sit down if you couldn't swim at the age of five. Stand up if you managed to finish your homework yesterday.
  2. Six or seven prompts, fast, with the target forms in your own mouth each time — they are hearing the grammar before they meet it.
  3. Last prompt is the hinge into the next stage: Stand up if you were able to understand every word of that podcast. Nobody? Good — neither was I the first time.
📺 On screen: no display: your voice and their legs. Backup: not applicable — this stage has no equipment.
👦 Students do: stand and sit repeatedly, listening for could / was able to / managed to in your prompts.

34–48′
Grammar in context 2a · articles, by rule
desks → pairs
Why: Articles are only learnable as a decision procedure. Every check in this stage asks for the rule, never for the letter — which is the habit that survives into a test.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.50 exercises 1–2 — six rules matched to six sentences, then the Isaac Newton text with seventeen gaps

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. On the board: Star Wars: New Hope · Lion King · Captain Marvel. Which titles are missing a or the? (A New Hope · The Lion King · Captain Marvel — no article.) Ninety seconds, and it wakes everyone up.
  2. Exercise 1 in pairs: match rules a–f to sentences 1–6. Answers: a a/an — rule 3 · b the — rule 4 · c no article — rule 6 · d no article — rule 2 · e the — rule 5 · f the — rule 1. Read each sentence aloud and ask the why-question after it: why the and not a here? — because superlatives always take the.
  3. Name the Russian problem out loud, once, and then stop apologising for it: Russian has no articles, so you cannot hear them. You will choose them by rule for about a year, and then they start to sound wrong when they are missing.
  4. Exercise 2 — the Isaac Newton text (Culture exchange), seven minutes, individually. Answers with the rule attached: the Isaac Newton text: a a · b the · c a · d the · e – · f the · g an · h a · i – · j a · k an · l a · m a · n – · o a · p a · q the.
  5. Check in Rally Coach: partner A does a–i naming the rule each time, partner B does j–q.
  6. Fast finishers, on the board: What subjects did Newton research? What did the Principia do? How did he discover gravity? What is at the National Library in London now?
📺 On screen: the three film titles and the six rule labels on the board. Backup: the rules are also printed in the Grammar reference on SB p.54 — send them there if the board is full.
👦 Students do: match rules in pairs, complete seventeen gaps alone, then justify each choice to a partner by naming a rule.

48–66′
Grammar in context 2b · ability across three times
desks → pairs
Why: The could / was able to line is the single highest-frequency error in Russian B2 speaking. It is taught as a decision (general or one occasion?), drilled orally, then written twice.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.50–51 exercises 3–5 — the rules, nine sentences and the Belzoni text

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Elicit first: which modal verbs talk about ability? What else can we use? Take can/could, then push for be able to, manage, succeed.
  2. Exercise 3 in pairs — match the rules to the sentences. Answers: a can (sentence 6) · b will be able to (2) · c to be able to / being able to (7 and 1) · d could (4) · e was/were able to, managed to, succeeded in (5) · f couldn't (3) · g could (8).
  3. Teach the one distinction that carries the whole lesson, on the board, in two lines: I could swim at five (general ability). Yesterday I was able to / managed to finish the race (one occasion). Concept-check it: «Yesterday I could open the door» — is that English? — No. Why not? — one occasion.
  4. Add the two patterns that trip everyone: manage TO do · succeed IN doing. Drill five quick transformations orally.
  5. Exercise 4 (nine sentences) individually, five minutes, then check with the rule named. Answers: 1 was able to · 2 managed · 3 be able to · 4 was able to · 5 couldn't · 6 will be able to · 7 managed to · 8 could swim · 9 couldn't read.
  6. Exercise 5 — the Belzoni text, one word per gap, six minutes. Answers: the Belzoni text: a could · b able · c to · d can · e in · f couldn't · g was · h be · i will.
  7. Mixed ability: while they work, put the five comprehension questions about Belzoni on the board (how many people could he carry? what two jobs did he have in Egypt? where is The Younger Memnon now?) — stronger students answer with books closed.
📺 On screen: the two-line contrast (general ability vs one occasion) written large and left up for the rest of the unit. Backup: the same contrast is in the Grammar reference, SB p.54 — point there if the board is lost.
👦 Students do: sort rules in pairs, complete nine sentences alone, then the gapped text; stronger students answer the extra questions from memory.

🎯 Freer practice · three things you managed to do
pairs → new pairs
Why: Retelling a partner's sentences forces third-person forms and a second processing pass — twice the practice for no extra time.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.51 exercise 7 — the structures used about the students' own lives, then reported to a second partner

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two minutes of silent writing: three sentences. One thing you could do as a child but can't now. One thing you managed to do last month. One thing you'll be able to do in five years.
  2. Timed Pair Share, ninety seconds each way — the listener may not interrupt but must ask one follow-up question at the end.
  3. Then close the books and change partners: tell your new partner what your first partner said. This is the memory stage that turns a sentence into language.
  4. Whole class, four or five students, and correct only ability forms.
  5. Board the best three sentences of the lesson with names next to them — teenagers write better when their sentence might go up.
📺 On screen: the three prompts on the board. Backup: none needed; this is a book-closed speaking stage.
👦 Students do: write three true sentences, tell one partner, then retell a different partner from memory.

Consolidation · six sentences, both grammars at once
desks
Why: Two grammar points taught separately have to be produced together at least once, or the class leaves with two isolated rules and no sentence.

WHAT WE DO: a short written check where articles and ability appear in the same sentences

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Dictate six sentence beginnings, or write them up: (1) Leonardo was … artist who … write backwards. (2) … scientists at Stanford … answer his questions about flight. (3) He couldn't … but he … (4) In … future I'll … (5) … most important thing about Belzoni is that he … (6) I've never … to …
  2. Individually, five minutes, in the notebook.
  3. Swap notebooks with a partner and mark only two things: articles and ability forms. Nothing else, and nothing in red.
  4. Take three sentences to the board and let the class fix them.
  5. Keep the notebooks open — this is the page they revise from before the unit test in three lessons' time.
📺 On screen: the six beginnings on the board. Backup: dictate them slowly if the board is unavailable — six lines takes two minutes.
👦 Students do: write six sentences, then peer-mark a partner's for exactly two features.

Workbook p.33–34
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will be better able to talk about ability in the past, present and future, and to use a/an, the and the zero article by rule, in the context of people who achieved something extraordinary.
  • Subsidiary. Listening for specific information in a four-minute podcast (two hearings, two tasks); note-taking before sentence-writing; weak forms in was able to and could.
  • Personal. Resist re-playing the recording a third time by default. Check what actually failed first — usually it is the question, not the audio.
By the end, students can…
  • take notes from a podcast and turn them into full answers
  • choose a, the or no article and name the rule for the choice
  • say what they could do generally in the past and what they managed to do once
  • use be able to where can cannot go (after will, to, and -ing)
  • tell a second partner what a first partner said

Structure
He was able to write from right to left. · They managed to move the statue. · He succeeded in finding the temple. · I'll be able to drive next year. · Scientists say animals have complex brains.
Form
a/an + singular countable, first mention or a profession · the + known, unique, superlative, first/last · zero article + plural or uncountable used in general · can / can't (present) · could / couldn't (general past) · was–were able to, managed TO + infinitive, succeeded IN + -ing (one past occasion) · will be able to (future) · to be able to / being able to after another verb or preposition.
Pron. watch
the weak forms carry the grammar: was able to is /wəz ˈeɪbl tə/, could is /kəd/ unless it is stressed, and a before a consonant is /ə/, not /eɪ/. If students say /eɪ/ for every article, the article stops being grammar and becomes a stumble
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
could: «I could swim at five» — one time or many times? — Many. So for one time? — was able to.
zero article: «Scientists say animals have complex brains» — all scientists or some particular ones? — All, in general. So what article? — no article at all.
the: How many Suns are there? — One. So? — the Sun.
ICQ before listening 2: Full sentences or notes? Do we write while listening or after?

Assumptions: Students use can/could confidently and have met the phrase be able to. They have had articles «explained» several times before and still get them wrong — this lesson assumes familiarity, not mastery.

Likely problemWhat you do
The recording is too fast and the group freezes after one hearing.That is planned for: the first task is only six true/false items. Predict before playing, check in pairs before the class check, and keep the Teacher's Book audio script within reach for the two lines that decide the answers.
Articles turn into a forty-minute lecture and the ability half dies.Timebox it: fourteen minutes, six rules, one text. Anything unresolved goes to the Grammar reference on p.54 as homework reading — the class gets articles again in every unit.
«Yesterday I could finish the test» keeps coming back.Keep the two-line contrast on the board all lesson and point at it instead of speaking. Silent correction is faster and less humiliating.
Stronger students finish the Belzoni text in three minutes.The five comprehension questions are already on the board for exactly this — books closed for them, open for everyone else.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 1–9 (listening for specific information) and tasks 19–25 (articles and modal-type forms in a text).

The listening half is the closest thing in the course to the Russian listening section: hearing a long informative monologue once and taking notes that survive checking. The grammar half is where Russian speakers lose most: Russian has no articles at all, so a/an, the and the zero article are chosen by rule, not by ear, and the codifier tests them inside a connected text.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
