🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Why (for the teacher): Jobs are not introduced as a list but as working material for an intelligence unit: every hero has a “day job” and you have to work it out from a description. The book already gives us the mechanism — ex.1 “Who am I?”, ex.3 the day jobs of six superheroes, ex.7 guess the job through yes/no questions. We simply push it as far as a product: the crew builds a file on a person and defends it until the class guesses. The unit's WDYT question (“what makes a hero”) opens here and stays open until the project. The past turns up inside the descriptions (“before she was a pilot”), but it is NOT announced as the topic of the lesson — that is the work of P3.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Agents, listen. Bruce Wayne is a businessman. Clark Kent writes for a newspaper. Peter Parker takes photos. Every hero has a day job — and that day job is the disguise. Today you build your own. Each crew picks a person — real or invented — writes the file: what they do, where they work, what they wear — and defends it while the class fires yes/no questions. Twenty questions maximum. Crack the file and you take the points. Files — open!”
The gist, if you need to restate it: every superhero has a “day job” — the disguise. Today each crew builds that kind of file on their own person and defends it: the class asks yes/no questions, twenty maximum. If they crack it, the points go to them.

🚀 Bonus level · not in the book
crews

WHAT WE DO: the level for our students: the suffix machine and the sound a native speaker hears a job by

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Weak file: 'He works.' Useless. A professional says the JOB — and the ending tells you it IS a job.
  2. The suffix machine (Job suffixes p.85, TB key ex.6): -er: photographer, police officer, waiter, lawyer, singer, taxi driver, engineer · -or: doctor · -ist: dentist, journalist, receptionist, scientist · -ant: sales assistant.
  3. The sound (Pronunciation p.117 — the schwa /ə/): The ending is never loud. Say it lazy: doct-ə, sing-ə, lawy-ə. Check against TB key ex.4: “She's a lawyer.” — two /ə/ · “My brother's an actor.” — three.
  4. TB ABCD note: Always a or an with a job. And most job names work for men and women — police officer, head teacher, flight attendant.
📺 On screen: a slide with the suffix machine -er / -or / -ist / -ant (Backup: four chalk columns on the board, students come up and add their own words; do not wipe them off before the end of the lesson)
👦 Students do: students sort words into the columns and stretch the schwa. Expect: the strong ones bring in manager and accountant themselves. Typical problem: “policeman” — correct it on the spot, it is the TB ABCD note

Product: the “secret identity” file
PRODUCT · the secret identity file
crews → stage

WHAT WE DO: a 60-second file: who they are + what they do + where they work + the detail that gives them away; then the defence under the class's yes/no questions (ex.7)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 7 minutes of preparation: Keywords only, no scripts. Everyone owns one block: name and job · what they do · where they work · the detail that gives them away.
  2. The intelligence rule: Every job word in English. A Russian word = the file is blown.
  3. The defence, from ex.7: Class — twenty yes/no questions, no more. 'Do you work in an office?' 'Do you cook food?' 'Are you a waiter?'
  4. Sixty seconds per crew. Judges: fluency, accuracy, richness — cards up!
📺 On screen: 60-second timer · judges' cards · the rubric (Backup: keep the question count in tally marks on the board — no screen needed)
👦 Students do: students perform in their roles and fend off the questions. Expect: the bar holds the format. Typical problem: reading off the page — the judges take fluency off

WB Unit 7 (pp. 52–59) · points onto the leaderboard next lesson
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, crews can name 25 jobs and say what each person does (A mechanic repairs cars and machines) and run a 60-second identity file under yes/no questioning.
  • Subsidiary. The gate exposes the real ceiling; ex.1 and ex.3 (track 44) are closed as a race; ex.4–5 turn into a duel; job suffixes (-er/-or/-ist/-ant) and the schwa /ə/ are taken as the bonus level; Video skills (Susie Wolff) is watched like producers.
  • Personal. Do NOT announce the past as the topic: for now it is simply “how people are talked about”. Log “She's lawyer”, “He's a engineer” silently — that is the material for the error clinic in P3.
By the end, students can…
  • write 15+ job words in 90 seconds
  • say what a person in each job does
  • hear a job suffix and predict the ending (-er / -or / -ist / -ant)
  • hold a 60-second identity file under yes/no questions

Form

Pron. watch

Concept checks (CCQs) · Assumptions
“She's lawyer” — what is missing? → the article: She's A lawyer.
“He's a engineer” — why not? → before a vowel sound AN engineer.
“You know jobs. But can you write 15 words in 90 seconds?” → the gate.

Assumptions: SB pp.84–85, syllabus pair 54. Some words are familiar from A1+ (teacher, doctor, singer, waiter); new ones — accountant, receptionist, sales assistant, web designer, mechanic, engineer, journalist, businessman/businesswoman.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
“We know jobs already” from the doorwayDon't argue — show the gate: not recognition but WRITING for 90 seconds, spelling included. The score is private, the rematch comes at the end of the lesson.
The file shrinks to a single factThe intelligence rule: three compulsory blocks — what they do · where they work · the one detail that gives them away. Judges take a point off for “just the job”.
The strong ones take all the air timeRoles inside the file: name and job · what they do · where they work · the one who answers the class. Everybody speaks aloud at least once.
Classroom management — signals
«Eyes up in 3-2-1» — count down calmly → all eyes on you at «1». No shouting over noise, ever.
Visible timer — timer on screen = we work; timer stops = pens/hands down. The timer is the boss, not you.
Volume dial — arm up/down = louder/quieter; «radio whisper» for rehearsals.
«Boards up!» — mini-whiteboard rounds: everyone commits, all boards up together — nobody hides, nobody shouts first.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, class answers with two and freezes — instant reset mid-game.
English-first — ideas in Russian are welcome, POINTS only for the English version — recast and repeat.
