🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use defining and non-defining relative clauses accurately — who, which, that, whose, where, when, why — including the two decisions that carry the meaning: when to put the commas in, and when the pronoun can disappear altogether. Then use eleven phrasal verbs of technology (back up, log on, pop up, run out of, pick up, set up).
What is genuinely hard
The comma. Russian punctuates every subordinate clause, so the whole group writes non-defining clauses by accident, and in English those two commas mean «you already know which one». The second difficulty is the mirror image of a Russian habit: «который» stays in the sentence and takes nothing with it, so students both keep an extra pronoun (a phone that IT takes great pictures) and refuse to drop the one that could go.
Typical error to expect
«My phone, which I bought last year, is broken» when there are three phones — Russian puts a comma in front of every subordinate clause, so the whole group writes non-defining clauses by accident. In English the two commas mean «extra information, you already know which one»; without them the clause tells the reader WHICH one. It is punctuation that changes meaning, and it is marked.
«I've got a phone that it takes great pictures» — the relative pronoun IS the subject, so the second pronoun has to go. Russian keeps «который» and adds nothing, which is why the extra it feels invisible.
«Alexa, that is a smart speaker, was launched in 2014» — that is impossible in a non-defining clause. Only who, which, whose, where and when survive after a comma.
Why the lesson is built this way
The grammar comes out of yesterday's reading — the six sentences on p.112 are taken from the graphene text the class has already read, so nothing has to be understood twice. Discovery is done as a sorting job (which sentences have commas? what changes?) rather than as a rule, and the rule is written by the class. The meaning-of-the-comma point is taught with the Teacher's Book sentence pair about sisters, because it is the one moment where students SEE that punctuation carries information. The lesson then turns outwards: the exam-style cloze on screen time, and phrasal verbs that describe what their own phones do every day.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“My sister, who has fair hair, is studying engineering. My sister who has fair hair is studying engineering. One of those sentences tells you I have several sisters. Two commas, one piece of information. Today you learn to put them where you mean them.”

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: six sentences from the graphene article sorted into two families

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open at p.112. Point out where the six sentences come from: they are the text the class read yesterday, so nothing has to be understood twice.
  2. ex.1 as a whole-class sort, question by question. Take a, b and c orally and fast: 1, 2 and 5 have no commas; 3, 4 and 6 have them; which for things, who for people.
  3. Question d in pairs, ninety seconds: whose, when, where, why — what does each one point at? (possessions, times, places, reasons.)

  6. The class writes the rule in two lines, in their own words. You do not dictate it.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2: error correction, where every error is one the class actually makes

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that puts the grammar into bodies

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Six students get a card each: WHO · WHICH · THAT · WHOSE · WHERE · a big red COMMA. Two more hold the two halves of a sentence.

  3. Four sentences, four assemblies, and the comma card is the one that causes the argument every time. That argument is the learning.
  4. Rotate the cards after every sentence so twelve students are on their feet inside four minutes.
  5. Sit down straight into ex.3.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3a and 3b: where that is possible, and where the pronoun can go altogether

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Before ex.3a, elicit the two conditions and write them up: that is possible only in a DEFINING clause, and only for a person or a thing — never for a place.
  2. ex.3a, three minutes: twelve items. Answers — a that, b that, c that, d –, e –, f –, g –, h that, i –, j that, k that, l that.
  3. Before ex.3b, elicit the three conditions: defining clause, the pronoun is who/which/that, and a noun or pronoun follows it immediately.
  4. ex.3b, two minutes: which pronouns can be dropped? c, g, h, j and l. Then the reading-aloud test — read each sentence without the pronoun and hear that it survives.
  5. Fast finishers get the Teacher's Book pair task: My flatmate, who makes a lot of noise, is annoying against My flatmate who makes a lot of noise is annoying — how many flatmates?

WHAT WE DO: p.113 ex.1–2: eleven phrasal verbs met in context, then defined

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Warmer straight out of the Teacher's Book, thirty seconds, books shut: Why do some laptops take so long to start up? What should you do before you shut down your computer? Two phrasal verbs found, two meanings elicited.
  2. ex.1, four minutes: six gaps with the correct form of the verb. Answers — 1 ran (out of), 2 pick (up), 3 pop (up), 4 back (up), 5 runs (on), 6 log (on).
  3. Elicit each meaning as it is confirmed: run out of — have no more · pick up — detect · pop up — appear · back up — copy your files · run on — take its power from · log on — get access.
  4. The transitivity note is worth ninety seconds because it decides sentences: run OUT OF takes an object (we've run out of time), run out does not (my battery is running out).
  5. ex.2, three minutes in pairs: go off, set up, zoom in, scroll, key in — write a definition for each in your own words.
  6. Drill the stress by clapping: back UP, log ON, pop UP, run OUT of. The particle is the loud part, and that is why they never hear these verbs in a recording.
📺 On screen: the eleven verbs go up in the third column of the unit wall as they are confirmed — paper only
👦 Students do: complete six gaps, define five more verbs in pairs, and clap the stress onto the particle

Structure
Graphene, which is one atom thick, is the strongest material we know. · The professor who discovered it works in Manchester. · That's the app (that) I use every day. · The man whose phone was stolen …
Form
defining clause — no commas, essential information, pronoun may be that, pronoun may be dropped when a noun or pronoun follows it · non-defining clause — two commas, extra information, never that, never dropped · which can refer back to a whole sentence.
Pron. watch
the stress moves onto the particle in every phrasal verb — back UP, log ON, pop UP, run OUT of — and the verb reduces, which is exactly why the class does not hear them in a recording · in non-defining clauses the voice drops before the comma and lifts after it; that pause IS the punctuation in speech
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«My phone, which is waterproof, is two years old.» How many phones have I got? → one. Take the commas out — how many now? → more than one. That is why the commas are not decoration.
«Alexa, that is a smart speaker, was launched in 2014» — possible? → no. After a comma we never use that.
«That's the app that I use» — can we delete «that»? → yes, a noun or pronoun follows it. «That's the app that works» — can we? → no, a verb follows.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Commas everywhereThe two-sister sentence pair stays on the board from minute six. Every disputed comma is settled by the same question, never by a rule: How many sisters have I got?
«that» after a commaWrite it up as a banned combination — comma + that — and let the class police it in the pair check. Peer correction fixes this one faster than teacher correction because it is visual.
The extra pronoun (a phone that it takes …)Ask for the subject of the relative clause out loud every time: Who takes the pictures? The phone. Then the phone is already there — delete it.
The cloze on p.112 looks like pure grammar and it is notSay it before they start: five of the ten items test vocabulary and earlier grammar, not relative pronouns. Students who expect only pronouns lose those five and blame the exercise.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 19–25 (grammar in a text) and tasks 32–38 (the multiple-choice lexical cloze the class meets today in its Cambridge form).

Relative clauses are not a separate line of the codifier, but they are the engine of the long sentence that tasks 19–25 are built out of, and the comma decides the meaning: my sister who lives in Moscow (I have several) against my sister, who lives in Moscow (I have one). Russian punctuates every subordinate clause with a comma, so the whole class will over-comma unless the difference is taught as MEANING. The phrasal verbs are quieter but heavily represented in tasks 32–38, where run out of and log on appear as distractors for each other.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
