🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Produce, sort and use the chunk vocabulary of Prepare 3 across six topics — travelling · city and places · sport and outdoor life · health · free time · shops and money — in sentences, not in lists.
What is genuinely hard
Recognising a chunk and producing it are different abilities, and after ten weeks off only the first survives. The group will understand almost everything today and produce about half — which feels like failure to them and is completely normal. The teacher's advantage over last year: the real Prepare 3 lists are in this plan, so the gaps are measured against what was actually taught, not guessed.
Typical error to expect
The chunk comes back half-melted: do a picnic, go to kayaking, at chemist — the verb or the little word wrong. And in the placement slice: answering band 3 (this year's language) slowly and painfully instead of guessing and moving on.
Why the lesson is built this way
Nothing is handed out in the first half — not one list. Everything is retrieval: they build six word maps from memory, write their own Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards, and defend categories in the team quiz. A chunk pulled out of memory is strengthened; a chunk read off a list is not — and the half-melted chunks they produce are exactly the data you need. Then, mid-lesson and not at the end, the measurement: questions 1–40 of the real Cambridge Placement Test, by the Teacher's Guide's own protocol, because band 2 (11–20) IS last year and answers «did the year hold» with an official table. It sits in the middle so the lesson does not end on a paper — the last stage is speaking, and they leave talking.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“You think the summer deleted your English. Let's check that properly. First: six topics from last year, no lists from me, everything out of your own heads — you will surprise yourselves. Then forty questions from Cambridge. Ten are baby level, ten are LAST year, ten are THIS year — you are not supposed to know those yet — and ten are for the brave. No mark, nothing in the register. You mark it yourself, and the number is yours. Then you talk for six minutes straight.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, the first error feedback of the year, and the bridge into the six topics

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — feedback from lesson 1. Four sentences from your correction sheet on the board, no names: Where you went in the summer? · I have been to Turkey last July. · I go to my grandmother in June. · What you did there?
  3. Kagan RallyCoach in pairs, two minutes: one pen, one writes the repair, the other says why. Then all four aloud. Name the two rules out of it: one past marker per sentence · a finished time pushes the present perfect out.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the lesson: All of that is grammar. Today is the other half: the words. Six topics from last year — and they are all still in there.
📺 On screen: the four sentences in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair four sentences in pairs. Expect the missing did to be found fast and I have been … last July to cause the argument — which is exactly the rehearsal lesson 3 needs. Typical problem: they fix the form but cannot say why; make them say it

Lesson frame

Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the six topics on the board as six headings and nothing else — no words under them yet.
  2. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: today the words come out of you, not out of me — in chunks, with their little words attached. By the end you use ten of them in true sentences.
  3. Name the measurement now rather than springing it later: In the middle of the lesson: forty questions from Cambridge, twenty minutes. No mark, nothing in the register — you mark it yourself and the number is yours.
  4. Set the bar: A word only scores if your team can put it in a sentence — and the chunk must be whole: «go camping», not «camping».
📺 On screen: the six topic headings in chalk — the board is the medium here and the headings stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: read the six headings and start volunteering words before you ask. Expect the announcement of the paper to drop the temperature for ten seconds and then be forgotten — which is why it is announced early

8–20′
Word maps · six topics of Prepare 3
teams of 3–4
Why: Retrieval, not re-teaching. Nothing is given to them: the chunks have to come out of their own heads, which is what strengthens memory — and unlike last year, YOU hold the real Prepare 3 lists, so you can see precisely which taught chunks did not come back.

WHAT WE DO: Kagan Jot Thoughts: six topics, everything out of their own heads, against the clock

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. One A3 sheet per team, folded into six. Kagan Jot Thoughts: everybody writes at once, one chunk per slip, no discussion, no repeats.
  2. Ninety seconds per topic; call the changes yourself. The six topics — and the chunks you should see coming back, because they were TAUGHT last year:
    • Travelling and holidays (P3 Units 3, 7, 20) — travel by plane · go on foot · by underground · get sunburnt · lose your wallet · go camping · get lost · a sleeping bag · change planes · go horse riding
    • City and places (P3 Units 11, 4) — an art gallery · a tall skyscraper · walk around the old town · a fountain · visit the palace · a football stadium · on the ground floor · a cosy living room · a peaceful place
    • Sport, games and outdoor life (P3 Units 9, 13) — play a board game · do karate · wear goggles · a membership card · climb a tree · explore a cave · kayak down a river · camp under the stars
    • Staying healthy (P3 Unit 17) — stay healthy · hurt yourself · break a bone · fall off your bike · by myself · help yourselves
    • Free time: films, music, books (P3 Units 12, 15, 18) — a horror film · an animated film · an exciting thriller · play an instrument · go out with friends · spend time online · have a laugh · take out a book · read it from cover to cover
    • Shops and money (P3 Unit 14) — at the chemist's · a department store · fresh bread from the bakery · buy a magazine at the newsagent's · get pocket money
  3. ⚠️ The lists above are for you, not for them — the minimum to expect, drawn from the school's own Prepare 3 materials. Do not put them on the board.
  4. After the sixth topic: teams count their chunks and shout the number. Then the frame condition: Cross out every chunk nobody in your team can put in a sentence. The second number is the honest one.
  5. Walk round with your notebook and note which topics come back thin — that is the real result of this stage.
📺 On screen: the six headings on the board and A3 paper on the desks — paper is deliberately the medium so the maps can be kept and reused in lesson 3's warm-up, and nothing can fail, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write chunks simultaneously, then cut their own list down. Expect «free time» and «sport» rich, «shops and money» thin. Typical problem: single words instead of chunks — model one public upgrade and the maps fix themselves

20–30′
Quiz-Quiz-Trade · on their own chunks
standing, whole class
Why: The cards are written by the class in the previous stage, so every card is a chunk somebody in the room could produce and somebody else could not. That is a better deck than any printed pack — and it costs nothing.

WHAT WE DO: the class's own chunks turned into cards and traded round the room

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Each team picks its ten best chunks and writes ten cards: the chunk on one side, a definition or a gapped example sentence on the other. Four minutes.
  2. ⚠️ Enforce one thing while they write: The clue may not be a translation. A definition or a gapped sentence, nothing else — this is the part that does the learning.
  3. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade, five minutes: quiz your partner, get quizzed, swap cards, find somebody new. Six trades each.
  4. Freeze the room twice and take one card at random: Read your clue. Everybody — the chunk.
  5. Collect the cards — they are the free revision deck for lesson 3 and for lesson 8's block review.
📺 On screen: blank cards on the desks and the ten-card instruction on the board — cards and chalk are the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write ten cards, then mingle and trade. Expect the writing slower than the trading and the first definitions poor — the two freezes fix that faster than any explanation. Typical error: translation clues; rewrite one publicly
blank cards · marker pens · board
30–38′
Team quiz · odd one out and say why
pairs → teams
Why: Sorting forces meaning to be handled rather than spelling. The «why» is the whole task, and collecting answers with Numbered Heads Together makes every member of every team own every reason — the syllabus's team quiz, delivered.

WHAT WE DO: eight sets of four, one intruder each — and the reason is the task

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Eight sets on the board, all built from chunks the class produced twenty minutes ago. For example: goggles · a membership card · a sleeping bag · a fountain — or the chemist's · the bakery · the newsagent's · the old town — or go camping · go horse riding · do karate · get sunburnt.
  2. Pairs, four minutes: cross out the odd one and write the reason in English. The answer is the reason, not the word.
  3. Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: the team agrees, then a called number answers for the team.
  4. Give the arguable set the airtime it earns — get sunburnt is an accident among activities, but a student who argues «all four happen on holiday» has made a category and deserves the point.
  5. Fast finishers: build one more set of four for the class and put it on the board themselves.
📺 On screen: the eight sets written on the board — the board is the medium here and the sets come from the class's own paper, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: solve in pairs with a written reason, then answer by number. Expect the sets to be solved fast and the reasons slowly — the reason is the language. Typical error: a one-word reason («different»); push for a full sentence every time

WHAT WE DO: two minutes of movement and the switch from words to the paper

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. I say a chunk — if it is about holidays, hands up; if it is about money, sit down; if it is neither, freeze. Ten chunks, fast: get sunburnt · pocket money · wear goggles · a department store · change planes · break a bone · the bakery · go camping · play an instrument · lose your wallet.
  2. Then chairs in, desks cleared, one pen out — the desk must be clean before the paper appears, and doing it standing costs nothing.

👦 Students do: move on ten chunks and clear their desks. Expect an argument about lose your wallet (holiday or money?) — one sentence settles it and the argument is free revision. Typical problem: they stay standing and talking; the clean-desk instruction closes the stage

Measurement briefing

Why: The four minutes that decide whether the next twenty produce data or anxiety. The rules are said before the paper appears: no mark, no register, self-checked — and band three is this year's language, so not knowing it is the expected result.

WHAT WE DO: the four minutes that decide whether the next twenty produce data or anxiety

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Papers face down on the desks before you say anything. Then the rules, in this order:
  2. Forty questions. Twenty minutes. Four options each — read all four before you choose. No mark. Nothing in the register. You mark it yourself afterwards.
  3. Then the band map, because it is what makes the paper fair: 1–10 — two years ago: fast. 11–20 — LAST year: this is the real check. 21–30 — THIS year: nobody has taught you these, so a guess is the correct move. 31–40 — next year: for the brave.
  4. One working rule: Thirty seconds a question. Don't know it — guess and move on. A blank scores the same as a wrong answer; a guess sometimes does not.
  5. Names on the sheets. Turn them over — go. Timer visibly on.
📺 On screen: the four rules and the band map written on the board while you say them, plus the visible timer — chalk and a timer are the medium here on purpose, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: listen with the paper face down. Expect one question about marks even though you have answered it — answer it again, calmly, the same way. Typical problem: somebody starts early; that is what «face down» is for

solo · silence
Why: A real Cambridge instrument used by its own Teacher's Guide rules: four bands, an official key, an official table. Twenty minutes buys a defensible answer to «did last year hold» for every student — including anyone who walked in from another school this week.

WHAT WE DO: the real Cambridge Placement Test, four bands, twenty minutes, silent mode

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Silent mode, palm down. Walk the room slowly; answer nothing about content, only procedure.
  2. Call the time at 8 and at 15 minutes with a target: Eight minutes — you should be near question sixteen. That one announcement saves three students from spending nine minutes on question six.
  3. At question 21 territory, one calm reminder if you see agonising: Band three is this year's language. Guessing it is the correct move.
  4. ⚠️ Stop on the timer even if half the class is at question 34. An unfinished paper is honest data; an overrunning paper eats the speaking stage.
  5. It is the same series-wide instrument the school used for the Prepare 3 group — byte-identical file, checked.
📺 On screen: the visible timer and nothing else on display — the room is deliberately quiet and free of anything projected for these twenty minutes, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: work alone in silence for twenty minutes. Expect 1–10 fast, 11–20 slower, 21–30 largely guessed and 31–40 finished in a rush — that is the designed shape of the instrument, not a failure. Typical problem: a student stops at 25 because «the rest is too hard»; tell them to guess and finish

Self-check + the personal gap list
solo → solo
Why: They mark their own paper against the printed key and the number stays theirs. What leaves the room is not a score but a list of chunks they could not produce — a homework task that writes itself.

WHAT WE DO: marking your own paper against the official key, and turning it into a list

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand out the key, one per desk, and read it aloud in blocks of ten so nobody gets lost. Ticks and crosses only, in their own hand.
  2. Then the numbers that matter, written on the sheet: out of 10 for each band, then out of 40. Official placement table (Teacher's Guide): 0–9 → Prepare 1 · 10–18 → Prepare 2 · 19–27 → Prepare 3 · 28–36 → Prepare 4 · 37–45 → Prepare 5. Out of our 40: 28–36 means this student is exactly where they should be; 37–40 means the entry point is already passed.
  3. ⚠️ Do not read scores out and do not ask for them. Walk round with your notebook and write one line per name — band 2 first. That notebook is the only record.
  4. The task that turns the paper into something useful: Look at your crosses in questions eleven to twenty. Write the six topics down the side of a page and put a dot where each cross belongs.
  5. Papers into the folder, keys collected. That page is your revision plan for the autumn. Nobody else has yours.
📺 On screen: the printed key, one per desk, read aloud in blocks of ten (Backup: if the key has not been photocopied, read all forty answers from the teacher's copy slowly, twice, and have them tick in pencil)
👦 Students do: mark their own forty, write five numbers, build a topic list. Expect relief at the band map — questions 21–40 were never theirs to know. Typical problem: two students comparing scores out loud; cut it immediately and remind them the number is private

🎯 Speaking out · one minute on each topic
pairs, standing
Why: The productive outcome: the chunks come back out in sentences, under a timer, to a partner. A chunk said in a sentence today is a chunk that survives to lesson 4.

WHAT WE DO: Timed Pair Share on all six topics — the chunks come back out in sentences

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everybody up, new partner, not the one they sat with. Kagan Timed Pair Share: sixty seconds each way on one topic, then change partners.
  2. The six prompts, one per round: A journey I remember… · My town, the good and the bad… · Sport and games in my life… · The last time I hurt myself or was ill… · What I watch, read and play… · What I do with my pocket money…
  3. The condition that makes it a language stage: Five chunks minimum from today's maps, and your partner counts them on their fingers.
  4. After round three, freeze and take two examples aloud — one good, one that stopped. Do not name the second; rebuild it with the class.
  5. Log errors silently; correct nothing aloud. The feedback slot opens lesson 3.
📺 On screen: the six prompts on the board, revealed one per round — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: speak for six minutes each across six rounds, counting each other's chunks. Expect rounds five and six to be the best English of the day — the chunks have been handled four times by then. Typical error: go to kayaking, do a picnic — both onto the sheet

WHAT WE DO: an “I can…” self-check, homework built from their own gap list, a teaser

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

Structure
I got sunburnt on the second day. · You have to wear goggles at the pool. · We walked around the old town for two hours. · I've read it from cover to cover. · I spend my pocket money on games.
Form
Nothing new is taught today. The chunks carry their own grammar — verb + noun with the right little word (go camping · do karate · play a board game · at the chemist's) — and that is precisely what is checked: the chunk whole, not the word alone.
Pron. watch
the stress inside the long chunks, because that is where a known chunk becomes unrecognisable: an ART gallery · a de-PART-ment store · SLEEP-ing bag · POCK-et money.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«go to kayaking» — what is wrong? → no to: go kayaking. The chunk is go + -ing.
lend or borrow: «Can I ___ your charger?» → borrow. You borrow FROM somebody, you lend TO somebody.
«I hurt» — finished? → no: I hurt myself. Which topic shelf does it live on? → staying healthy.
ICQ before the placement slice: How many questions? (forty) How long? (twenty minutes) Which ten are you NOT supposed to know? (21–30 — this year's) Does it go in the register? (no)
«I don't know question 33 at all.» Is that a problem? → no. Questions 31–40 are next year's language. Guess and move on.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Half the group is convinced the summer deleted everythingThis is why the word maps come first and the paper second. After twelve minutes their own sheet has fifty chunks on it — an argument nothing you say can match. Point at the sheet, not at them.
A word map that fills up with single words instead of chunksModel one correction publicly: «museum» — half a point. «Visit the museum» — a point. The little words ARE the topic. The maps upgrade themselves within a minute.
The placement slice runs over twenty minutesStop it on the timer and have them mark what they reached. An unfinished paper is honest data; an overrunning paper eats the speaking stage, which is what makes them leave talking.
A student scores 37 or more out of 40Tell them privately and act on it: Plus versions of the vocabulary and grammar worksheets from lesson 4 onwards, with the sentence that keeps a strong teenager on board: You are ahead, so your sheets are harder. Never announce it to the class.
A student scores under 19 and knows itDo not soften, do not dramatise: Ten of those questions are language nobody taught you yet, and ten more are next year's. Look at your first twenty — that is your real number. Then Standard worksheets, weekly workbook checks — and a quiet word with the head of studies about whether the room is right.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the OGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
