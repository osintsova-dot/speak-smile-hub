🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use ten travel verbs and phrases (set off, check in, land, reach, sail, go away, be held up, tour, unpack, abroad) and choose between the modals of obligation, necessity and advice — have to, need to, don't have to, needn't, should — plus let and make, which Russian expresses with one verb and English with two.
What is genuinely hard
Four modal meanings that Russian flattens into two: a rule (have to), something important but not a rule (need to), a free choice (don't have to, needn't) and advice (should). The expensive confusion is don't have to against shouldn't, and it is worth eight marks on the block test. Then let and make, where the pattern is the trap: both take a bare infinitive, and Russian speakers add to every time.
Typical error to expect
«I must to go», «you don't must» — must takes a bare infinitive; need takes to (need TO buy) except in needn't (needn't take). The Student's Book plants both traps in the same exercise.
«My parents let me to go», «she made me to wait» — both verbs take a bare infinitive: let somebody DO, make somebody DO. Russian uses one verb for both meanings, which is why the difference has to be taught as choice against force.
Why the lesson is built this way
The topic does the work today: a blog post about travelling without your parents, which is a subject sixteen-year-olds have opinions about before the lesson starts. The vocabulary arrives through four speakers and four photographs, then goes straight into a matching task so it is used twice within ten minutes. The reading is organised by headings, which is a different reading skill from the last three blocks and worth naming. The grammar is built as a two-by-two grid on the board — NECESSARY / NOT NECESSARY against RULE / ADVICE — because the difficulty is meaning, not form, and a grid is a picture of meaning. The productive stage is a set of rules the class writes for a trip they invent, which forces every modal in the grid.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“In Britain you can fly alone at sixteen. Between eleven and fifteen you need a special form at the check-in desk. Two airlines will not take you at all until you are sixteen. Today: the verbs of travelling — and the difference between «you don't have to» and «you shouldn't», which is worth eight marks next week and rather more at an airport.”

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and draw the two-by-two grid

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4 under a clock: the words found, then immediately re-used

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that sequences the verbs physically

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ten students get one verb card each and stand in a line in the order a journey happens. The rest of the class directs them from their seats, in English only.

  3. Last thirty seconds, in pairs: The last time you were held up. Where, and for how long?
  4. Freeze and sit: Page sixty-nine. What you are actually allowed to do without your parents — and at what age.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: the five headings matched, which is the reading skill of the lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Background in three sentences, and it is the hook: in Britain you can fly alone at sixteen. Between eleven and fifteen you need a special form at the check-in desk. Two big airlines will not carry an unaccompanied minor at all until sixteen; two others start at fourteen.

  5. Optional: track 45 (the post is recorded) for the weakest readers.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4: the detail questions and the six highlighted words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 individually, six minutes. Rule: before you read anything, write next to each question WHICH HEADING it belongs under. Then read only that section.

Grammar · the modal grid, and let against make
class → individual
Why: Grammar · the modal grid, and let against make

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–4: four meanings placed in four squares, then the two verbs of permission and force

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Back to the empty grid. Four sentences from the text go into it, one per square: You have to show ID. · You need to check in two hours early. · You don't have to take a suitcase. · You shouldn't leave it until the last day.
  2. ex.1: the class matches the four meanings — have to is a rule, need to is important but not a rule, don't have to / needn't is a free choice, should is advice. Write the modal into its square.
  3. Then the three to-frames on the board, because the patterns are where the marks go: need TO buy · don't need TO take · needn't take.
  4. ex.2 individually, three minutes: six items. Check by asking for the SQUARE first, then the modal. Several answers are acceptable on three of the items — say so before they start.
  5. ex.3: let and make. Two sentences, one question — In which one did the person have a choice? Answer: let. Write the two patterns with the word to crossed out: let me GO, made me GO.
  6. ex.4, two minutes: six items, let or make. Check fast — made, let, make, let, let, make.
  7. Grammar reference is SB p.158 for anyone who wants it in writing.
📺 On screen: the two-by-two grid, now full, and the three to-frames underneath — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: place four sentences in four squares, then produce twelve modal answers with the square named first

🎯 Speaking out · the rules of our trip
groups → class
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · the rules of our trip

WHAT WE DO: ex.5: a real set of rules written for an invented trip, using every square of the grid

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of three. The task: your group is going away for four days without adults. Write SIX RULES for the trip — and the six must cover all four squares of the grid plus one let and one make.
  2. Six minutes. Circulate and push on the squares rather than on the content: That's three «have to»s. Where is your «don't have to»?
  3. Then ex.5 individually as a written finish, three minutes: My parents tell me I should … · When I stay with my grandparents, I don't have to … · My teachers never let me … · Last week my parents made me …
  4. 🎯 Kagan Timed Pair Share, two minutes each way: read your four sentences, and the partner asks one follow-up question about each.
  5. Take the six rules from two groups to the floor and let the class vote on the strictest set. Then correct only the modal choice — nothing else today.
  6. Close with the count: Six rules, four sentences about your own life, and every square of the grid used at least once.
📺 On screen: the full grid still on the board while they write — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: write six rules in threes covering four modal meanings, then four personal sentences and defend them in pairs

Structure
You have to check in two hours before the flight. · You don't have to take a suitcase. · You shouldn't leave it until the last day. · My parents let me travel alone. · They made me phone home every evening.
Form
have to = a rule · need to = important · don't have to / needn't / don't need to = not necessary · should / shouldn't = advice · let somebody DO (permission) and make somebody DO (force), both with a bare infinitive.
Pron. watch
abroad /əˈbrɔːd/ — one syllable of stress and no /d/ before it, and the class will say «a-bro-ad» · the on-phrases run together: /ɒnˈfʊt/, /ɒnˈtaɪm/, /ɒnˈbɔːd/ · needn't /ˈniːdnt/ loses its d · and have to is /hæftə/ in every natural sentence, which is why students do not hear it in the listening
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«You don't have to bring food.» Is it forbidden? → no, it is my choice. «You shouldn't bring food»? → it is a bad idea. Two different sentences.
«My parents let me go.» Did I have a choice? → yes, they allowed it. «They made me go»? → no choice at all.
«They let me to go» — right? → no. Let and make take the verb with no to. Say both aloud: let me GO, made me GO.

Likely problemWhat you do
don't have to is used to mean shouldn'tThe two-by-two grid stays on the board all lesson and every disputed sentence is placed in a square before it is corrected. Point at the square rather than explaining a second time.
«I must to», «let me to go»Three frames on the board: I have TO go · I need TO go · they let me GO / made me GO. The word to is written in capitals where it belongs and crossed out where it does not.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 12–18 (multiple choice on a long text) and tasks 19–25 (modal verbs).

Modals of obligation and advice are a named line of the grammar section, and the distinction the Russian exam tests is exactly the one this lesson builds: have to is a rule, need to is important, don't have to and needn't are a free choice, and should is only advice. Add let and make, which Russian expresses with one verb and English with two different patterns — let somebody DO, make somebody DO, both without to.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
