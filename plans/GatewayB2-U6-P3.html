🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Match five short monologues to what each speaker says when the answer is always a paraphrase, and use the third conditional and I wish / If only to talk about regret — a past that cannot be changed, a present you would like to be different, and other people's habits that annoy you.
What is genuinely hard
The listening fails for a mechanical reason: there are six options for five speakers, the wrong option always contains a word the speaker actually said, and students stop listening the moment they hear it. The grammar fails for a different reason: the contraction 'd is «had» in one half of a third conditional and «would» in the other, so the class hears one sound and writes one form. And wish is the structure everybody understands and nobody produces, because Russian marks regret with a particle and English marks it by moving the tense back one step.
Typical error to expect
«If I would have known, I would have called» and «If I had knew» — the third conditional needs the past perfect after if and would have + past participle after it. The contraction hides the difference: I'd is had in one half and would in the other, and students hear one sound and write one form. «I wish I have more time» / «I wish I didn't say that» — wish moves the tense one step back: present wish → past simple (I wish I had), past regret → past perfect (I wish I hadn't said). Russian marks regret with a particle and no tense change at all, so the shift feels arbitrary until it is drilled in pairs.
Why the lesson is built this way
The listening is set up so nobody can guess: the sports are identified first (ex.2) and only then the statements matched (ex.3), which forces two passes over the same recording for two different jobs. The grammar then grows straight out of what they have just heard — two of the five speakers say «I wish I'd learnt to swim properly» and «If I'd lived closer to the sea, I would have begun much earlier», so the target language is already in the room before it is named. The Naismith text on p.77 is the checkpoint: all four conditionals in one paragraph, and the only way through is to read the verb that IS given.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“A Canadian PE teacher was given fourteen days to invent an indoor sport that nobody could get hurt playing. He nailed two fruit baskets to a wall — and somebody had to climb a ladder every time anyone scored. That is basketball. Today: five people telling you about their sport, and the grammar for everything that could have happened and did not.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, ninety seconds of play/go/do recycled at speed, and the bridge into the listening

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one full sentence back: One sentence — how did you sleep last night, and how do you feel about it? Any answer counts as long as it is in English and it is a sentence.
  2. 2 minutes — play, go or do? Kagan RallyRobin in pairs: take turns naming a sport, but the sport must be preceded by the right verb — play football · go swimming · do karate. Ninety seconds, as many as they can, one point lost for a wrong verb.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that puts the new grammar into bodies before it is analysed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands in one line down the middle of the room, facing a partner.
  2. You give a prompt — a sport you gave up · a subject you stopped studying · something you ate yesterday · a message you sent — and both partners say one sentence beginning I wish I … Fifteen seconds each.
  3. One line steps one place to the right, everyone has a new partner, next prompt. Four prompts, four partners.
  4. Do not correct the form yet. Listen instead: note two sentences you will use as the discovery examples in three minutes' time.
  5. Freeze and sit: You have all just made the same grammatical decision without knowing it. Now we look at it.
📺 On screen: nothing to project — the prompts are called out and the line does the work
👦 Students do: produce four wish sentences with four different partners, standing

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: discovered from the recording, then built seven times and rewritten seven more

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Start with a sentence from the listening they have just heard, written on the board: If I'd lived closer to the sea, I would have begun surfing much earlier. Ask: did he live close to the sea? did he begin early? is this real?
  2. ex.1, four minutes as a class: answer a, b, c. Answers — a past perfect after if · b would + have + past participle in the other half · c no, it is for imaginary situations in the PAST.
  3. Fill the two coloured halves on the board and make the contraction point out loud: The first 'd is HAD. The second 'd is WOULD. They sound the same and they are not the same. Drill three sentences chorally.
  4. ex.2, four minutes individually with the Teacher's Book prompt on the board (verb after if = past perfect; other verb = would have + past participle). Check by nomination — seven sentences.
  5. ex.3, four minutes in pairs with Kagan RallyCoach: seven situations rewritten as third conditionals, all of them beginning with If. One writes, the other coaches, swap every sentence. The first is modelled in the book.
  6. Catch «would of» the moment it appears in writing, write it on the board and cross it out.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4: the Naismith Culture exchange, where every gap needs a different decision

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set it up as the checkpoint it is: Eight gaps, and they are not all the same conditional. One text, four grammars.
  2. Put the four skeletons on the board first and elicit the two that are missing — zero and first are already known, second and third go up now — so the whole system is visible in one place.
  3. Give the method the Teacher's Book gives, because it is the whole trick: Read the verb that IS given. Its tense tells you the tense of the one that is missing. Model gap a aloud: the sentence says «would be», so the verb after if is past simple — didn't exist.
  4. ex.4, five minutes individually, then compared in pairs. Answers: a didn't exist · b 'll see · c hadn't been · d had had · e don't/won't get · f wouldn't have made · g had · h would have forgotten.
  5. Fast finishers get the Teacher's Book questions on the board: what did James Naismith do, why did he do it, and why was it difficult to get the ball back? The answer to the third one is worth telling the whole class — the original baskets were fruit baskets with no hole in the bottom.
📺 On screen: the four conditional skeletons written on the board together for the first time — chalk only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: read a continuous text and choose between four conditionals gap by gap, using the given verb as the clue

pairs → individual → class
Why: Grammar · I wish / If only

WHAT WE DO: ex.5–6: three different uses separated, then six sentences produced

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read sentences a, b and c on p.77 with the class and elicit what wish means — to want something that is unlikely or impossible.
  2. ex.5, four minutes in pairs: the six questions that separate the three uses. Answers: 1 past perfect · 2 a PAST situation · 3 past simple · 4 a PRESENT situation · 5 would(n't) · 6 a habitual behaviour we want to criticise and change.
  3. Put the three uses on the board as three lines and keep them there: I wish I HAD more time — present · I wish I HAD HAD more time — past · I wish you WOULDN'T interrupt — somebody else's habit.
  4. The Teacher's Book test, ninety seconds: put «I wish I was better at maths» and «I wish you wouldn't always interrupt me» on the board and ask which refers to the present. Both do — and that is the point that makes the third use click.
  5. ex.6, four minutes individually: six sentences with the right form. Answers: 1 had · 2 wouldn't shout · 3 hadn't bought · 4 'd gone · 5 was/were · 6 'd clean. For each one, the student names WHICH of the three uses it is.
  6. If time is short, ex.7 (the blog «Study versus Sport») goes home instead — say so now rather than starting it and abandoning it.
📺 On screen: the three uses of wish written on the board as three parallel lines — chalk only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: separate three uses of wish in pairs, then produce six wish sentences and name the use of each

🎯 Three true wishes
individual → groups of four
Why: 🎯 Three true wishes

WHAT WE DO: ex.8–9: one sentence for each use, all of them true, then defended in groups

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 🎯 ex.8, five minutes, silent and individual: three sentences with I wish — one about a PRESENT situation you would like to be different, one about a PAST situation, and one about somebody who does something you would like to change. All three must be true.
  2. Circulate and fix the form quietly at the desk, not aloud. This is the moment the tense shift is either learnt or lost.
  3. 🎯 ex.9, six minutes, groups of four, Kagan Round Robin: each student reads one sentence and the other three must each ask a follow-up question. Put the questions on the board — Why do you say that? Why do you regret it? Why does it annoy you? Have you spoken to them about it?
  4. Three rounds so that all three sentences get an audience.
  5. Four minutes to the floor: take the best sentence from each group and put three of them on the wall under REGRET, written by their authors.
  6. Suggest, as the Teacher's Book does, that they learn their own three sentences by heart. It sounds old-fashioned and it works: the form sticks to a sentence that means something.
  7. Delayed correction: three sentences you heard, one with would after if, one wish without the tense shift, one «would of». Unattributed on the board, repaired by the class.

Structure
If I'd lived closer to the sea, I would have begun much earlier. · If she hadn't injured her knee, she'd have gone for a run. · I wish I'd learnt to swim properly. · I wish I was/were fitter. · I wish you wouldn't interrupt me.
Form
third conditional: if + past perfect, would(n't) have + past participle · I wish / If only + past simple = a present situation you want to be different · I wish / If only + past perfect = a past regret · I wish / If only + would(n't) + infinitive = somebody's habit you want to criticise and change.
Pron. watch
the contraction is the trap and it has to be drilled aloud: in If I'd lived closer, I'd have begun the first 'd is HAD and the second is WOULD, and they sound identical · would have collapses to /wʊdəv/ and is then written «would of», which is the single commonest spelling error in B2 writing · hadn't and wouldn't have are the two forms to over-articulate when modelling.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«If I'd lived closer to the sea, I would have begun much earlier.» Did he live close to the sea? → no. Did he begin early? → no. So is this real or imaginary? → imaginary, and it is finished — nothing can change it.
«I wish I was fitter» — am I fit now? → no. «I wish I'd trained harder» — when? → in the past, and it is too late. Which one uses the past perfect?
«I wish you wouldn't shout.» Am I talking about the past? → no. Am I making a wish about myself? → no — about somebody else's habit, and I want it to stop.

«If I would have known …»Put the two halves on the board in different colours and keep them there: IF + had + past participle in one colour, would have + past participle in the other. Any sentence with would after if gets a silent hand from the class and is repaired by its author.
«would of» in writingCatch it the first time it appears and make it visible: write would of on the board and cross it out. Say plainly that it is what would have sounds like and that it costs a mark every time in an exam script.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
