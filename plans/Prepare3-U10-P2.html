🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Build and use the present simple passive — is / are + past participle — and, more importantly, decide when it is the right choice and when the active is.
What is genuinely hard
Not the form. The form is two words and takes four minutes. The hard part is that Russian marks the passive on the verb itself, so the extra word is feels redundant and disappears — and that the choice between active and passive is made by looking at the subject, which nobody thinks to do.
Typical error to expect
It gave to me by my uncle instead of It was given to me — the passive built without be. Russian marks the passive on the verb ending, English marks it with a second word, and the second word is the one that disappears (SB p.113 ex.5) [high priority, A2/B1]
The letter was writed / The cakes were baked next to The photo was taked — an invented third form. The passive is only as good as the irregular verb list on SB p.167 [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
Meaning arrives before form and it arrives as a quiz: six «every day around the world» facts with the numbers missing. Guessing them is fun, and every one of the six is a passive waiting to be built, so the class rewrites sentences it has already argued about. The controlled practice is a single connected text rather than a list, because the active-or-passive decision cannot be made sentence by sentence in isolation — two of the twelve gaps in the orange-juice text are deliberately active, and those two are the whole lesson. The Grammar reference page gets its own slot rather than being sent home: this is the block where the form has to end up somewhere they can look it up. The final task is a product invented by a pair, because you cannot describe how a snack is made without the structure, and nobody has to be told to use it.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Twelve billion phone calls are made every day. Not «people make». «Are made». There is an extra word in that sentence and English needs it. By the end of today you will know exactly when to put it in — and you will invent a snack and tell the class how it is made.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, three minutes of collective feedback, and the bridge into the grammar

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

WHAT WE DO: name the one structure of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

Why: Six numbers to guess turn a grammar lesson into a quiz for four minutes, and every one of the six sentences is a passive waiting to be rewritten. Meaning first, form second.

WHAT WE DO: six «every day around the world» facts with the numbers missing — a guessing game that turns out to be the grammar

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.214, the meaning-first way in if the class needs one. Dictate the six sentences with the numbers missing and let them guess (phones allowed): every day around the world people make ______ phone calls (12 billion) · eat ______ bananas (1.6 billion) · drink ______ litres of water (14 billion) · buy ______ cars (200,000) · cut down ______ trees (40 million) · kill ______ sharks (250,000). Then ask what the subject of all six is (people) and whether that is worth saying (no) — and rewrite them: 12 billion phone calls are made. 1.6 billion bananas are eaten. 14 billion litres of water are drunk. 200,000 cars are bought. 40 million trees are cut down. 250,000 sharks are killed.
  2. Run it as a quiz first: teams write six numbers, closest team wins. Phones allowed for the last two if the room has gone quiet.
  3. Then the two questions that turn the quiz into the lesson: What is the subject of all six sentences? (people) Is that worth saying? (no).
  4. Rewrite two of the six together on the board, then Kagan RallyRobin in pairs for the other four, out loud, no writing.
  5. Leave all six passive versions on the board — they are the model bank for the next stage.
📺 On screen: the six facts written up with the numbers blanked out — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed; the phones are optional and the stage works without them
👦 Students do: guess six numbers, then rewrite the sentences. Expect the shark figure to cause an argument and the trees figure to shock them. Typical error: 12 billion phone calls make — the missing are, which is what the two boxes on the board are for

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.109: find the passives in Tuesday's cereal text and complete the two rules

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books at p.108, the text they already know. Pairs, 3 minutes: Underline every verb that is two words long. They will find eight or nine.
  2. ex.1 on p.109, in pairs: complete the two rules. Key: 1 to be + the past participle · 2 we don't always have to say who does the action.
  3. Now the CCQs, and take them slowly — they are the lesson, not a check: «Cereals are eaten everywhere.» Who eats them? → we are not told, and it does not matter. That is why the sentence is passive. · «The grain is taken to the factory.» Does the grain take itself anywhere? → no. So the grain cannot be the doer, and the verb has to be passive. · «Bread is sold in that shop» — how many words make the passive? → two: is and sold. Take one away and the sentence stops working.
  4. The restriction, said once and written up: Only verbs with an object have a passive. There is no «was died» and no «was come». Test it: eat · sleep · sell · arrive · make · come — which three?
  5. Ninety seconds at SB p.167: pairs find the past participles of cut, put, send, throw and sell. They will need all five in four minutes' time.
📺 On screen: the cereal text on the screen so the class can underline the same lines together (Backup: it is printed on SB p.108 — everyone underlines in the paper book and you write the eight two-word verbs on the board as they are called out)
👦 Students do: underline, then complete the rules. Expect the underlining to be almost complete and the rule about the agent to need the CCQs. Typical error: they underline are popular as a passive — worth two minutes, because it shows the difference between be + adjective and be + participle

Grammar · practice: how orange juice is made
solo → pairs
Why: One connected text rather than twelve separate sentences, because the real decision — active or passive — can only be made when there is a subject to look at.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.109: twelve gaps in one text, and two of them are not passive at all

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set it up with a question and a number: How many oranges make one litre of juice? (about ten). Then the board pair: Ten oranges ______ for one litre. (are needed) / You ______ ten oranges for one litre. (need).
  2. Warn them before they start, because it is the point of the task: Two of these twelve gaps are NOT passive. Find them.
  3. Solo, 6 minutes, silent mode. Key: 1 are pulled off · 2 are put · 3 are sent · 4 makes · 5 is washed and dried · 6 are thrown away · 7 is cut · 8 is removed · 9 heat · 10 is put · 11 take · 12 are sold.
  4. Kagan RallyCoach to check: the partner asks What is the subject of that sentence? before accepting any answer.
  5. Take the two active gaps last and make somebody explain them: gaps 4, 9 and 11 all have a doer that matters — the factory, the factories and the lorries.
  6. For your own reference: SB p.109 ex.2, «How orange juice is made»: the oranges are pulled off the trees and put into boxes, which are sent to a factory that makes juice. At the factory the fruit is washed and dried, bad oranges are thrown away, the fruit is cut in half and the juice is removed by a machine. Most factories then heat the juice to 94 °C, which makes it last six to eight months outside the fridge. The hot juice is put into cartons or bottles and left to cool, and lorries take the cartons to supermarkets, where they are sold.
📺 On screen: the gapped text on the screen, one paragraph at a time (Backup: it is printed on SB p.109 — work from the page, and keep the BE + 3rd FORM boxes in chalk on the board where everyone can see them)
👦 Students do: fill twelve gaps alone, then justify each one. Expect 8–10 out of 12. Typical errors: gap 5 (is washed and dried — they write be twice) and gap 9, where heat is made passive because everything else was

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.109: five active sentences rewritten, and the agent left out on purpose

Grammar · error hunt
teams
Why: Correcting other people's sentences is faster and safer than producing them, and the Showdown means every student answers every item instead of one student answering one.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 p.109: seven sentences where the choice between active and passive is the whole question

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Demonstrate the shape of the task first: My mum makes / is made great pizza. (makes) — Can pizza make anything? (no).
  2. Seven items, 4 minutes. Kagan Showdown: the team agrees, writes it on the mini-whiteboard, and on Showdown! every board goes up at once.
  3. Key: 1 is painted · 2 cost · 3 is called · 4 are called · 5 is broken · 6 includes · 7 is always cooked.
  4. For every item, one team says WHY — not what. The question is always the same: Can the subject do this by itself?
  5. Item 2 (cost) and item 6 (includes) are the two that matter: neither can be passive, and both look as though they should be.
📺 On screen: the seven sentences one at a time on the screen so the whole class decides the same one (Backup: write the seven sentences on the board before the lesson and uncover them one by one with a sheet of paper)
👦 Students do: choose as a team and reveal together. Expect 5 out of 7. Typical error: item 2 — These trousers are cost £15 — the exact mistake the test will ask about

WHAT WE DO: ex.5–6 p.109: invent a snack, draw it, describe how it is made, and let the class choose which one goes into production

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the frame: Invent a snack. A biscuit, an ice cream, a bar, anything. Draw it. Then write four sentences: what it is made of, how it is made, what it is covered or filled with, and where it is sold. 6 minutes.
  2. The standard, and it is the one from the lesson frame: At least three of your four sentences are passive. Two words each.
  3. Pairs present, 45 seconds each. The rest of the class is not passive audience: everybody is the manager of a food company (ex.6) and has to choose one snack to produce.
  4. Vote at the end with a show of hands and one sentence of justification from three students.
  5. Log errors silently on your correction sheet; do not correct aloud — the feedback slot is the start of the next lesson.
📺 On screen: the four required sentence types written on the board as a checklist — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed; the drawings are on paper
👦 Students do: invent, draw, describe and vote. Expect the drawings to take longer than they should and the language to be better than in any drill today. Typical error: the fourth sentence slides into the active (we sell it in shops) — which is not wrong, and worth pointing out as a real choice rather than a mistake

  • build the present simple passive with the right form of be
  • explain why a sentence does not name who does the action
  • choose between the active and the passive inside a text

Structure
Cereals are eaten everywhere. · The grain is taken to the factory. · Bad oranges are thrown away. · The juice is removed by a machine. · Most factories heat the juice to 94 °C.
Form
am / is / are + past participle. Negative: isn't / aren't + past participle. The agent, when it is worth naming, goes at the end with by. ⚠️ Only verbs that take an object have a passive (SB p.165).
Pron. watch
the weak are in the middle of a passive — /ə/, not /ɑː/: Cereals ə eaten everywhere. If the class stresses it, the sentence stops sounding like English.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Cereals are eaten everywhere.» Who eats them? → we are not told, and it does not matter. That is why the sentence is passive.
«The grain is taken to the factory.» Does the grain take itself anywhere? → no. So the grain cannot be the doer, and the verb has to be passive.
«Bread is sold in that shop» — how many words make the passive? → two: is and sold. Take one away and the sentence stops working.
«My house is painted red» against «My house paints red» — which is possible? → only the first. A house cannot paint anything.
«The accident was happened» — what is wrong? → happen has no object, so it has no passive.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
