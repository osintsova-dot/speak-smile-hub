🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use six weather phrasal verbs (blow away, burn down, clear up, come out, fall down, put out) in the right form, survive a six-question exam listening, and answer questions about past experiences with used to, the past simple, and the phrases that buy thinking time.
What is genuinely hard
Phrasal verbs are two words with one meaning and an unpredictable object position; in the past they also have to be irregular correctly (blew away, burnt/burned down, fell down). In the listening, the trap is that all three options are usually mentioned in the recording — only one answers the question that was asked.
Typical error to expect
«I didn't used to like pizza», «I was used to play football» — after didn't the form is USE to, and used to never takes be. The d disappears in speech, which is exactly why it survives in writing.
Answering the listening from memory of the words — Jake mentions helicopters, his parents and a search party, so all three options «appear». The question asks who was OUT LOOKING for him.
Why the lesson is built this way
Vocabulary first and short, because the listening is full of it. The listening is run the exam way and twice, with the questions read and the pictures described BEFORE the first play — prediction is what turns listening into searching. The debrief spends its time on why the wrong options are attractive, using the Teacher's Book's own line-by-line reasons. The speaking half is model-first: Mariann answers three questions about her childhood, the class harvests both her tenses and her time-buying phrases, and then everybody answers the same three questions in a rotation.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“A sixteen-year-old lost one ski, then the other, then the daylight. He survived the night in a snow cave he built from what he'd seen on television. Today: how to answer six exam questions about that — and how to talk about your own past without freezing.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, three minutes of repairing the class's own used-to errors, and the bridge into the skills lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 (EP): six verbs in context, then the past forms nailed down

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the six on the board as verb + particle, with an object each: put out a fire · burn down a building · blow away a hat · clear up (the weather) · come out (the sun) · fall down (a building).
  2. Meaning check by situation, not translation: The rain stopped and the sky went blue. What did the weather do?
  3. ex.1 individually, four minutes. Warn them about the form: two of the six need a past form, one needs a past participle shape after didn't use to.
  4. Check by nomination. Then the form drill in RallyCoach pairs: partner A says the infinitive, partner B says the past and a whole sentence. Six each way.
  5. Add all six to the wall in the VERB column with both forms.
📺 On screen: the six phrasal verbs on the board with objects attached — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: meet the verbs in situations, use them, then drill both forms in pairs

WHAT WE DO: ex.2: one sentence per verb, then the best six read aloud

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.2: write one true or invented sentence for each of the six verbs. Four minutes. The only rule: it has to be about a real place or a real person.
  2. Pairs read their sentences to each other and choose the best one from each pair — one sentence, six pairs, six sentences on the board.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer on the six phrasal verbs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three teams. One student from each team comes out, sees a phrasal verb card, and mimes it — no words, no drawing.
  2. The team must say the whole phrasal verb WITH an object to score: put out a fire, not put out.
  3. Six rounds, thirty seconds each. Rotate the mimer every round.
  4. Freeze and sit: Page twenty-seven. Now you're listening to somebody it actually happened to.
📺 On screen: six phrasal verb cards prepared in advance — nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone watches and calls out; the object rule keeps the language complete

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 (track 15): the model, the tenses, and the time-buying phrases

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: read the three questions, then play track 15. One question only: what verb forms does Mariann use to talk about her past?
  2. Check: used to and the past simple. Ask why both — used to for the repeated things, past simple for the single ones.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4: the three questions answered to four different partners

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two circles, inside facing outside (Kagan Inside-Outside Circle). Inside are examiners with the three questions from ex.1; outside answer.
  2. Ninety seconds per pair, then the outside circle moves one place. After three rounds, swap roles.
  3. Two standards, both on the board: at least one used to in every answer, and at least one time-buying phrase. The listener counts them and says the number at the end of the ninety seconds.
  4. Monitor with a pen: two excellent answers written word for word, three errors written without names.
  5. Sit. Read the two excellent answers aloud and name exactly what made them good — the tense choice, the detail, the phrase that bought the thinking time.
  6. Repair the three errors as a class in ninety seconds.
📺 On screen: the three questions and the six phrases on the board while they rotate — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: every student answers three questions four times and asks them four times. That is roughly six minutes of continuous speech each

Structure
They put out the fire before it spread. · My hat has blown away. · The shop burnt down last month. · The weather cleared up. · I used to build a snowman every winter. · Let me think… we used to stay with my grandparents.
Form
phrasal verb = verb + particle, one meaning; past forms are irregular where the verb is (blew away, fell down) · past simple and used to for past experiences · time-buying phrases (Well… · Let me think… · Do you mean…?).
Pron. watch
the irregular past forms are a listening problem before they are a writing problem: blew /bluː/ against blow /bləʊ/, rose /rəʊz/, shook /ʃʊk/ · and the extra syllable rule that decides half the past-tense marks: created /kriˈeɪtɪd/, needed, lifted, succeeded — three syllables, not two
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«They put out the fire.» Did they put it outside? → no. Two words, one meaning: they stopped it burning.
«The weather cleared up.» Is that better weather or worse? → better. The particle carries the meaning, not the verb.
In question 4 Jake mentions helicopters AND his parents. Does that make both options right? → no. Only one answers who was OUT LOOKING for him.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The phrasal verbs are learnt as single wordsAlways say and write both parts, and always with an object: put out a fire, burn down a shop. A bare put or clear is handed back.
Students answer the listening after the first play and stop listeningThe instruction before play one is explicit: First listening — pencil, and only where you are sure. Second listening — pen. Two colours make the second play matter.
The debrief becomes you explaining six answersIt is a pair task with a written outcome: for each question, write why ONE wrong option is attractive. The Teacher's Book gives you the reasons if the class dries up.
The rotation collapses into chattingIt is timed, signalled and moving: ninety seconds per pair, then the outside circle steps one place. The listener has a job — count how many time-buying phrases they hear.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
