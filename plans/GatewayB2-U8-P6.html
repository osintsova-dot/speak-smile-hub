🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Systematise everything in the unit — the passive across all tenses and modals, the two passives of two-object verbs, the reporting passive in both its shapes, thirty-nine disaster words and fourteen prepositional verbs — and then produce all of it in writing under time in the Student's Book unit test: 40 marks, six sections, every single item written out.
What is genuinely hard
The test is entirely productive. There is not one multiple-choice item on page 107, which means the three mechanical failures of the unit all cost marks here: the wrong participle, the missing auxiliary, and the fused reporting structure. On the vocabulary half, the failure is different — spelling. Casualty, debris, evacuate and torrential are all misspelled by half a class under time pressure.
Typical error to expect
«The bridge was build in 1890», «the houses were destroy» — the passive takes the third form, always. This is the commonest single error in tasks 19–25 and it is mechanical, not conceptual: the student knows the passive and writes the infinitive. Drill the three-part chant aloud — build, built, built · destroy, destroyed, destroyed · hit, hit, hit.
«It is said that people believe …» → «People say that …» is fine, but the reverse trips them: It is said that needs the that, and He is said to be needs the infinitive with to. Two structures, two shapes, and students mix them into He is said that he is.
Why the lesson is built this way
Two halves of forty-five minutes, exactly as the syllabus specifies. The first half is not revision by reading — the Grammar reference is used as a reference, in a task, not read aloud. The systematisation is done as a grid the students fill in themselves (fifteen minutes), then Showdown makes every student produce a passive simultaneously on a mini-whiteboard, then Quiz-Quiz-Trade does the vocabulary on their feet, and then there is a genuine doubt clinic — seven minutes where the class asks and you answer, which is the single most efficient revision activity there is because it is targeted by the people who need it. The second half is the test, in silence, thirty-five minutes, followed by five minutes of immediate self-marking.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
Second half of today is the unit test: forty marks, thirty-five minutes, and not one question where you can guess between four options. Everything is written out. That sounds worse than it is — every single line on that page has been done in this room, and in the first forty-five minutes I will tell you which lesson each part comes from. What you cannot do at ten past ten, ask me before eleven.

Why: Reading a Grammar reference aloud teaches nobody anything. Filling a grid from it, with a self-generated example in the last column, is retrieval plus production, and the vocabulary audit tells each student what to revise rather than telling them all the same thing.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.106 Grammar reference — used as a reference, in a task, not read aloud

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand out or draw a grid with three columns — STRUCTURE · HOW YOU FORM IT · ONE EXAMPLE OF YOUR OWN — and six rows: the passive · the passive with a modal · questions in the passive · verbs with two objects (person as subject) · it is said that … · he is known to have …
  2. Books open at page 106, but you are not reading it — you are using it. Ten minutes, in pairs. Fill in the grid. The example in column three must be YOUR sentence, not the book's.
  3. Circulate. The two rows that stall are always questions in the passive and the last row. Feed, do not fill in.
  4. Check row by row on the board, four minutes, taking the student examples rather than the book's. Where two pairs disagree, send them to the exact line of page 106 rather than adjudicating yourself.
  5. Then the vocabulary half of page 106, two minutes, and make it a task rather than a read: Three lists on the bottom of that page. Tick every word you could use in a sentence right now. Count your ticks. Circle the five you cannot. Those five are what the next stage is for.
📺 On screen: the six-row grid drawn on the board and completed with student examples. Backup: draw the grid by hand on the board and have them copy it — no handout is required.
👦 Students do: fill a six-row grid from the Grammar reference in pairs, with their own examples, then audit the three vocabulary lists and circle five gaps.

20–30′
Showdown · every student writes a passive at the same time
teams of four, mini-whiteboards
Why: Showdown is the only revision game with 100% simultaneous participation and no waiting: twelve items means twelve productions per student in ten minutes, and the team compare step gives instant peer correction.

WHAT WE DO: ten minutes of simultaneous production on the exact forms the test demands

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Showdown, and the reason it is the right game here is that it has no queue: everybody writes every item.
  2. Teams of four, one mini-whiteboard (or a sheet of paper) each. You read a prompt; everybody writes their answer face down; you say Showdown!; all four turn over at once and compare within the team; the team agrees one answer and holds it up.
  3. Twelve prompts, all in the shape of the test. Six transformations: A boat rescued the boy and girl. · A Japanese company is going to rebuild the towers. · A famous architect had designed the airport. · They gave me a camera for my birthday. · My sister lent me some money. · The government has offered him a job.
  4. Six reporting: People often claim that Paris is the most beautiful city. · They say Galileo invented the telescope. · People believe avalanches are caused by loud noises. · They think the eruption turned the skies orange. · People used to believe the Moon was made of cheese. · Yesterday they reported that a boy had started the fire.
  5. Point at the board after each item — the two shapes from lesson 3 are still the reference. Score by team if it helps, but the score is not the point; the twelve simultaneous productions are.
  6. Keep a note of which two prompts produced the most wrong boards. Those are the two you re-teach in the doubt clinic.
📺 On screen: the twelve prompts read aloud from your list, and the two structural shapes on the board. Backup: mini-whiteboards are ideal but ordinary paper works identically — the game does not depend on equipment.
👦 Students do: write every answer individually, reveal simultaneously, agree one team answer and hold it up.

30–38′
Quiz-Quiz-Trade · the vocabulary, on their feet
open floor
Why: A stirrer with a purpose immediately before a thirty-five-minute sitting test, and the compulsory spelling converts a recognition game into preparation for a written paper.

WHAT WE DO: eight minutes of movement covering the three word lists and the prepositional verbs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two cards each from a set of forty. Front: a definition or a gapped sentence (somebody who is injured or killed in an accident = ______ · Everyone complained ______ the government. · a ______ of wind). Back: the answer, spelled.
  2. Quiz-Quiz-Trade, six minutes, non-stop: quiz, be quizzed, coach if your partner is stuck, praise, trade cards, find a new partner.
  3. One rule added for today, and it matters because the test is written: When you answer, SPELL the word out loud. Every time.
  4. Sit them down and do a fast whole-class round of the eight items that caused most hesitation. Write those eight on the board and leave them there — they will still be there during the test, which is fine, because a word you can read off the board is a word you have to have understood to use.
  5. The four that always need it: casualty · debris · evacuate · torrential. Spell all four aloud, chorally, twice.
📺 On screen: the eight hesitation words written on the board. Backup: handwritten cards work as well as printed ones; if there are no cards at all, read the forty definitions aloud and take choral answers, though the movement is lost.
👦 Students do: quiz and be quizzed by six or seven partners, spelling every answer aloud.

Doubt clinic · seven minutes of their questions, not yours
whole class
Why: Revision aimed by the learner is worth three times revision aimed by the teacher, and the Numbered Heads priming removes the social cost of admitting you do not know something.

WHAT WE DO: the highest-value revision activity there is, because the class targets it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Say it plainly: Seven minutes. You ask, I answer. Anything from this unit. There is no such thing as a stupid question in the seven minutes before a test.
  2. If the room is silent for more than ten seconds — and with teenagers it will be — prime it with Numbered Heads Together: fours, sixty seconds, agree ONE question your group wants answered, then call a number. That converts individual embarrassment into a group task and it works every time.
  3. Answer the two items that produced the most wrong boards in Showdown whether or not anybody asks about them.
  4. Keep every answer to sixty seconds and write it, do not just say it. If a question needs longer than a minute, put it on the board as a worked example and move on.
  5. Close the clinic on time and say the sentence that sets up the test: Six sections, forty marks, thirty-five minutes. Read every instruction — two of them ask for two different things. Write clearly, and spell.
📺 On screen: every answer written on the board rather than only spoken. Backup: no equipment — board and voice.
👦 Students do: ask real questions, or agree one group question in fours and have it asked by number.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.107 — six sections, 40 marks, everything written out

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open at page 107 and nothing else on the desk. Say the three rules once: Thirty-five minutes. Silent. If you are stuck, leave it and come back — a blank costs the same as a wrong answer and less time.
  2. Grammar test, 20 marks. Exercise 1 (7 marks): seven sentences turned active↔passive across seven tenses and modals. Exercise 2 (7 marks): seven jumbled two-object passives put in order, then made active — two things per item; say so, because half the class does one and stops. Exercise 3 (6 marks): six sentences with a mistake in the reporting passive, found and rewritten.
  3. Vocabulary test, 20 marks. Exercise 1 (7): seven compound expressions completed. Exercise 2 (6): six definitions matched to words from page 106. Exercise 3 (7): seven verbs matched to prepositions.
  4. Visible timer. Announce the halfway point and the last five minutes, nothing else. Circulate but answer nothing except a genuinely unclear instruction.
  5. The two instructions worth clarifying if a hand goes up: exercise 2 of the grammar test wants both the passive and the active version, and exercise 3 of the grammar test says there may be more than one way to do this — one correct version is enough.
  6. For anyone who finishes early, on the board before you start: Finished? Go back to exercise 3 of the grammar test and write the SECOND possible version of any two sentences. Silent, and it is genuinely useful.
📺 On screen: a visible timer on the desk. Backup: if there is no timer, write the finishing time on the board and call the halfway point aloud. The test itself is printed in the students' own book — nothing to copy, nothing to project.
👦 Students do: work in silence for thirty-five minutes on six sections, leaving blanks rather than stalling.

WHAT WE DO: five minutes of immediate feedback, which is worth more than a week of waiting

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the answers; students mark their own paper in a different colour. Grammar first, then vocabulary. Do not slow down for discussion — the marking is four minutes.
  2. Full key: Grammar test · 1 (active → passive and back): 1 The boy and girl were rescued by a boat · 2 The damaged towers are going to be rebuilt by a Japanese company · 3 A famous architect had designed the airport · 4 The port won't have been destroyed by the waves · 5 The awards are being presented tonight by a survivor of the tragedy · 6 A report should have been written about the catastrophe · 7 A local politician might open the school. 2 (two objects): 2 I was given a camera for my birthday / They gave me a camera · 3 They are being shown a film about natural disasters · 4 Young children are often told stories by their parents · 5 I was lent some money by my sister · 6 We were taught mathematics by a new teacher · 7 He has been offered a job by the government. 3 (say/know/believe): 1 It is often claimed that Paris is one of the most beautiful cities in the world / Paris is often claimed to be … · 2 Galileo Galilei is said to have invented the telescope · 3 Yesterday a young boy was reported to have started the fire · 4 Avalanches are said to be caused by loud noises · 5 A long time ago the Moon was believed to be made of cheese · 6 The eruption of Krakatoa is thought to have turned the skies orange in 1883
  3. Structure
The boy and girl were rescued by a boat. · I was given a camera for my birthday. · It is often claimed that Paris is one of the most beautiful cities in the world. · Galileo is said to have invented the telescope.
Form
the whole unit in one place: be + past participle across seven tenses; modal + be + past participle; two-object verbs give two passives and English prefers the person as subject; It is said that + clause versus He is said to (have) + infinitive; compounds are fixed (flash flood, dust storm, landslide); fourteen verb + preposition pairs.
Pron. watch
the test is written, so today's pronunciation work is spelling: the four words that are misspelled under pressure are casualty (not casualities), debris (silent s, and not debree), evacuate (one c) and torrential (double r, -tial). Spell all four aloud chorally in the Quiz-Quiz-Trade stage and write them on the board before the test.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
before the test: Exercise 2 asks for how many things per item — one or two? Two: the passive AND the active.
the passive with a modal: «should have written» in the passive — where does «be» go? After «have». «should have been written».
two objects: «They offered him a job» — who is the person? — Him. So the passive starts with…? — He.
ICQ before the test: How many minutes? What do you do if you are stuck on item three? Do you write your name on it?

Assumptions: Students have done all six lessons of the unit and the Workbook pages for each. They will be secure on the simple passive, unstable on modal and perfect passives, and roughly half will still fuse the two reporting structures. Vocabulary recognition will be good and spelling will not.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The revision half turns into reading page 106 aloud.It cannot, if the grid is handed out first. The Grammar reference is a resource for a task, and the last column — your own example — is what makes it productive.
Showdown collapses because one strong student writes and the others watch.The whole point is that all four write face down BEFORE anyone reveals. Walk round during the first two items and check four boards per team; after that it runs itself.
Nobody asks anything in the doubt clinic.Prime it with Numbered Heads: fours agree one question in sixty seconds, then you call a number. Individual embarrassment disappears when the question belongs to a group.
Thirty-five minutes is not enough for a weaker group.Give forty and cut the marking to three minutes, or drop Vocabulary exercise 3 (7 marks) and mark out of 33. Do not cut the grammar half — that is what the unit was about.
Students want their marks compared.Totals are written by the student, collected by you, and never read out. The public information is the class profile — which section was hardest — and that is the information that changes the next three lessons.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
