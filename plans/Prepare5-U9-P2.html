🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use fairly, pretty, quite and reasonably to say that something is true BUT NOT ABSOLUTE, and write a 100-word article that answers three set questions and gives an opinion the reader can disagree with.
What is genuinely hard
Russian teenagers own exactly one intensifier — very — and use it for everything, so the four new adverbs feel like decoration rather than meaning. They are not: they WEAKEN the adjective, which is a different message. The second difficulty is mechanical and constantly wrong: quite goes before the article (quite a confident person) while the other three go after it (a pretty confident person).
Typical error to expect
«very good», «very interesting», «very nice» for everything — Russian teenagers own one intensifier and use it forty times an hour. Fairly, pretty, quite and reasonably say something is true BUT NOT ABSOLUTE, which is a different meaning, not a synonym.
«a quite confident person» — quite goes BEFORE the article: quite a confident person. Pretty, fairly and reasonably go after it: a pretty confident person. It is one line on the board and it saves a mark.
Why the lesson is built this way
The adverbs get eight minutes and a personality task, because that is all they need and because they then walk straight into the writing — an article about your own habits is unwritable without hedging. The writing half is run the way the examiner reads it: the model comes first and is analysed against the four assessment words (content, communicative achievement, organisation, language), the opinion phrases are practised on five ready-made topics before anyone faces the exam task, and the writing itself happens IN CLASS, in silence, with a word count, so that the homework version is a second draft rather than a first attempt. Peer feedback uses the same four words the examiner uses — the class marks with the real instrument.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“There is a difference between «I'm good at this» and «I'm pretty good at this», and English says it in one word. Today: four of those words — and then you write an article for a real magazine page, in a hundred words, with an opinion in it.”

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: what the adverbs actually mean, then a description built from them

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 as a class, and run it as three questions off the six example sentences on the page. Do the bold adverbs mean very? No. What do they mean? Quite, or a little — they make the adjective LESS strong. What comes after them? An adjective (1, 2, 3, 6) or an adverb (4, 5).
  2. Then the position rule, on the board, two lines, underlined: a pretty confident person against quite a confident person. This is the one that gets marked wrong for the next two years if it is not fixed today.
  3. Pairs, ninety seconds: ask and answer questions 1 and 2 from the page about yourselves — Do you keep your bedroom reasonably tidy? Are you fairly reliable as a person?
  4. ex.2 individually, three minutes: the five gaps in the self-description, combining an adverb with a word from the box. More than one adverb is possible and they should know that. Answers: 1–2 and 4–5 take pretty / fairly / reasonably, and item 3 is the idiom quite happily, which means willingly.
  5. Check by nomination, whole sentences read aloud, and add the four adverbs to the third column of the wall.
📺 On screen: the two position lines on the board, underlined and left up — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: answer two questions about themselves in pairs, then build a five-gap self-description alone

A5 glue-in SCALE OF DEGREE: adverbs from moderate to very strong — fairly (to a moderate degree, less than expected) · reasonably (acceptable or sensible) · quite (fairly, but not extremely with gradable adjectives; completely with extreme ones: quite impossible) · pretty (informal, like quite) · very / really (to a high degree) — each with an example, plus a PLACEMENT note (before adjectives and adverbs) and the MINI COMPARISON fairly easy < pretty easy < very easy
  • Glue the scale in and mark where each adverb sits on it.
  • Note the two lives of quite: fairly with normal adjectives, completely with extreme ones.
  • Rewrite three of your own sentences moving up and down the scale.
  • Pretty is informal — fine in speech, careful in writing.

Move break · very is banned
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that installs the ban physically

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. You read a flat statement with very in it — That film was very good. This exercise is very easy. My room is very tidy.
  2. The class repeats it, out loud, together, with one of the four new words instead — and steps forward if they think the new version is actually TRUE for them, back if it is not.
  3. Eight statements, eight repetitions, eight small movements. It takes four minutes and it kills «very» more effectively than any rule.
  4. Freeze and sit: Page one hundred and one. Somebody wrote an article. Your job is to work out which task they were answering.
📺 On screen: no technology in this stage — this stage is voice and feet only, and nothing can fail
👦 Students do: everybody speaks every round and everybody moves every round

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 = the exam task: about 100 words, all three points, an opinion in the first line

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the task: choose Task B (how you send messages, which app is best, how many a day) or Task C (posting photos, which sites, how many a day). Tell them you may assign one if the choice is taking longer than the writing would.
  2. Three numbered boxes on the board — the three questions of the chosen task. Five minutes of notes first, and nobody writes a sentence until all three boxes have something in them. Walk the room and check the boxes, not the English.
  3. 🎯 Fifteen minutes, silent, individually. Visible timer. Rules on the board: about 100 words · all three points · at least two phrases from the box · at least one adverb of degree · «very» costs a point.
  4. Announce the last three minutes out loud so nobody is caught: Three minutes. Now count your words and write the number at the bottom. The count is part of the task and it takes thirty seconds.
  5. You do not circulate correcting. You circulate reading first sentences, and you say one thing to anyone whose opening has no opinion in it: That is a fact. Where is you?
  6. Deal with anyone who finishes early: they check their own work against the four assessment words before they are allowed to look up.

Form

Pron. watch
apologise /əˈpɒlədʒaɪz/ — the stress is on the SECOND syllable and the class will put it on the first · disagree /ˌdɪsəˈɡriː/ carries its stress right at the end · in a reported question the reporting verb is stressed and the rest of the line flattens: she ASKED me where I LIVED · and quite is /kwaɪt/, one syllable, not the two the spelling suggests
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I'm pretty good at tennis.» Better or worse than «I'm very good»? → worse. These four words make the adjective SMALLER, not bigger.
«a quite confident person» — right? → no. Quite jumps in front of the article: quite a confident person. The other three stay behind it.
An article that starts «The internet is a global network» — is that an article? → no, that is a definition. An article starts with an opinion, because the reader has to want to argue.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Everything is «very»Ban the word for the second half of the lesson and say so out loud, with a smile. One point deducted per «very» during the writing stage, announced in advance. It works and it takes ten seconds to set up.
The article turns into a list of factsPoint at the frame: OPINION → REASON → OTHER PEOPLE → ME AGAIN. If the first sentence has no I in it and no opinion in it, it gets rewritten before anything else is written.
Only two of the three points get answeredThe three questions from the task go on the board as three numbered boxes. Before anyone writes a word, they write ONE note in each box. No notes, no writing — this is the single commonest way marks are lost in Writing Part 2.
Fast finishers in the opinion stageThe Teacher's Book extension: two more topics on the board — replying to messages instantly, and using your phone while walking down the street. Write an opinion on each, read them out at the end of the stage.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 38 (the opinion piece) and tasks 32–38 (lexical choice).

An article and the Russian opinion essay are not the same genre, but the machinery underneath is identical: a stated position, a reason, an acknowledged other view, and a closing line that does not repeat the opening. That is criterion К3 in Russian clothes. The adverbs of degree are the quieter half — fairly, pretty, quite and reasonably let a candidate say something is TRUE BUT NOT ABSOLUTE, which is exactly the hedging the essay rubric rewards and which Russian teenagers replace with a flat very.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
