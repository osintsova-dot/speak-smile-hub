🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Ask for and give personal details (first name, surname, age, address, email, phone) and spell them out clearly enough for a partner to write them down without a single mistake.
What is genuinely hard
They know the alphabet, but spelling at speed while somebody else is WRITING what you say is new. On top of that they read a phone number as one unbroken stream of digits, which nobody can write down.
Typical error to expect
How old have you? instead of How old are you? — translated straight from Russian [A2/B1]
becouse · tomorow · alot · wich · beatiful · intresting — the most frequent misspellings; a misspelt word scores nothing, in a class test and later in the exam
Why the lesson is built this way
The lesson is built backwards from the last stage: the full two-phase conversation at 72′ needs the form vocabulary, the alphabet and the repair phrase, so those three arrive in exactly that order and each one is used the minute it is met. Reading comes first because the school website supplies the form labels inside a real text, so vocabulary is met in context rather than as a list. The spelling duel sits immediately after the pronunciation stage on purpose: it turns an abstract letter drill into a consequence — one wrong letter and the partner's sheet is wrong, and no teacher has to say so. Listening is placed before the speaking stage because hearing names and numbers is easier than producing them, and it rehearses the same details one more time. Feedback in the productive stage is on two things only — being understood and answering in full — so it stays usable instead of turning into a list of everything that went wrong.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Today one letter changes everything. Not grammar — one letter. If you say 'i' instead of 'e', your partner writes the wrong name. So: the alphabet, an email address, a phone number — and then a real conversation where you have to be understood the first time.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a clinic on yesterday's three errors, and the bridge into the lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. A clinic on yesterday's correction sheet: three sentences on the board with no names attached — I'm playing football every week. · She don't talk much. · I'm usually wearing a jacket. Thirty seconds — fix all three on your board.
  3. Check them together; the student who put a hand up first explains why it has to be that way.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Yesterday you talked about yourself. Today you have to be UNDERSTOOD — letter by letter.
📺 On screen: the three sentences written on the board before the lesson — the board is the medium, no backup needed
👦 Students do: correct them on mini-whiteboards. Expect: everybody fixes the first and the third, about two thirds fix the second (doesn't). Typical error: they fix the tense but forget the -s

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson: the form, the letters, being understood first time

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two lines on the board — the whole lesson is built this way: Phase 1 — the form. Phase 2 — the conversation.
  2. Say it honestly: Phase 1 — the form: name, surname, age, first language. ⚠️ Address, email and phone are asked only as spelling practice — nobody asks for them in a real conversation.
  3. What counts as failure: Where it goes wrong: a one-word answer («Fifteen.»), silence instead of asking for repetition, and a long prompt answered with one sentence instead of three or four.
  4. Set the bar: Your partner must write your email address correctly. If he can't — it's on you, not on him.
  5. Say the point of the stage out loud: today it isn't the teacher who checks you, it's your partner's sheet of paper — either it says the right thing or it doesn't.
📺 On screen: a slide reading «Phase 1 / Phase 2» (Backup: the same two lines in chalk on the board — they stay up all lesson)
👦 Students do: listen; one student puts the standard into their own words. Expect surprise that the address and the phone number are only there as letter practice. Typical problem — they relax: bring them back to “your partner writes down what he hears”

Why: A second, factual text on the same topic: the same vocabulary comes back in a different genre, which is what makes it stick.

WHAT WE DO: the school's Award website, p.12: first “who is this for”, then seven right/wrong statements

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed: What information could there be on a website for this award? Collect ideas for one minute (technique from TB p.21).
  2. Books open, ex.1 against the clock: One minute. Who is this page for? Key: It is for students who want to do The Duke of Edinburgh's Award.
  3. The provocation from TB p.21: There are only four skills you can do — true or false? (false — the site only gives examples).
  4. ex.2, seven statements, 5 minutes solo. Kagan RallyCoach to check in pairs: a wrong statement must not simply be ticked as wrong, it has to be put right.
  5. Key (TB p.21): 1 ✓ · 2 ✗ · 3 ✓ · 4 ✓ · 5 ✓ · 6 ✓ · 7 ✗
📺 On screen: the website page on the screen, with the evidence lines underlined in marker (Backup: everyone works from the book and you keep the numbers of the true/false items on the board)
👦 Students do: read and decide, then repair the false ones in pairs. Expect 2 and 7 to catch some of them out. Typical error: they put ✗ at random without finding the line — hold them to the “finger on the line” rule

32–40′
Pronunciation · the alphabet
class → pairs
Why: Spelling out loud is a separate skill from knowing the alphabet. Six letters cause almost every mistake, so those six get the drilling.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4 p.13: the look-alike letters and the groups where one sound is the odd one out (track 06)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write B F T on the board: Which letter is different? (F — it has /e/, not /iː/) — technique from TB p.22.
  2. ex.4: six groups, find the odd letter in each. Track 06 to check. Key: 1 U /juː/ (not /e/) · 2 M /em/ (not /iː/) · 3 Y /waɪ/ (not /e/) · 4 K /keɪ/ (not /iː/) · 5 X /eks/ (not /juː/) · 6 O /əʊ/ (not /iː/)
  3. Drill the three trap pairs chorally on their own: E–I, G–J, A–R.
  4. A quick duel: I spell — you write. Ready? W-Y-E-J-A-R. Compare the mini-whiteboards.
📺 On screen: the letters and the ex.4 groups large on the screen (Backup: write B F T and the six groups in chalk on the board and use your own voice instead of the recording)
👦 Students do: hunt for the odd letter, then take a letter dictation. Expect nearly everyone to confuse E and I — that is exactly what the stage is for. Typical error: they write G for J
SB p.13 ex.3–4 · track 06 · mini-whiteboards
40–45′
Spelling duel
pairs
Why: Instant proof that letters matter: one wrong letter and the partner writes the wrong thing. Nobody has to be told to concentrate after that.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5 p.13: act out Sam's and Jo's details — one asks, the other dictates, the first writes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pre-teach the repair phrase: Can you repeat that, please? — they will need it in the exam too.
  2. One student in the pair is Sam; the other asks the questions and WRITES the answers down without looking at the book. Then they swap roles using Jo's details.
  3. Check: compare what was written with the book. One wrong letter — one lost point.
  4. Extension (TB p.22): invent a celebrity's contact details and swap them around the class.
📺 On screen: the phrase Can you repeat that, please? on the board — the board is the medium, no backup needed; Sam's and Jo's details are open in the book on p.13
👦 Students do: dictate and write, then compare. Expect half the pairs to make one mistake each — that is precisely what you then discuss. Typical error: they dictate the email address as one block, with no dot and no at

60–72′
Talking points
fours → circles
Why: A short opinion exchange so the lesson isn't only mechanics: they use the unit's language to say something they actually think.

WHAT WE DO: the discussion on p.12: which parts of the award are the most useful, which look the most fun, and what awards like this are for

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three prompts on the board: I think … is the most useful because … · For me, … looks the most fun. · Awards like this help you to …
  2. Kagan Inside-Outside Circle: two circles facing each other, one question per round — 40 seconds, then the outer circle steps one place to the right and everyone has a new partner.
  3. Three rounds, three questions. On the fourth move ask them to report not their own opinion but their partner's: What did your partner say?
  4. Write the good things you overhear on the board — and the errors too, but with no names attached (technique from TB p.21).
📺 On screen: the three prompts written on the board — the board is the medium, no backup needed
👦 Students do: talk round the circles, then report a partner's opinion. Expect speech to be noticeably freer by the third round. Typical error: I think is useful with no subject

pairs
Why: The full version of the conversation they started last lesson: details plus two topics, with a listener whose job is to notice whether the answer was really an answer.

WHAT WE DO: the full round of both phases from SB p.13: each other's form details plus questions on two topics

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Phase 1: In pairs, ask and answer to complete the form. Spell your surnames. 4 minutes, then compare what was written down — did the partner hear it right?
  2. Phase 2: the two question sets from the book — School and Free time. Kagan RallyCoach: one student asks all the questions first, then they swap on the bell.
  3. The long prompt at the end, for everyone: Now, please tell me something about your school. Hold it for 20–30 seconds with no help.
  4. Give feedback on two things only, aloud and with no names: how clearly they were understood and how full the answers were.
  5. Bridge to home: more sample questions and a walk-through — SB p.133.
📺 On screen: the two question sets (School / Free time) on the screen (Backup: the questions are open in the book on p.13 and you time it on the wall clock)
👦 Students do: run both phases in pairs. Expect phase 1 to move briskly and the first 5 seconds of the long prompt to be silent — that is normal on a first run. Typical error: they answer Tell me something about… with a single word

  • ask seven questions about personal details and answer them
  • spell a name, an email address and a phone number so a partner writes it correctly

Structure
What's your surname? — It's Hopkins, H-O-P-K-I-N-S. · What's your email address? · What numbers can we call you on? · Can you repeat that, please?
Form
questions with What's your …? and How old are you?; the answer comes in a full form, not as a single word.
Pron. watch
look-alike letters: E /iː/ – I /aɪ/ · G /dʒiː/ – J /dʒeɪ/ · A /eɪ/ – R /ɑː/. a dot in an address is read as dot /dɒt/, the @ sign as at /æt/; a zero in a phone number is usually said as oh /əʊ/; break a phone number into groups of digits with a pause — easier to say, to hear and to remember (TB p.21).
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I'll just say 'my surname is Petrov'» — is that enough? → no, not if the partner is writing it down: you have to spell it out, P-E-T-R-O-V.
How do you read 020 7946 0945? → in groups, with a pause between them, and the zero as oh /əʊ/ (TB p.21).
You didn't catch the question — what do you do? → Can you repeat that, please? Silence is not an answer.

Assumptions: SB p.12–13, syllabus lesson 5. Students know the alphabet, but spelling at speed while a partner writes it down is new. Wh-questions are familiar; what is new is the intonation — falling at the end (TB p.21: wh-questions take a falling tone).

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
“The alphabet is for little kids”Kill it with the format, not with an argument: we don't sing the alphabet, we dictate a real email address so that the partner writes it down correctly. The check is the comparison of the two sheets, not the teacher's opinion.
They read a phone number as one stream of digitsThe rule of three groups: 020 / 7946 / 0945, with a breath between the groups. Beat the rhythm out with your hand.
In pair speaking the stronger student takes all the air timeFix the roles hard: first one student asks every question, then they swap on the timer bell. Kagan RallyCoach keeps the turns.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
