🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name nine natural features and work out which verbs never go into the continuous — and be able to say why, instead of just learning a list.
What is genuinely hard
Intuitively they already speak this way, but the moment the word «now» appears in a sentence the hand reaches for ‑ing by itself. On top of that come the two th sounds that Russian does not have at all, and the word desert, which everybody reads as dessert.
Typical error to expect
I'm needing help · She is understanding the question · Are you knowing your neighbours? — stative verbs put into the continuous (TB p.26 names this as the main error of the unit).
Sun is bright instead of The sun; a second mention also takes the [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The reading comes before the grammar on purpose: the article on p.14 is where the stative verbs actually live, so students underline them inside a real text and discover for themselves that every single one is in the present simple — a list handed to them would be gone by Friday. The nine geography words sit between the reading and the grammar because they arrive inside that same article anyway; the photo-matching stage only makes the meaning explicit and the mingle gives the repetitions. Pronunciation is deliberately small and placed straight after the movement break — two sounds taught by feel in ninety seconds, then back into use before attention drops. The move break itself is bait: somebody always produces I'm preferring, and that mistake is the perfect door into the grammar block. The productive outcome — five lines about their own country — sits at 80′, after the error hunt and the class survey, because personalised writing only pays off once the form has been produced correctly at least twice under supervision.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Some verbs never wear -ing. Not because a rule says so — because they are not actions. You don't DO 'know'. You don't DO 'belong'. Today you learn which ones — and nine words for the planet you live on.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a spelling blitz on last lesson's letters, and the bridge into the unit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. A spelling blitz: I spell a surname, you write it. H-O-P-K-I-N-S. … T-O-W-N-S-E-N-D. The two surnames from last lesson.
  3. Check the mini-whiteboards: Hands up if both are perfect.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Enough about people. Today the topic is bigger: the planet — and one grammar trap that Russian speakers fall into every single time.
📺 On screen: the two surnames go up on the board after the dictation — the board and the mini-whiteboards are the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write from the spelt-out letters. Expect: part of the class loses the -d in Townsend. Typical error: E and I mixed up in Hopkins

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language trap of the lesson — straight away on a live example

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Show the trap live, two options on the board: I ___ this song. (A am liking / B like) Which one looks nice? Which one is right?
  2. Explain it in 40 seconds: there are verbs that never take ‑ing. Not because «a rule forbids it», but because they are not actions: to know, to belong, to want.
  3. Set the bar: By the end you can name at least six verbs that never take -ing.
  4. The other half of the aim — nine words about geography: today they go straight into five sentences about your own country.
📺 On screen: the trap example and the lesson bar in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here and it stays up to the end of the lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: choose an option and argue. Expect: part of the class reaches for am liking — that is exactly what you want. Typical problem: they believe the continuous is «more polite» or «more modern»

Why: Recycles last lesson's alphabet in a game, so the review costs no lesson time and starts the lesson with everyone talking.

WHAT WE DO: battleships played by letter names with last unit's words (TB p.25)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Explain the rules in one minute: two 8×8 grids, letters A–H along the bottom, I–P up the side. In the first grid they write six words from Unit 1, one letter per square — horizontally, vertically or diagonally. The second grid stays empty, for the hits.
  2. Player A: B slash P? · Player B: No, my turn. D slash J? · Player A: Yes, the letter K.
  3. 5 minutes of play. The winner is the first to find all six of the partner's words.
  4. Say the point of the stage out loud: last lesson's alphabet goes on living instead of being forgotten.
📺 On screen: a sample pair of grids and the three model lines on the board — the board and their notebooks are the medium, no technology and no backup needed
👦 Students do: draw the grids and play. Expect 3–4 words found in 5 minutes. Typical error: they call the letter with its Russian sound — fix it inside the game, not with a remark

16–26′
Reading · the Earth
pairs → solo
Why: Gist reading on a topic they have opinions about, and the source of the nine geography words — the words arrive inside a text, not as a list.

WHAT WE DO: the article on p.14: two questions first, then a heading for each of the four paragraphs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Before reading, 1 minute in pairs: How old is the Earth? What's happening to the weather at the moment? Write the hypotheses down.
  2. Read and check. Key (TB p.25): 1 The Earth is 4.6 billion years old. · 2 The weather is changing faster than usual: some places are getting wetter, others drier, and there are more big storms.
  3. ex.2 — four headings for four paragraphs. Strategy prompt (TB p.25): Look at heading A. What information are you looking for? Something positive in one place.
  4. Key: 1 C · 2 D · 3 B · 4 A. Every answer is confirmed with a line from the text.
  5. Extra: find Mount Everest, the Amazon, the Baltic Sea, Tonga and Greenland on the map.
📺 On screen: the article on the screen so an evidence line can be underlined for everybody at once (Backup: read from the paper book and keep the paragraph numbers and heading letters on the board; for the map use a paper atlas or a world sketch drawn by hand)
👦 Students do: read, place the headings, point at the line. Expect paragraph 4 (Greenland) to be right for almost everyone. Typical error: B and D are mixed up — separate them on the word problems

Vocabulary · geography
class → pairs
Why: Picture-to-word matching gives meaning instantly; the mingle that follows gives repetition.

WHAT WE DO: nine geography words: photos A–I (ex.3, track 10) plus Quiz-Quiz-Trade

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed first: People climb these. (mountains) — give three definitions and let them guess (technique from TB p.25).
  2. ex.3: match the blue words from the article to photos A–I. Track 10 — check and choral repetition. Key: A desert · B hills · C volcano · D sea · E lake · F river · G mountains · H forest · I valley

  4. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with photo ↔ word cards, 4 minutes, at least five partners each.
📺 On screen: photos A–I large on the screen and track 10 from the whiteboard app (Backup: the same photos in the paper book on p.14, the nine words listed on the board and read aloud by you twice each; the Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards are printed in advance and need nothing at all)
👦 Students do: match the photos, then mingle with cards. Expect hill and mountain to be confused. Typical error: dessert in the spelling — catch it on the spot

46–54′
Pronunciation · th
class → pairs
Why: Two sounds that Russian doesn't have, taught by feel (voice on / voice off) rather than by explanation. Ninety seconds, then straight back into use.

WHAT WE DO: the two th sounds: ex.6 on p.15 (track 12), sorted into the earth / weather columns

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.26: the only difference between /θ/ and /ð/ is voice. Ask students to cover their ears with their hands and hear the buzz on /ð/. Minimal pair to show it: breath /θ/ — breathe /ð/.
  2. Show the pair: breath — breathe. Students put their palms over their ears and hear the difference.
  3. ex.6: sort the nine words from the box into two columns. Track 12 — check and repeat. Key: /θ/ earth: north, south, thing, think · /ð/ weather: other, that, there, these, this
  4. Tongue twister to fix it: These three things are the thing. Three runs: slow, medium, fast.
📺 On screen: the θ earth | ð weather table on the screen (Backup: the same two-column table in chalk on the board, and you replace the sound with your own voice — say each word twice)
👦 Students do: sort the words, repeat chorally and individually. Expect /ð/ to come more easily. Typical error: think comes out as sink — ask to see the tongue between the teeth

54–70′
Grammar · stative verbs
solo → pairs → class
Why: The rule is short but counter-intuitive after a lesson on the continuous. Guided discovery from the text, then concept questions to close the obvious traps.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 on p.15: underline the verbs in the text, derive the rule, practise on six sentences

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1: Find these verbs in the article on page 14 and underline them. What tense are they in? Key: present simple — every one of them.
  2. Formulate the rule together: these verbs are about thoughts, feelings and owning — we don't normally put them in the continuous;
  3. ex.2: which other words from the box are about thoughts, feelings and owning. Key (TB p.26): belong to, hate, have, love, prefer, need, want
  4. Give the two exceptions at once (TB p.26): have a shower is fine; What are you thinking about? is a process, so that is fine too.
  5. ex.3, six sentences, 5 minutes solo. Kagan RallyCoach — check in pairs with a «why» for every answer. Key: 1 hate · 2 wants · 3 are you singing, need · 4 belongs · 5 love · 6 am running
  6. Point at items 3 and 6: those are continuous — the proof that the rule is not a blanket ban on -ing.
📺 On screen: the verb list and the rules table on the screen (Backup: write the two boxes on the board — believe/hope/know/like/own/think/understand and belong to/hate/have/love/prefer/need/want)
👦 Students do: underline in the text, derive the rule, do ex.3. Expect them to formulate the rule almost word for word themselves. Typical error: in item 3 they are afraid to put are you singing — show that sing is an action

70–80′
Grammar · error hunt
teams → class
Why: Correcting other people's sentences is faster and safer than producing them; the class survey afterwards puts the same forms into their own mouths.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 (five sentences with a mistake) plus ex.5 (Do you… / Are you… questions and a class survey)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4 — Kagan Showdown, 4 minutes. Key (TB p.26): There are things in my bag that I need at the moment. · The weather's cold at the moment, but I prefer it like that. · I think the party was great. · My brother likes summer and I like winter. · Get into the pool with me! The water feels great.
  2. ex.5: build eight questions from the prompts. Key: Do you own a bike? · Are you learning to play an instrument? · Do you like cabbage? · Are you doing an art project at the moment? and so on.
  3. Kagan Find Someone Who: they walk round the class looking for someone who answers yes to each question, and write the name down. One name per question — you cannot use the same person twice! 5 minutes.
  4. Collect it in with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Who owns a bike? Number ones — report!
📺 On screen: the eight ex.5 prompts on the screen (Backup: the prompts stay open in the book on p.15 and the students rule a «Find Someone Who» grid in their notebooks in 30 seconds)
👦 Students do: correct the mistakes, build questions and survey the class. Expect the movement to lift the pace. Typical error: Do you learning? — catch it on the spot, that is the whole point

WHAT WE DO: ex.7–8 on p.15: five sentences about the geography of your own country, then listening in fours

WB Unit 2 (p.8–11)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can name nine geographical features and decide when a verb cannot take the continuous form, explaining why.
  • Subsidiary. Reading an article and choosing a heading for each paragraph (SB p.14 ex.2); the two th sounds /θ/ and /ð/; five sentences of their own about the geography of their country.
  • Personal. Do not hand the list of verbs over ready-made — students underline them in the text first and see for themselves that every one of them stands in the present simple.
By the end, students can…
  • name nine geographical features and match them to photos
  • choose the best heading for a paragraph
  • say which verbs don't usually take -ing and why
  • produce /θ/ and /ð/ in earth and weather

Structure

Form
stative verbs (know, believe, own, belong to, need, want, prefer, hate, love, understand, and think in the sense of an opinion) — simple forms only.
Pron. watch
/θ/ voice off: north, south, thing, think · /ð/ voice on: other, that, there, these, this. Words of the unit: valley /ˈvæli/ · desert /ˈdezət/ (stress on the first syllable!) · volcano /vɒlˈkeɪnəʊ/.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I'm needing help» — is that OK? → no: need is a stative verb, I need help.
«She is understanding the question» — correct it. → She understands the question (TB Cooler).
«I'm having a shower» — why is this one fine? → it isn't «own», it's an action.

Assumptions: SB p.14–15, syllabus lesson 6. Some of the words are already familiar (mountain, river, forest, sea); the new ones are usually desert, valley and hill. The idea of «stative verbs» is formally new to most of them, although intuitively they already speak that way.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
desert read as dessertTake it apart on the board: desert /ˈdezət/ — a dry place, one s, stress on the first syllable; dessert /dɪˈzɜːt/ — the sweet course, two s. Mnemonic: «you want dessert twice, so it gets two s».
th produced as /z/ or /s/The TB p.26 technique: palms over the ears — on /ð/ you hear the buzz, on /θ/ it is silent. Minimal pair for contrast: breath — breathe.
The stative rule is heard as «-ing is never allowed»Give the exception from TB p.26 immediately: have a shower / have lunch are fine; think about is a process, so that is fine too.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
