🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use defining relative clauses (who, that, which, where) to say WHICH person, thing or place is meant, and non-defining relative clauses (who, which, whose, between two commas) to add information that could be deleted — and hear the difference in meaning between the two.
What is genuinely hard
Russian has ONE pronoun, который, for people and things alike, and it always takes a comma. English splits it three ways and makes the comma carry meaning: «The recipes which teenagers created will be posted» means only some of them; «The recipes, which teenagers created, will be posted» means all of them. Two commas change the number of recipes. That is the hardest single idea in this block, and it is worth the time.
Typical error to expect
«the girl which I met», «a man which works here» — who or that for people, which or that for things. Russian has one pronoun (который) for both, so the choice has to be made consciously every time.
«My sister, that lives in Moscow, is a doctor» — that is impossible in a non-defining clause, and the pronoun there can never be dropped. The exam tests exactly this line.
«Pico's Café which is near the football ground is closing» — a non-defining clause lives between two commas, and in the Russian written tasks a missing comma is a punctuation error and therefore a lost accuracy mark.
Why the lesson is built this way
The examples are not new: every one of the five short texts read last lesson contained a relative clause, and the class was told to go and look for them. So the lesson opens by harvesting yesterday's texts, which makes the grammar something they have already understood rather than something being introduced. The defining rule comes first and fast; the non-defining one is taught with the VOICE — read on a lower pitch, and the commas explain themselves. Then the meaning contrast (ex.3 and the four pairs in the grammar reference) is given the biggest slice of the lesson, because form without that contrast is just punctuation. The productive stage is a class cookbook page, where non-defining clauses are the natural way to introduce a person.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two identical sentences. One says some of the recipes. The other says all of them. The only difference is two commas. Today: the grammar that was hiding in every text you read last lesson.”

WHAT WE DO: ex.1: the rule derived from five examples, then extended by the class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1, first item together: which pronouns define a PERSON? Then individually, three minutes, for the rest.
  2. Check: defining relative clauses: a who, that (people) · b which, that (objects and things) · c where (places). For each of the five example sentences ask what is being defined — a person, a thing or a place — before confirming the pronoun.
  3. Then the extension that proves it is learned: every student writes ONE sentence of their own with a defining clause about somebody in this room, without naming them. Two minutes.
  4. Read four aloud and let the class guess who: She's the person who always finishes first.
  5. One extra rule from the reference page, said once and written up because the exam tests it: the pronoun may be DROPPED when it is the object (the cakes my brother makes) and never when it is the subject (the woman WHO runs the café).
📺 On screen: the pronoun table and the drop rule on the board — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: complete the rule alone, then write and read a defining sentence about a classmate

Grammar · non-defining clauses, taught with the voice
class → pairs
Why: Grammar · non-defining clauses, taught with the voice

WHAT WE DO: ex.2: three examples read aloud with a pitch drop, then the rule completed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read example 1 aloud twice — once flat, once with the middle part on a LOWER pitch — and ask which one sounds like English. That is the comma, made audible.
  2. In pairs: underline what the pronoun refers to in each of the three examples (Ella, Pico's Café, Jim), then complete the rule.
  3. Check: non-defining relative clauses: a which (things) · b who (people) · c whose (belonging to). Now add the two prohibitions to the board, beside the THAT line: the pronoun can NEVER be dropped, and there are always TWO commas, not one.
  4. Drill the voice, thirty seconds, whole class, three sentences: My mum, who hates cooking, made a cake. · This café, which opened last month, is already full. Lower pitch in the middle, both times.
  5. Ask the question that stops the two structures merging: If I delete the middle part, does the sentence still tell you WHO I mean? If yes — commas. If no — none.
📺 On screen: the two prohibitions written under the THAT line — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: hear the pitch drop, complete the rule in pairs, then produce three sentences with the intonation

Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer where the pronouns are people

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Five students get a big card each: WHO · WHICH · THAT · WHOSE · WHERE. Two more get a card with a COMMA.
  2. You read a sentence with a gap and the room has to send the right card-holder to the front — and decide whether the two comma-holders go with them.
  3. My sister ___ lives in Kazan is a chef. · That's the shop ___ I bought the pan. · Anna ___ won the competition is only fifteen.
  4. Everyone who is not holding a card votes with a thumb before the cards move — so the whole room answers, not the seven with props.
  5. Rotate the cards twice so different students hold them. Then freeze and sit: Exercise three. Two sentences that look the same and are not.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 + the four pairs in the grammar reference: what the commas actually do

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 as a whole class, and read sentence 1 with the pitch drop so the parenthesis is audible. Which is defining, which is non-defining, and what does each one MEAN?
  2. Answer: 1 b — non-defining, so ALL the recipes are by teenagers and all of them will be posted · 2 a — defining, so only the teenagers' recipes will be posted. Say it in numbers, because that is what makes it stick: with commas, one hundred per cent of the recipes are teenagers'. Without commas, only some of them.
  3. Now p.160, Practice ex.4 — four pairs, and this is the gold of the lesson. Run it as a physical quiz: you read a sentence, hands UP for «all of them», hands DOWN for «only some».
  4. The biscuits which my brother made were eaten. · The biscuits, which my brother made, were eaten. · The lettuces which weren't fresh were thrown away. · The lettuces, which weren't fresh, were thrown away.
  5. After each pair, one student says the difference in a full sentence. Nobody moves on until the room's hands agree.
  6. Full answer key for the reference page: Teacher's Book p.267.

Why: 🎯 Writing and speaking out · the class cookbook page

WHAT WE DO: a task where non-defining clauses are the natural way to introduce a person

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set it in one line: We are making one page of a class cookbook. Every dish needs a cook, and every cook needs a sentence.
  2. Each student writes three sentences, six minutes: one about the dish (defining — the dish that my grandmother makes at New Year), one about the cook (non-defining — my grandmother, who has cooked it for forty years, …), and one about the place (where).
  3. Kagan Fan-N-Pick in fours, five minutes: sentences face down, one student fans, one picks and reads it aloud, the third says whether the punctuation is right and why, the fourth praises or corrects. Roles rotate every card.
  4. Groups choose their best sentence, write it on a strip of paper and pin it up. Four minutes.
  5. Gallery walk, three minutes, pencil in hand: tick every strip with correct punctuation, question-mark any that is missing a comma or has THAT after one.
  6. Two minutes at the board repairing the question-marked ones — that is where the lesson lands.
  7. Close with Kagan RallyRobin, 60 seconds: Say one sentence about somebody in your family who cooks.
📺 On screen: the strips of paper on the wall with ticks and question marks — paper and pencil only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: write three sentences alone, audit four in a rotating group, then audit the whole class's work with a pencil

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can build both kinds of relative clause with the right pronoun and the right punctuation, and can say what the difference in meaning is between a defining and a non-defining version of the same sentence.
  • Subsidiary. Recycling the cooking verbs inside a new structure (the chef who barbecues everything); the fall in pitch that marks parenthetical information; reading a grammar reference page to settle a dispute.
  • Personal. One board line survives the whole lesson: THAT is impossible after a comma. It is the single most tested line of this grammar and the one that will be broken most often.
By the end, students can…
  • say the man who we interviewed and the place where I bought it
  • add extra information between two commas without dropping the pronoun
  • explain what changes when the commas are removed
  • correct which for people and that after a comma

Structure
He's the man who we interviewed last month. · That's the restaurant where the famous chef works. · Ella, who is in my tennis club, has won a competition. · Jim, whose sister is in my class, is having a barbecue.
Form
defining: no commas, and who/which/that may be DROPPED when it is the object (the cakes my brother makes) but never when it is the subject · non-defining: two commas always, the pronoun can never be dropped, and THAT is impossible.
Pron. watch
the cooking verbs are short and the class will swallow the endings: roast /rəʊst/ against roasted /ˈrəʊstɪd/ — the -ed is a whole syllable after t and d, and only there · barbecue /ˈbɑːbɪkjuː/ has three syllables and the stress on the first · whose and who's sound identical, which is why the spelling has to be seen on the board · and the non-defining clause is heard before it is punctuated: read it on a LOWER pitch and the commas explain themselves
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«The students who hadn't had lunch went to a café.» Did all the students go? → no, only the hungry ones. Now put in the commas: «The students, who hadn't had lunch, went» — how many went now? → all of them.
«My brother, that is a baby, cries a lot» — possible? → no. After a comma, that is impossible: who.
«The cakes my brother makes are good» — is anything missing? → which or that, and it is allowed to be missing, because it is the object. «The woman runs the café is friendly» — is that allowed? → no: who is the subject and cannot go.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
which for people, that after a commaOne board line, up all lesson: THAT is impossible after a comma. And a two-second oral fix for the other: every time which is used for a person, you say only the word «person?» and wait.
The commas are treated as decorationThey are worth marks and worth meaning, so they are read aloud: the non-defining clause is said on a LOWER pitch and the class hears the brackets. Every rewrite is checked for two commas, not one.
The meaning contrast is nodded at and not understoodUse the four pairs in the grammar reference practice (ex.4) as a mini-quiz with a physical answer — hands up for ALL, hands down for SOME. Nobody can hide, and you see who has it.
Fast finishers race ahead and get boredThe Teacher's Book task: write two facts about a person, place or thing on a slip, swap with somebody, and build ONE sentence with a non-defining clause out of their two facts.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 11 (inserting the missing parts of a text) — six gaps, and in almost every paper at least half of the missing parts are relative clauses.

This is the most under-taught task in the Russian paper and the one today's grammar answers directly: the candidate has to see that a gap needs a clause that describes the noun in front of it, and to match it by pronoun and by comma. Non-defining clauses also buy marks in the written tasks — one relative clause per paragraph raises the K3 mark for range without any risk.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
