🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Learn and apply the four steps of a good presentation (organise, use visuals, rehearse, connect with the audience) with the language of giving examples, and run the two Cambridge speaking tasks that the block has been building towards — Part 2 (describing a photograph alone) and Part 3 (discussing options with a partner).
What is genuinely hard
Presenting is a language problem disguised as a confidence problem: students who freeze in front of a class are usually students with no phrases for the joins — for example, such as, for instance, overall. The exam parts are hard in opposite directions: Part 2 is one person talking for a minute with nobody to help, and Part 3 is two people who have to disagree politely and then agree — which teenagers avoid because agreeing at once is faster and less awkward.
Typical error to expect
In presentations: reading a written text aloud, and putting everything on the visual. In Part 2: stopping at twenty seconds because everything certain has been said. In Part 3: Sushi? — OK. Two turns, almost no marks.
Why the lesson is built this way
The Life skills spread is not decoration and it is not a filler at the end of the year: it belongs to this block by the book, its four steps are the structure of the exam's long turn, and its Useful language box (giving examples) is exactly what fills the middle of a one-minute description. So the lesson runs it FIRST and then cashes it in twice — Part 2 with a photograph, Part 3 with a decision. Both exam parts are run in threes with the printed examiner script, because reading the script aloud is what teaches the format faster than any explanation. The lesson — and the block — ends with a real mini-presentation, which is the only way the four steps become something they own.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Five steps, and the fourth one is the one nobody does: watch the people you are talking to. Today you learn how to be listened to — and then you use it twice, in the exam's own two speaking tasks, with one of you holding the script and the clock.”

WHAT WE DO: the article read for its four steps, then argued about

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the reading task before they open the page: Four steps. Find them, and find the ONE sentence in each that you actually agree with.
  2. Five minutes, silent reading.
  3. Take the four steps and put the headings on the board: the five essential steps, and they go on the board as five headings: 1 Get organised — brainstorm, then choose; three or four main ideas is plenty, and the opening should be a photo, a clip or a short funny story · 2 Use visual materials — photos, charts, diagrams, but not too many: «the star of your presentation should be you, not your materials» · 3 Rehearse your performance — read it aloud until you know it, then note cards, then a mirror, then time it and cut · 4 Connect with your audience — watch them, stand still, keep your energy up, and thank them at the end
  4. Groups of four, four minutes, one step each: which step do people ignore most, and what happens when they do? Every group reports one sentence.
  5. Take the sentence that always gets a reaction and put it on the board: The star of your presentation should be you, not your materials. Ninety seconds on whether the class believes it.
  6. Kagan Numbered Heads Together for the check: which step is the one THIS class needs most? Take the vote and leave it on the board.
📺 On screen: the four step headings on the board with the class's chosen sentence beneath them. Backup: the article is on the page and needs nothing else
👦 Students do: read for structure, argue one step in a group of four, then vote as a class

Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that is also the presentation rehearsal step

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands, in two lines facing each other.
  2. Fifteen seconds each way, on your signal: Tell your partner about a place you like — and give one example with such as or for instance.
  3. Line B moves one to the right. New partner, same fifteen seconds, different place. Four rotations.
  4. Then the point, said in one line: That is step three. You just rehearsed the same thing four times and the fourth was the best one.
  5. Sit: Page one hundred and thirty-two. Two photographs, and one of you is the examiner.
📺 On screen: the phrase columns on the board behind them — nothing is displayed
👦 Students do: every student speaks four times in four minutes and hears four different partners

🎯 Speaking Part 2 · one photograph, one minute
groups of three
Why: 🎯 Speaking Part 2 · one photograph, one minute

WHAT WE DO: SB p.132: the examiner's script read as printed, roles rotating

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Name the format and the tips: Speaking Part 2 — describing a photograph. The examiner gives each candidate a different photo; you talk on your own for about a minute while your partner listens. Part 2 takes two to three minutes for the pair. Say as much as you can: who the people are, what they are doing, where they are, what the weather is like, what objects you can see. If you cannot remember a word, describe it another way. Keep talking until you are stopped — and do not describe anything that is not in the picture.
  2. The script and the photographs: the two photographs in the script are «People eating together» (Candidate A) and «People doing sport» (Candidate B), and the examiner's back-up prompts are printed: talk about the people · talk about the place · talk about other things in the photograph
  3. both parts are run in threes with the roles rotating: one examiner reading the printed script, two candidates. Everybody is the examiner once — reading the script aloud is what teaches the format
  4. Two minutes to look at both photographs and note vocabulary. Collect a few words on the board — food, table, sharing; team, pitch, kit.
  5. Round one, four minutes: the examiner reads the script exactly as printed, times a minute for Candidate A, then a minute for Candidate B, and uses the back-up prompts the moment there is silence.
  6. Rotate. Round two, four minutes. Rotate again, round three, four minutes — everybody has described a photograph twice and been the examiner once.
  7. Monitor with a sheet: six good phrases WITH names, six silences or errors WITHOUT. Interrupt nobody.
  8. Two minutes: the six good phrases read out with names, and one question to the room — What did these six do when they ran out of certain things to say?
📺 On screen: the examiner script on p.132 and the phrase columns on the board — no devices, this is the speaking stage
👦 Students do: every student talks for two full minutes as a candidate and runs the script and the clock once as examiner

Why: 🎯 Speaking Part 3 · discussing options

WHAT WE DO: SB p.133: the penfriend situation, discussed and decided in twos

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Name the format and the tips: Speaking Part 3 — discussing options. The examiner describes a situation, gives the pair some pictures, and they talk together for about two minutes and try to reach agreement. Listen to the situation and look at the pictures before you start. Give your partner time to speak and pull them in if they go quiet — the marks are for the conversation, not for the monologue.
  2. The situation: the situation in the book: A teenager is writing to a new penfriend and is deciding what to write about. The pictures are possible topics for the first email; the pair discusses them and decides which would be most interesting to read about
  3. Put the phrase bank from block 7 back on the board in three columns — How about…? · What if we…? · If we…, it would be… · … might be a better choice · It's time to decide · Are you OK with that? They used these in Unit 14 and they need them again today.
  4. The rule, said once and policed by walking past: No decision until you have discussed at least three topics with a reason each. Agreeing immediately is the only way to fail this part.
  5. Round one, four minutes: examiner reads the script, the two candidates talk for two minutes, examiner closes it. Rotate.
  6. Round two with a new examiner and, if the clock allows, a different pairing — the second run is fuller, faster and better.
  7. Two volunteer pairs repeat for the class, three minutes: the class listens with the columns visible and marks every phrase used, and says at the end whether an agreement was actually reached.
  8. Mixed ability: stronger pairs redo the task with a different decision — which topic would be WORST to write about, and why.
📺 On screen: the suggestion-and-decision columns rebuilt on the board — no devices, this is the speaking stage
👦 Students do: every student discusses for four minutes in two different pairings and examines once

Why: 🎯 Speaking out · the two-minute presentation

WHAT WE DO: the Life skills project, delivered — the four steps used for real

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the project: PROJECT — a presentation about a nature park you want to visit, built on seven questions: the name of the park · where it is · what time of year you want to go · what the weather is like then · the interesting natural sights · the plants or animals you hope to see · the activities you want to do. Notes first, then a rehearsal in pairs, then the class
  2. Six minutes to plan, alone: seven questions, key words only. Walk round and swap full sentences for key words wherever you see them — that is step three being enforced.
  3. Two minutes rehearsing in pairs, quietly and simultaneously — the whole room talking at once. Nobody listens critically yet; this is the mirror stage.
  4. Groups of four, six minutes: each student gives their two-minute presentation to their group. The listeners have one job each — one counts the giving-examples phrases, one watches whether the speaker looks up, one notes the best sentence.
  5. Each group chooses one presentation and that student repeats it for the class, three minutes total. Applause is compulsory and costs nothing.
  6. One minute of feedback against the four steps, not against the English: which step did the class do best today, and which one is still the weakest?
  7. Cooler if the clock allows: copy the four step headings and add key words under each — a one-page reference sheet for the next presentation they have to give, in any subject
📺 On screen: the four step headings and the phrase columns, both used as checklists by the listeners — no devices, this is the speaking stage
👦 Students do: every student plans, rehearses and delivers a two-minute presentation, and audits three others with a named job
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE task 38 (the opinion piece built on a data table) and the oral tasks 3–4. A presentation and task 38 are the same job: select from data, compare, comment, and end with a position — and the giving-examples phrases raise the range mark in both.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: give one data table on the board (film genres, five rows) and have pairs say two comparisons and one opinion aloud, using such as and overall. Five minutes, taken from the group presentations, which can run with three students per group instead of four.

Form

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Three or four main ideas» — why not eight? → because nobody remembers eight, and you run out of time. What do you do with the other four? → cut them.
«The star of your presentation should be you, not your materials.» What does that mean in practice? → fewer slides, fewer words on them, more of you.
In Part 3 your partner suggests the first idea and you agree immediately. What have you lost? → the marks are for discussing the options, so you have lost most of them.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The Life skills page is treated as fillerIt is framed as the tool for the two exam tasks that follow in the same lesson, and the frame is said aloud at minute five. Nothing on the page is done that is not used later.
Presentations become read-aloud essaysStep 3 is enforced physically: notes on cards, not sentences, and the cards are checked before anybody speaks. Anyone holding full sentences swaps them for five key words.
Part 2 dies at twenty secondsThe phrase columns from last lesson go back on the board and the examiner in each three is instructed to use the back-up prompts — talk about the people, the place, the other things — the moment there is a silence.
Part 3 is over in thirty secondsThe rule is announced and policed by walking past: no decision until at least three of the topics have been discussed aloud with a reason. Pairs who finish early get a second situation, not praise.
Only the confident students presentEverybody presents, but to a group of three, not to the class — and only two volunteers repeat for the room. That is the difference between a lesson everyone speaks in and a lesson four people speak in.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
