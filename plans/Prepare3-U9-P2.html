🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Say how somebody feels with ten adjectives, and give advice four different ways — You should … · Why don't you … · Why not … · How about … — each of which takes a different verb form.
What is genuinely hard
Two things at once. The adjectives split into ‑ed and ‑ing pairs that Russian does not distinguish (I am boring for I am bored), and the four advice openers take three different forms after them: bare verb after should and why don't you, and ‑ing after how about.
Typical error to expect
The film was excited / I am boring instead of exciting and bored — ‑ing describes the thing, ‑ed describes the person. The TB flags this on p.197 with surprised and surprising [high priority, A2/B1]
You should to ask the teacher / Why don't you to go? — a to-infinitive after should and after why don't you. Both take the bare verb; only How about takes ‑ing [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The reading comes early and fast, because its job is to hand the class the content of the advice they will give an hour later — every piece of advice in the final stage can be traced back to a line on the Teen Health page. The pronunciation slot is short and sits between two heavy stages as a change of gear, not as a topic. The listening is the hardest format the course has met so far and it is deliberately followed by eight minutes on why the other two pictures are wrong: choosing the right answer is luck at this level, explaining the wrong ones is the skill. The lesson ends on a role-play rather than an exercise because advice is a speech act — it only exists when somebody is actually asking for it.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Somebody in this room is going to fail an exam this year. Not because they are stupid — because that is what happens. Today you learn the four sentences you say to that person. And one warning: «I am boring» and «I am bored» are not the same sentence, and one of them is about you.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a fast recycle of last lesson's reflexives, and the bridge into the health website

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.

  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page one hundred. Same unit, different question: what do you do when a friend has a problem?

Lesson frame

Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Four sentence openers on the board, in a column: You should … · Why don't you … · Why not … · How about …
  2. These four all do the same job. Three of them are followed by the same kind of word and one is different. Which one? Take answers for 30 seconds — do NOT confirm.
  3. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: today ends with four people who have a problem, and everybody in the room will give advice to one of them using all four openers.
  4. Set the bar: «That's terrible» is not advice. Advice has a verb in it and tells the person what to do.
📺 On screen: the four openers in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: guess which opener is the odd one out. Expect them not to know, which is fine — the answer arrives at minute 74. Typical problem: somebody offers you must; accept it and park it, it is too strong for advice

Lead-in · four health questions
pairs
Why: They answer the website's four questions before they read it, so the reading becomes a check on their own opinion rather than a comprehension exercise. That is the difference between reading a text and using one.

WHAT WE DO: the four questions on the Teen Health page, answered before the page is read

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Scratch your head, look worried, and ask (TB p.197): How do I feel? What do you think my problem is? Take three guesses.
  2. Then read out the website's four questions of the day: How much exercise should I do? Are some foods healthier than others? Can it be dangerous to listen to loud music? Is it OK to feel sad sometimes?
  3. Kagan Think-Pair-Share: pairs answer all four in three minutes, in one sentence each. Books stay shut.
  4. Collect one answer per question on the board and leave them there — the reading is going to agree with some of them and disagree with others.
📺 On screen: the four questions in chalk, with space under each for the class's own answer — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: answer four questions they have never been asked in English. Expect confident answers to questions 1 and 2 and hesitation on question 4. Typical problem: the answers come in Russian — take the idea, give them the English, write the English

Reading · Teen Health
solo → pairs
Why: Four short answers to four questions teenagers genuinely ask. Matching headings to paragraphs is real gist reading: it cannot be done by finding the same word twice.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.100 (track 128): read the website and match the four questions to the four paragraphs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pre-teach one word only (TB p.197): An ache is a pain that goes on for a long time. Stomach ache. Headache.
  2. Give them a time limit — 3 minutes — to read all four paragraphs and match. Key: 1 Can it be dangerous to listen to loud music? · 2 How much exercise should I do? · 3 Is it OK to feel sad sometimes? · 4 Are some foods healthier than others?.
  3. Play track 128 while they follow, then check the matching as a class and compare with the answers on the board from the lead-in: Where were we right? Where does the website disagree with us?
  4. ex.3 in pairs, 4 minutes: tell your partner what the text says about eight things — earphones · hearing well · too much sport · playing sport after meals · feelings · a friend, parent or teacher · a plate of chips · variety. Key: 1 sometimes the person next to you on a train can hear the music through your earphones · 2 if you play music too loudly now, you won't hear well when you are older · 3 too much sport can be bad for you — you can hurt something · 4 it is not a good idea to play sport just after eating; you can get a stomach ache · 5 it is normal for feelings to change — everyone feels unhappy sometimes · 6 talk to a friend, a parent or a teacher about your problems · 7 a plate of fruit is healthier than a plate of chips · 8 if you eat a bit of everything, you will be healthier.
  5. For your own reference, what the page actually says: SB p.100–101, a health website for teenagers with four questions of the day: How much exercise should I do? · Are some foods healthier than others? · Can it be dangerous to listen to loud music? · Is it OK to feel sad sometimes? The answers are practical rather than preachy: the test for «too loud» is whether the person sitting next to you on the train can hear your earphones; about an hour of activity a day is enough and you should not play sport straight after eating; feeling unhappy sometimes is normal and the advice is to talk to a friendly adult; and a variety of ordinary food — fruit, vegetables, eggs — gives the energy and the confidence to study.
📺 On screen: track 128 in the board app with the website on the screen (Backup: the page is printed on SB p.100–101 and the audioscript is on TB p.303 — read it out yourself at natural speed while the class follows in the book)
👦 Students do: read against a clock, match, then retell eight points to a partner. Expect the matching to be quick and ex.3 to be the slow part — retelling is harder than finding. Typical error: they read the whole page before starting to match, which is what the time limit is for

34–40′
Pronunciation · food and good
class → pairs
Why: Six minutes on two vowels that Russian merges into one. It looks like a detail and it is the reading of a hundred very common words.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5 p.100 (track 129): /uː/ and /ʊ/, and why they are not the same vowel

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the model sentence on the board and say it: These types of food are good for you. Ask the question from the book: Do both «oo» words have the same sound?
  2. Play track 129. Listen, then repeat chorally, then individually.
  3. Name the difference physically, not phonetically: TB p.197: the vowel in food is longer and the lips are rounder; the vowel in good is short and the lips are relaxed. The model sentence has both: These types of food are good for you. Say it, then say it wrong on purpose — swapping the two vowels is what makes the difference audible.
  4. Two minutes in pairs: find more words for each column. Key: The two sounds are different. Words like food /uː/: blue, move, school, room, two, shoe, cool. Words like good /ʊ/: put, could, would, wood, sugar, woman..
  5. Mixed ability, TB p.197: TB p.197, for the pronunciation: read the possible answers out in a jumbled list and have weaker students only sort them into /uː/ and /ʊ/, instead of generating their own.
📺 On screen: track 129 in the board app (Backup: say the model sentence yourself, twice, then say the two vowels in isolation and exaggerate the lip position — this is a stage that works better from a face than from a speaker anyway)
👦 Students do: listen, repeat, then hunt for more words. Expect sugar and woman to surprise them. Typical error: could and would pronounced with the long vowel — hold up two fingers for short

WHAT WE DO: two minutes of movement between the reading half and the listening half

Vocabulary · adjectives for feelings
pairs → class
Why: Ten words for emotions, learnt from faces rather than from translation, and immediately used about situations the students have actually been in. The ‑ed / ‑ing pair is dealt with here, once, instead of being corrected for the rest of the year.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 p.100–101: ten adjectives matched to ten faces, then used about real situations

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 in pairs, 4 minutes: match the ten adjectives to photos A–J. Key: A lazy · B embarrassed · C upset · D lonely · E angry · F worried · G confident · H surprised · I friendly · J unhappy.
  2. Name the positives before the negatives take over: TB p.197: three of the ten are positive — confident, friendly, surprised — and the rest are not. Worth naming, because students file every emotion adjective under «bad». And the ‑ed / ‑ing pair belongs here: I was surprised when I came first against a surprising number of teenagers never watch TV.
  3. ex.2, ten gapped sentences, in pairs, 4 minutes. Demonstrate item 0 yourself (TB p.197): I was ______ when I got to the front of the queue and remembered I had no money. (embarrassed). Key: 1 lazy · 2 embarrassed · 3 friendly · 4 confident · 5 lonely · 6 surprised · 7 angry · 8 worried · 9 unhappy · 10 upset.
  4. Now the ‑ed / ‑ing pair, once, on the board, and never again: I was surprised when I came first in the test. A surprising number of teenagers never watch TV. One line under each: person / thing.
  5. ex.3, Kagan Think-Pair-Share: Tell your partner about a time you felt one of these. Two rounds, different partners.
📺 On screen: the ten photos on the screen, and the ‑ed / ‑ing pair written in chalk beside them (Backup: the photos are printed on SB p.100–101 — students work from the book and you keep only the two model sentences on the board)
👦 Students do: match, complete, then tell a real story. Expect lonely and upset to be the two that need a second pass. Typical error: I was boring in the lesson — this is the moment to catch it, and the board line is why

Listening · five conversations
solo → class
Why: The hardest listening format of the course so far: three pictures, all three mentioned. The prediction stage comes first because without it they answer from the first word they recognise.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.101 (track 130): five short conversations, three pictures each

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Before anything plays, ex.1 and the strategy: TB p.198: the questions are mostly about numbers, so students should say the number in each picture in their heads BEFORE the recording starts. Then one orientation question per item: Who is speaking to whom in question 1? (a trainer and someone in the team) Where is the girl in question 2? (in a café) How much are the cheapest earphones? (£10.20) What season is it in question 4? (autumn) Will question 5 be about the future or the past? (future).
  2. Key to ex.1: 1 three clocks · 2 three pictures of food · 3 three prices in pounds and pence · 4 three pictures of the weather · 5 three activities.
  3. Say the trap out loud once: You will hear something about all three pictures every time. The question decides, not the word.
  4. Play track 130. First time you answer. Then thirty seconds of silence, pens down.
  5. Play track 130 again. Second time you check. Change an answer only if you can say why.
  6. Check with Kagan Showdown — A, B or C on the mini-whiteboard, all boards up at once. Key: 1 B · 2 A · 3 C · 4 A · 5 A.
📺 On screen: track 130 in the board app with the five sets of pictures on the screen (Backup: the pictures are printed on SB p.101 and the audioscript is on TB p.303 — read it out yourself, twice, at natural speed and with no extra help)
👦 Students do: predict, listen twice, then reveal together. Expect 3 or 4 out of 5. Typical error: Where marks go: question 3. Three prices are said out loud and the one that counts is the one the boy actually pays, not the one he looks at first.

66–74′
Why the other pictures are wrong
pairs → class
Why: Naming why a wrong option is wrong is harder than choosing the right one, which is exactly why it earns eight minutes. Seeing the trap built is what stops you falling into it.

WHAT WE DO: the distractor analysis: for each question, say why the OTHER two pictures are wrong

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Numbered Heads Together: each pair takes one question and must produce a sentence starting B is wrong because …
  2. Play the relevant piece of track 130 again for any question that stalls — one question at a time, not the whole recording.
  3. Write the rule they take away in their own words: Hearing a word is not an answer. Ask three students to say it back before you move on.
  4. Then flip it: Question 3 had three prices in it. If you were writing this test, which price would you put in as the trap? Making the trap is what proves they have seen it.
📺 On screen: the five sets of pictures back on the screen, one question at a time, with track 130 ready to replay in fragments (Backup: the pictures are in the book and the audioscript is on TB p.303 — read out the line that proves each answer and write the key phrase on the board)
👦 Students do: explain why each wrong picture is wrong. Expect this to be harder than choosing, which is exactly why it is here. Typical error: «because it's wrong» — push for the line from the recording every single time

🎯 Speaking out · giving advice
pairs → class
Why: The productive outcome: four real teenage worries and four ways to answer them. The phrase box is the language aim of the stage, not a support — You should, Why don't you and How about take three different verb forms.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.101: four teenagers with a problem, and four ways to answer them

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 first, 2 minutes: match the four photos to the four problems. Key: 1 C · 2 A · 3 B · 4 D. The four problems are: 1 I failed my exam. · 2 They haven't picked me for the team. · 3 My parents don't understand me. · 4 I think it's broken.
  2. Ask which is the most serious (TB p.198) and take a vote — it makes them choose rather than take the first one.
  3. Now the four openers from the board get their forms, and this is the language aim of the lesson: You should GO. Why don't you GO. Why not GO. How about GOING. Three bare verbs and one ‑ing. Drill it four times.
  4. Read the model exchange together: SB p.101, the model exchange: I failed my exam. What can I do? — You should ask the teacher what you need to work on. You're always listening to music. Maybe you should spend more time studying.
  5. Role-play in pairs, 5 minutes: one is the teenager, the other gives advice, and the advice must come from the Teen Health page wherever possible. Swap after two problems.
  6. Three pairs perform. TB p.198: weaker students may write the dialogue down before they say it. Some pairs then role-play at the front. Ask which of the four situations is the most serious before they start — it makes them choose rather than take the first one.
📺 On screen: the four photos and the phrase box on the screen, with the four openers and their forms in chalk underneath (Backup: the photos and the box are printed on SB p.101 — keep the book open and write the four openers on the board, which is where they need to be anyway)
👦 Students do: match, then advise. Expect How about to be the one that produces the infinitive by mistake — that is the whole point of drilling it four times. Typical error: advice that is really sympathy — the rule from the frame catches it

  • match a question to the paragraph that answers it
  • hear the difference between food and good

Structure
You should ask the teacher what you need to work on. · Why don't you talk to your parents about it? · Why not join another club? · How about spending more time studying?
Form
should + bare infinitive · why don't you + bare infinitive · why not + bare infinitive · how about + ‑ing. Adjectives: ‑ed describes the person, ‑ing describes the thing.
Pron. watch
/uː/ as in food — long, lips rounded; /ʊ/ as in good — short, lips relaxed. The model sentence carries both: These types of food are good for you. Watch also the stress in emˈbarrassed and ˈconfident.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«The film was boring.» Is the film asleep? → no. ‑ing describes the thing.
«I was bored.» Who is asleep now? → me. ‑ed describes the person.
«You should ask the teacher.» Is that an order or a suggestion? → a suggestion. That is why it is not must.
«How about ___ (spend) more time studying?» Which form? → spending. Only how about takes ‑ing.
«Why don't you to talk to her?» What is wrong? → no to. The verb is bare.
In question 3 of the listening the boy looks at three prices. Which one is the answer? → the one he pays, not the one he sees first.

Assumptions: SB p.100–101, syllabus lesson 57. Reflexive pronouns were taught last lesson and are recycled here without being retaught. should is known passively from Unit 9's modal work and is used, not introduced. Of the ten adjectives they usually have angry, happy-adjacent words and worried; embarrassed, confident, lonely and upset are the ones that need teaching, and lonely will be confused with alone.

The class translates lonely and alone identicallyOne board line, no theory: I live alone and I like it. I feel lonely and I don't. Alone is a fact, lonely is a feeling. Then point back at Ben, who went for a ride by himself and was perfectly happy.
«Why are we listening to five conversations about earphones?»Because the format is the hardest one in the book and because every option is mentioned. Say it in one sentence at the start of the stage: You will hear something about all three pictures every time. Hearing a word is not an answer.
The advice role-play turns into one-word sympathyPut the four openers on the board and make them a rule of the stage: Every piece of advice starts with one of these four. «That's bad» is not advice. Weaker pairs write the dialogue first, which TB p.198 explicitly allows.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
