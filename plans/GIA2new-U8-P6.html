🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Why (for the teacher): The speaking and the writing of this unit are one task in two channels, which is why they share one lesson. For new students the hardest thing here is not the grammar but the social formula for refusing: sorry + I can't + a reason + another time. Give it on cards and drill it until it is automatic. The dialogue is built from the cards, not invented from nothing; the messages are written in seven steps with the model in front of them.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“The hardest thing in this language isn't grammar. It's saying no to a friend without losing them. Watch Sam. Maria wants a bike ride. Saturday morning? Dentist. Saturday afternoon? Barbecue. And Sunday morning Maria plays tennis. Three no's — and they still meet at the park at half past two. Today you learn ten phrases that make that possible, and you use them twice: out loud, then in a message. Cards in your hand the whole time. Nobody speaks without cards.”
The gist, if you need to restate it: the hardest thing in the language is not the grammar but how to say no to a friend and not lose them. We learn ten phrases that do that, and we use them twice: out loud first, then in a message. The cards stay in their hands the whole time.

WHAT WE DO: circle + P5 rewind: the three columns of the sorting sheet

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Circle: One thing you're doing this weekend. Your model first — I'm meeting my sister on Saturday. — and the frame on the board: I'm … on …
  2. Then Kagan RoundRobin: go round the circle, each student gives one phrase from the frame on the board, and the order is known in advance — nobody sits in dread of being called on. Stuck? Read the frame off the board out loud, that is allowed and does not count as failure.
  3. Rewind from the sheet: 'I'll probably come.' Which column? 'I'm going to save money.' 'We're meeting at seven.'
  4. Bridge: You can lock a day now. But what if the other person's day is locked too?

Model · how people arrange to meet

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–4 p.103: the video twice, Key phrases onto cards, completing the dialogue

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 as a run-up: Two sentences about your plans for next weekend. Write them first.
  2. Video play 1 — two questions only (TB key: 1 They're going to go for a bike ride. · 2 at the park at half past two on Sunday afternoon).
  3. Simple TB follow-up questions: What is Sam doing on Saturday morning? (he's going to the dentist) · What is Maria doing on Sunday morning? (she's playing tennis).
  4. Video play 2 with a pause on every Key phrase — repeat CHORALLY (TB routine). TB key ex.3: How about Saturday morning? · I can't Saturday morning. I'm … · Are you free in the afternoon? · No, sorry, I'm … · Are you doing anything on Sunday? · I'm free in the afternoon.
  5. Hand out the Key phrases cards to everyone — they stay in their hands to the end of the lesson.
  6. ex.4 complete the dialogue from the cards (TB key: 1 I can't · 2 Are you free · 3 No, sorry · 4 Are you doing · 5 I'm free). Close the first gap together on the board — show out loud how to find the right phrase on the card.
  7. Then Kagan RallyCoach: in the pair the first student says their version of the gap out loud, the second holds the cards and works as coach — either confirming (Yes! That's it!) or pointing to the right card; on the next gap they swap roles. One always answers but the other always checks — which is why neither of them goes quiet.
  8. Then read the completed dialogue in roles and swap roles.
📺 On screen: video SB p.103 (Backup: read the dialogue in roles with one of the students, twice, the second time with pauses on the Key phrases) · Key phrases cards
👦 Students do: watch, repeat chorally, complete. Expect: choral repetition with pauses works better than any explanation

Dialogue from the cards
pairs

WHAT WE DO: the refusal formula → drilling → their own dialogue from the cards, written first, then out loud

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The formula on the board: Sorry + I can't + reason + another time.
  2. Choral drill: you invite — the class refuses and offers another time. Ten goes: Let's go for a pizza tonight! · How about the skate park tomorrow? · Are you free on Saturday morning?
  3. Once the formula is sounding chorally, move it into pairs — Kagan RallyRobin: the first invites, the second refuses with the formula and offers another time, then they swap, and so on invitation by invitation for a minute and a half. The cards are in their hands and may be read from — and everyone is speaking at once, not one student at the board.
  4. Three ways to suggest (TB Extra activity) onto the cards: Why don't we … ? · Let's … · How about … ? Plus Poor you! and Great idea!
  5. Skills boost with support: map out the week (two days free), then WRITE the dialogue from the cards — 8 lines, at least one refusal.
  6. Then read it in pairs in roles and swap roles (TB advice).
📺 On screen: SB p.103 Skills boost · Key phrases cards · the refusal formula on the board · timer
👦 Students do: write the dialogue, then perform it. Expect: writing it down before speaking removes the freeze; the refusal is easiest of all with a card

WHAT WE DO: pp.104–105: emojis, the message exchanges, the phrase table, apostrophes through two questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 emojis as a run-up (TB key: 1 camping · 2 bike · 3 Bye! · 4 happy · 5 a party · 6 sad). The TB Culture note briefly: emojis were invented in Japan in the 1990s, they have been in the dictionary since 2013, and there are about 3000 of them now.
  2. ex.2–3 with the TB prompt: write out the three names — Ian, Jake, Emma — and tick each one as you find the information (TB key ex.3: 1 He's leaving on Friday around six. · 2 It's her sister's birthday and they're having a party. · 3 He's meeting her at 11 o'clock. · 4 They're meeting in the park. · 5 He's going to his grandparents' house for lunch.).
  3. ex.4 the phrase table — fill it in together on the board (TB key: Inviting — Do you fancy coming? Would you like to come? · Yes — I'd love to. Great! Sounds fun! · No — Sorry, I can't. Sorry, I'm busy …). TB routine: you invite a student — they answer and invite the next one, in a chain.
  4. Apostrophes through two questions instead of a rule: 1 Is it a short form? (I'm, can't, we're, I'd) · 2 Is it 'belongs to'? One person → 's. More than one person → s'.
  5. ex.7 put them in (TB key: 1 It's, I'm · 2 I'd, I've · 3 can't, We're, Paul's · 4 parents', they're). Work the first sentence through together on the board, asking both questions out loud — that is the model.
  6. The other three — Kagan Showdown: everyone writes the sentence with the apostrophes on a mini-whiteboard, holds it with the text facing them, and on the command Showdown! they all lift together. You see the whole class at once, there is no time to copy the neighbour — and getting it wrong is not frightening, because everyone lifts at the same moment.
📺 On screen: SB pp.104–105 · mini-whiteboards (Backup: print the three message exchanges on sheets and students circle the apostrophes in pencil; write the two apostrophe questions on the board)
👦 Students do: work through the messages and the apostrophes. Expect: “parents'” is the hardest; explain it on your fingers and draw two parents on the board

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, learners can ask about plans, refuse politely with a reason and agree on a time and place using Key phrases from cards, and write an invitation exchange with correct apostrophes.
  • Subsidiary. ex.2–3 the video and the Key phrases; ex.4 completing the dialogue; Skills boost with support; US/UK and Phrasebook p.125; pp.104–105 emojis, three message exchanges, the phrase table, apostrophes ex.6–7, their own exchange ex.8.
  • Personal. The Key phrases cards are physically in everyone's hands — they are not a “crib sheet” but a tool. Explain apostrophes with two questions, not with a lecture.
By the end, students can…
  • ask about someone's plans in three different ways
  • say no politely, give a reason and offer another time
  • agree on a place and a time
  • write an invitation exchange with correct apostrophes

Structure
What are you doing Saturday? · Are you free in the afternoon? · How about Saturday morning? · I can't Saturday morning. I'm going to the dentist. · No, sorry, I'm … · I'm free in the afternoon. · Let's meet at the park at 2:30. · Do you fancy coming? · I'd love to. · Sorry, I can't. · Sounds fun!
Form
after fancy comes the -ing form (Do you fancy COMING?); after would like comes the infinitive with to. Apostrophes: contraction (I'm, can't, we're, I'd) and possession (my sister's birthday, my grandparents' house).
Pron. watch
the intonation of a refusal is soft and falling: “No, sorry, I'm …” · “Poor you!” — falling · “Awesome!” — rising
Concept checks (CCQs) · Assumptions
“Do you fancy to come?” — what is wrong? → Do you fancy COMING?
“Its my sisters birthday” — how many apostrophes are missing? → two.
“my parents' house” — how many parents? → two, which is why the apostrophe comes after the s.

Assumptions: SB pp.103–105, syllabus pair 67. going to and present continuous have been in use since P5; new are the refusal phrases and the apostrophes.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
They go silent because they don't know what to sayThe Key phrases cards in everyone's hands; the rule: you may read from the card, that is fine.
The refusal sounds rude, or there isn't one at allThe formula on the board: sorry + I can't + a reason + another time. Drill it with ten invitations chorally.
Apostrophes go in everywhereTwo questions instead of a rule: is it a contraction? and if it is possession — one person or more than one?
Classroom management — signals
«Eyes up in 3-2-1» — count down calmly → all eyes on you at «1». No shouting over noise, ever.
Visible timer — timer on screen = we work; timer stops = pens/hands down. The timer is the boss, not you.
Volume dial — arm up/down = louder/quieter; «radio whisper» for rehearsals.
«Boards up!» — mini-whiteboard rounds: everyone commits, all boards up together — nobody hides, nobody shouts first.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, class answers with two and freezes — instant reset mid-game.
