🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Make, ask and correct the present perfect — have/has + past participle — with ever and never, and know when English wants it instead of the past simple.
What is genuinely hard
This tense does not exist in Russian. There is nothing to translate it from, so it is not a form they get wrong — it is a distinction they cannot see. Two separate problems: the MEANING (a time is named or it is not) and the FORM (the third column, which is irregular for exactly the verbs they use most).
Typical error to expect
Have you ever saw a whale? · I have went there — the past simple form used where the participle is needed [high priority, A2/B1]
I have seen him yesterday — the present perfect used with a finished time. If the sentence says WHEN, English wants the past simple [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
This lesson exists because the unit has three of them. In a two-lesson unit the present perfect would get twenty minutes at the end of a lexis lesson and would then have to be re-taught in Unit 15 and again in Unit 16. Here it gets ninety, and the shape follows the two problems in order. First the meaning, discovered from two questions about walking home from school: one names a time and one does not, and the students state the difference. Then the form, on its own, for fourteen minutes and with no meaning question attached — because trying to build has ridden while also deciding whether the tense is right is two jobs at once and neither gets done. Pronunciation follows immediately, because the ‑ed ending is part of the form and not a separate topic. Only after the break does correction come, which is where the two problems finally meet. The ten minutes with the Grammar reference are the luxury this three-lesson shape buys: the students write the rule in their own words, with their own examples, which almost never happens in a 90-minute unit lesson. And the last stage is a lie detector, because you cannot play it without the tense.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Today, one tense — and it is the one English has and Russian doesn't. I climbed a tree tells me when. I've climbed a tree tells me about your life. By the end you write five sentences about yourself, four true and one false, and we catch the lie.”

Grammar · discovery · present perfect
board → solo
Why: Two questions about walking home from school do the whole job: one names a time and one does not. The rule is stated by the students, because a rule given from the front is a rule that has to be given again next week.

WHAT WE DO: guided discovery of the MEANING from two questions: ex.1 p.77

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. Ask a student: Did you walk home from school yesterday? If the answer is no, ask a different student until you get a no. Then ask the same student: Have you ever walked home?
  2. Write both questions on the board, one under the other. Then the three questions that do the teaching (TB p.150): In the first question, am I asking about a specific time in the past? (yes) · So what tense is it? (past simple) · Is the second question about a specific time? (no — it is asking about ANY time in the past).
  3. Wait for a student to say the difference out loud. Do not say it for them. This is the silence the lesson is built on.
  4. Books open. ex.1 — complete the five rules. Key: 1 past · 2 past · 3 can't · 4 ever · 5 never
  5. Kagan Think-Pair-Share on one question before you move on: Why can't we say «I have seen him yesterday»? Twenty seconds alone, then the partner, then two answers to the class.
  6. Add the superlative note from TB p.150 in one sentence and one example: with a superlative English usually takes this tense — It's the highest mountain I've ever climbed.
📺 On screen: the two questions written on the board as they are asked (Backup: none needed — this stage is a live conversation with one student and a piece of chalk, and it cannot fail)
👦 Students do: answer the two questions, then state the rule. Expect one student to get it in about fifteen seconds and the rest to understand once it is said in their own words. Typical problem: they explain it in Russian — take it, then ask for the English version

Why: Controlled practice on the form alone — have/has plus the third column — before any meaning question is added. This is the lesson the whole block exists for, so the form gets its own uninterrupted block of time.

WHAT WE DO: the FORM on its own: ex.2 p.77, with the participle cards

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Demonstrate on the board first, exactly as TB p.150 does: They / pick / three apples → They have picked three apples.
  2. ex.2 — make the six sentences, alone, 5 minutes. Key: 1 My mum has visited a safari park. · 2 I've never camped in the winter. · 3 Have you ever picked fruit from a tree? · 4 Has Kris ever climbed a mountain? · 5 We've never returned to the amazing campsite in the woods. · 6 My little sisters have played in snow.
  3. Then the second half of ex.2, which is where the form gets tested: turn the statements into questions and the questions into statements. Point at column 3 every time somebody hesitates.
  4. Kagan RallyCoach for the check: A says the sentence, B coaches, swap every two items.
  5. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with the participle cards, 4 minutes: the infinitive on one side, the three forms on the other. Quiz your partner. Coach if he's wrong. Trade cards. Find a new partner!
  6. Mixed-ability note worth reading before the lesson, and it is the most useful one in the block: TB p.150, and this is the note that matters most in the block: the form AND the meaning of the present perfect are hard for weaker students, so extend the presentation rather than the practice. Explain that ever means «in your life» and never means «not ever». Point out that a regular past participle is the same as the past simple form but the spelling rules still apply — travel – travelled, worry – worried. And the book's own suggestion, which is worth taking: the song Brighton in the Rain, which begins «I've never been to Athens and I've never been to Rome» and has the present perfect with never in every single line, regular and irregular verbs together.

👦 Students do: build six sentences, then transform them, then mingle with the cards. Expect ridden, flown and swum to be the three that never come automatically. Typical error: My little sisters has played — the plural subject with has

34–42′
Pronunciation · past participles
class → teams
Why: The ‑ed ending is an extra syllable only after /t/ or /d/. It takes eight minutes, it is discovered from two words, and without it the practice sounds like reading a list.

WHAT WE DO: the ‑ed ending: ex.4 p.77 (tracks 101–102)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.150: play the recording and pause after the first two words. «Is the ‑ed in camped an extra syllable?» (no) · «Is the ‑ed in recorded an extra syllable?» (yes). That is the rule, discovered in fifteen seconds.
  2. Play the rest of track 101 and have them complete the table. Then play track 102 to check and repeat. Key: camped (no extra syllable): climbed, explored, finished, jumped, played, tidied, tried · recorded (extra syllable): collected, ended, hated, painted, wanted
  3. Elicit the rule in one sentence: ‑ed is an extra syllable when the word ends in a /t/ or a /d/ sound.
  4. TB p.150 goes one step further and it is worth the minute: within the camped column, ‑ed is /d/ after a voiced sound and /t/ after an unvoiced one — opened = /d/, walked = /t/. Do not turn it into a rule to memorise; just say the two words next to each other.
  5. Extension in teams, two minutes: add the past participle of as many verbs as you can to each column. It is a race and it is free revision of the whole participle list.
📺 On screen: tracks 101–102 in the whiteboard app (Backup with no sound: say all twelve words yourself, twice — for this task a live voice is actually better because you can exaggerate the extra syllable. The table is printed on SB p.77)
👦 Students do: sort, check, repeat, then race. Expect climbed and played to be put in the wrong column by half the class on the first attempt — the /d/ letter is not a /d/ sound at the end. Typical error: adding a syllable to everything, because that is what the spelling suggests

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.77: repair five sentences — where the meaning and the form finally meet

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read out the instruction and add the one thing TB p.150 adds: with a superlative, as in sentences 2 and 4, English uses this tense — that is why they look odd.
  2. ex.3 — correct the five sentences, in pairs, 5 minutes. Key: 1 I've never tried sleeping under the stars before. · 2 These are the best apples I've ever picked. · 3 My mother has never played the piano. · 4 And it is in a forest. The best place I've ever visited. · 5 No, I've never failed an exam.
  3. Kagan Showdown on the five: everybody writes, everybody reveals at Boards up!, nobody is exposed alone.
  4. Then five more sentences of your own on the board, and these are the ones that matter: I have seen him yesterday · Have you ever saw a whale? · I haven't never been to Moscow · She have finished · We have went there in July. Thirty seconds each, in pairs.
  5. Point at the boxed question on the board — Does the sentence say WHEN? — for the first and the last one. Do not explain; point.

🎯 Speaking out · four truths and a lie
fours
Why: The productive outcome: five present perfect sentences about themselves, four true and one false, and a group that has to catch the lie. Nobody can play without the tense.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5 p.77 and the cooler from TB p.150, run as one productive stage

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. First, ex.5: Add two questions of your own to the ones on the page. Drill them all before anybody speaks — and drill the short answers too, because Yes, I have is what makes the exchange sound English.
  2. In pairs, 5 minutes: ask and answer, and every YES must come with one extra piece of information. Model it: Have you ever camped under the stars? — Yes, I have. I camped with my friends in a forest last year.
  3. Feedback round, 2 minutes, in the third person: Maria's never kayaked down a river but she's always wanted to climb Mount Elbrus.
  4. Then the lie detector, 8 minutes, in fours: five present perfect sentences about yourself, four true and one false. They take turns; the others guess the lie.
  5. Circulate with the correction sheet and log errors silently. Nothing is corrected out loud during this stage.
  6. Close the loop: three of the logged sentences on the board, no names, thirty seconds to repair.
📺 On screen: the drilled questions on the board (Backup: none needed — the questions are in the book on p.77 and the board carries the two extra ones)
👦 Students do: ask, answer with extra information, then hunt the lie. Expect the extra-information rule to double the amount of language. Typical error under pressure: the short answer collapses to yes — which is exactly the habit the exam punishes, so it is worth one reminder

Structure
Have you ever climbed a tree? · I've never camped in the winter. · My mum has visited a safari park. · These are the best apples I've ever picked.
Form
have / has + past participle. Regular = past simple form, with the same spelling rules (travel → travelled, worry → worried). Irregular = the third column, and it has to be learnt.
Pron. watch
‑ed is an extra syllable ONLY after /t/ or /d/: recorded = three syllables, camped = one. Inside the one-syllable group, ‑ed is /d/ after a voiced sound (opened) and /t/ after an unvoiced one (walked).
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Did you walk home from school yesterday?» — am I asking about a specific time? → yes, so past simple. «Have you ever walked home?» — a specific time? → no, any time in your life. That is the whole rule.
«I haven't never been to Moscow» — what's wrong? → one negative is enough in English: I've never been to Moscow.
«Have you ever saw a whale?» — correct it. → seen. After have comes the third form, not the second.
«I have seen this film yesterday» — why not? → because yesterday names the time. Name the time and English switches to the past simple.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
