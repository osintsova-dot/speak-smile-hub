🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Describe a place so that someone else can picture it — twelve adjectives sorted into five kinds, and a written description that does not repeat the same noun five times.
What is genuinely hard
Two different things. In listening, all three pictures are mentioned in the recording, so recognising a word is worth nothing. In writing, the problem is not vocabulary but repetition: the house … the house … the house, because pronouns feel risky.
Typical error to expect
A word from the option was said in the recording → they tick it. A matching word is not a correct answer; meaning decides [exam report]
In the house was two rooms instead of There were two rooms — the dropped there, straight out of Russian word order [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The adjectives are not given as a list of twelve but as five columns — opinion, size, temperature, sound, light — because a system of five is learnable and a list of twelve is not, and because the columns are exactly what the writing stage needs later. The reading comes first and supplies them in context: students find the words in the article rather than meeting them cold. The listening is placed at the middle of the lesson, at 42′, when attention is highest and before the writing tires them; it is also the one stage where the strategy matters more than the score, so it gets a second play and a stated rule. «Make it cosy» sits between the listening and the writing as the bridge: the adjectives have to be used to argue for something before they are used to describe something. The writing stage is twenty minutes and ends inside the lesson, with a partner check on one thing only — pronouns — so the feedback is usable instead of general.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Describe it so well that I can draw it. Twelve words, five kinds — what you think of it, how big, how warm, how quiet, how light. And one rule for the writing: you may not say the house twice in a row. Use it. That is what pronouns are for.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a clinic on three past tense sentences and the bridge into the spread

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. A clinic on the stories you collected: three sentences on the board with no names attached — While I had breakfast, the phone rang. · I was leaving the house when you called me and I answered. · When I was opening the cupboard, there was no food. Thirty seconds — fix them on your board.
  3. Check together against the two timelines, which are still on the board from yesterday. Ninety seconds, no lecture.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Yesterday you told me what happened. Today you tell me what it LOOKS like — page twenty-six.
📺 On screen: the three sentences and yesterday's two timelines on the board — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair the three sentences on mini-whiteboards. Expect the first to be fixed by everybody. Typical error: they rewrite sentence 3 in the continuous throughout — point at the timeline

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim: describe a place so precisely that it can be drawn

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Five words on the board in a column and nothing else: OPINION · SIZE · TEMPERATURE · SOUND · LIGHT.
  2. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: twelve adjectives, but only five kinds — and a written description at the end of the lesson.
  3. Set the bar: By the end you write six sentences about your home, and you may not repeat the same noun twice in a row.
  4. Name the listening rule in advance because it saves ten minutes later: In the listening, you will hear ALL three pictures. Only one of them answers the question.

👦 Students do: read the five words and listen. Expect the fifth line to be met with suspicion. Typical problem — they hear «six sentences» as «six lines»: show them the model text on p.27 for five seconds

8–22′
Reading · strange houses
solo → pairs
Why: Four unusual homes: gist matching first, then eight detail questions. The text is also the source of the adjectives, so the words arrive inside description, not as a list.

WHAT WE DO: «Strange Houses» on p.26: photos to paragraphs first, then eight detail questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open, one minute on the photos: SB p.26: four unusual homes — a plane-shaped house in Lebanon, a house cut into rock in Mexico, a tiny house on a rock in the middle of the river Drina in Serbia (built in 1968 by swimmers), and a Japanese football-shaped house from 2006 with 32 sides standing on four legs.
  2. ex.1 — What do you think they are like INSIDE? Twenty seconds in pairs, no reading yet.
  3. ex.2 against the clock: Two minutes. Match photos A–D to the four paragraphs. Key: 1 A · 2 D · 3 B · 4 C
  4. ex.3 — eight questions, 7 minutes. Kagan RallyCoach to check: one answers and points at the line, the other checks. Key: 1 It looks like an aeroplane / an Airbus A380. · 2 Because they keep the house cool in summer. · 3 It is made of rock. · 4 The house is more than 30 years old. · 5 A small house on a rock. · 6 Most people use it in summer, because it's too cold to use it in winter. · 7 It looks like a football. · 8 Because it has big windows.
  5. Two questions from TB p.49 that speed the whole thing up: Which four countries are the houses in? (Lebanon, Mexico, Serbia, Japan) · Which is the newest? (the Japanese one)
📺 On screen: the four photographs and the article on the screen (Backup: everybody reads from the book, and you write the four country names on the board as they are found)
👦 Students do: match, then answer eight questions with the line. Expect the football house to be the favourite. Typical error: question 2 (why small windows matter) — they answer from common sense instead of from the text

Talking points
pairs → class
Why: A short opinion exchange so the lesson is not only mechanics: they use the unit's language to say something they actually think, about a city they can picture.

WHAT WE DO: which of the four houses you would live in, and which you would not

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The three questions off the page: Which of these houses would you like to live in? Which would you NOT? Do you know any other unusual homes?
  2. Two minutes in pairs, then three opinions to the class — with a reason each time.
  3. One minute of background if the class is hungry for it (TB p.49): TB p.49, other unusual homes if you want one more minute of it: the Keret house in Poland is 92–152 cm wide and needs a ladder instead of stairs; House NA in Japan is entirely of glass; the Hobbit House in Wales was built by one family in four months out of natural materials; the Major Oak in Sherwood Forest is a tree of 800–1,000 years with a legend of Robin Hood; 432 Park Avenue in New York is the tallest residential building in the world at 425 metres.
  4. Close it deliberately: Now we need the words to say WHY. That is the bridge into the vocabulary stage.
📺 On screen: the four photographs stay on the screen (Backup: the same photographs are in the book on p.26 — students point in their own copy and you keep the vote on the board)
👦 Students do: give an opinion with a reason. Expect the rock house in Mexico to divide the room. Typical error: I like it because it is good — push for one concrete word

Vocabulary 2 · describing homes
pairs → class
Why: The adjectives are sorted into five columns — opinion, size, temperature, sound, light — so students leave with a system, not with twelve separate words.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.26: find the twelve adjectives in the article and sort them into five columns (track 30)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Concept-check first, TB p.49: very small (tiny) · it looks nice (attractive) · the opposite of bright (dark) · calm (peaceful).
  2. ex.1 — find the words in the article and put them into the five columns, in pairs, 5 minutes. Key: Opinion: attractive, comfortable, cosy, unusual · Size: tiny · Temperature: cold, cool, warm · Sound: peaceful · Light: bright, dark, light
  3. Play track 30 — check and repeat. Drill comfortable separately: three syllables, /ˈkʌmftəbl/, not four.
  4. The sorting question that settles every argument: is this what you THINK, or a fact you could measure?
  5. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with adjective cards, 4 minutes: the partner must name the column, not the translation.
📺 On screen: track 30 in the whiteboard app with the five-column table on the screen (Backup if the board is down: draw the five columns in chalk and read the twelve words aloud yourself twice; the Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards are printed in advance)
👦 Students do: find, sort and trade. Expect cool to be put in opinion by half the class — it is the useful mistake of the stage. Typical error: light and bright treated as the same word

WHAT WE DO: 90 seconds of movement before the listening

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. I say an adjective — you show me the column. Opinion: hand on your heart. Size: hands apart. Temperature: fan yourself. Sound: finger on your lips. Light: hands up. Eight words, fast: tiny, cosy, peaceful, warm, dark, unusual, cool, bright.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: show the five columns with their bodies. Expect the sorting to become automatic in ninety seconds. Typical problem: they hesitate on unusual — say it twice

🎯 Listening out · five conversations
solo → pairs
Why: The productive listening outcome: five short exchanges where all three pictures are mentioned and only one answers the question. The strategy, not the answers, is the point.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 p.27 (track 31): five short conversations, three pictures each, played twice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the format in one sentence: Five short conversations, five questions. Each question offers three PICTURES and only one of them answers it. The recording is played twice.
  2. Predict before you play, question by question (TB p.50): TB p.50, before you play it, question by question: «What can you see in the three pictures?» (a door with a number) · «Which house has no stairs?» (B) · «Which time is latest?» (C) · «What two ways can you say 3.50?» (three fifty, ten to four) · «What is Sarah doing in picture C?» (making a cake).
  3. First play — answers in pencil, nothing said out loud. Do not compare. Do not speak.
  4. State the rule before the second play: TB p.50: all three pictures WILL be mentioned in the recording — that is how the task is built. Listen for meaning, and listen right to the end: the answer can come at the beginning, in the middle or in the last sentence.
  5. Second play — change what you want to change. Kagan Numbered Heads Together to collect: Question three — number ones, boards up!
  6. Key (TB p.50): 1 B · 2 A · 3 B · 4 A · 5 C
  7. Then, and only then, ask the question that teaches: Question one — why is it not A? What did you HEAR that made you want A?
📺 On screen: track 31 in the whiteboard app with the fifteen pictures on the screen (Backup if the board is down: read the five conversations aloud from the audioscript on TB p.288, twice, taking both voices — the pictures are in the book on p.27)
👦 Students do: listen twice and choose by meaning. Expect 3–4 of 5 after the first play and 4–5 after the second. Typical error: question 1 — they hear «number five» and tick it, though Maria only VISITS number 5

54–64′
Make it cosy
groups of 3–4
Why: A design task in groups: the new adjectives have to be used to argue for something, which is the difference between knowing a word and owning it.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2–3 p.27: choose one of the strange houses and make it comfortable — then sell the idea to the class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.2 first, 3 minutes in pairs: Tell your partner about your home. Use the new adjectives. This is the rehearsal, not the task.
  2. ex.3 in groups: Choose one of the four strange houses. How do you make it comfortable and cosy? You have five minutes and you have to convince us.
  3. Each group gets 40 seconds to present. One condition: Three adjectives from the board, minimum.
  4. Mixed ability (TB p.50): pair a stronger student with one who needs support — the stronger one explains WHY the design works, the other says WHERE things are.
  5. Vote at the end, in one word each: which house would you now live in?
📺 On screen: the five-column table stays on the board and the four photographs stay on the screen (Backup: everything happens from the book and the columns in chalk — this stage needs no technology at all)
👦 Students do: design in groups and present for 40 seconds. Expect the rock house to attract the best ideas. Typical error: the presentation turns into one student talking — the «where things are» role fixes it

Writing · a description of a home
solo → pairs
Why: Writing built on a model, a pronoun rule and a plan, started in class so nobody takes an empty page home.

WHAT WE DO: the Prepare to write block on p.27: the model, the pronoun rule, the plan, then sixty to eighty words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. GET READY, 4 minutes: read Fernanda's description and find what the pronouns replace. The model text, SB p.27: Hi, I'm Fernanda. I live with my family in an apartment in São Paulo, Brazil. It's on the ninth floor of a big block near the city centre. It's got a kitchen, a living room, a bathroom and two bedrooms. I share one of the bedrooms with my sister, Luiza. She's 15. I'd like my own bedroom, but it's OK sharing with Luiza. We like the same things and we enjoy talking at night.
  2. Key: Fernanda lives in São Paulo; the family's apartment has five rooms. · It = the apartment · She = Luiza · We = Luiza and Fernanda · The house = It / it · Helen and Francisco = They
  3. Then the second half of GET READY: rewrite David's paragraph replacing the underlined nouns with pronouns. Three minutes, checked in pairs.
  4. PLAN, 3 minutes of notes only: PLAN — where your home is, what kind of home it is, who lives there, how many rooms, whether you share a bedroom;
  5. WRITE, 8 minutes, silent: 60–80 words. The rule of the stage on the board: You may not use the same noun twice in a row.
  6. IMPROVE, 2 minutes: pairs swap and check ONE thing — the pronouns. Not spelling, not tenses. Pronouns.
  7. Show the model answer only at the end: Model answer, TB p.50: I live in a small apartment in Granada, Spain. It's cosy and very comfortable. There is a kitchen, a bathroom, a living room, two bedrooms and a balcony. I share a bedroom with my sister, Gabriela. She's very tidy but I'm not!
📺 On screen: the model text and the pronoun rule on the screen (Backup: both are printed in the book on p.27 — put the one rule «no noun twice in a row» on the board and work from the paper page)
👦 Students do: read the model, rewrite a paragraph with pronouns, then write their own. Expect 60–80 words from most of them in eight minutes. Typical error: the pronoun is used before the noun it replaces — the partner check catches it

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can describe a home with twelve adjectives and write a description of their own home using pronouns instead of repeating nouns.
  • Subsidiary. Reading four descriptions for gist and detail (SB p.26 ex.2–3); five three-option listening items (track 31); speaking in groups to redesign an unusual home.
  • Personal. In the listening, do not give the answers after the first play. And in the writing feedback, correct one thing only — the pronouns. Everything else goes on the correction sheet.
By the end, students can…
  • sort twelve adjectives into opinion, size, temperature, sound and light
  • answer a three-option listening question by meaning, not by a matching word
  • argue for a design choice using the new adjectives
  • write a description of their home with pronouns instead of repeated nouns

Structure
It looks like a football. · It's very bright inside because it has big windows. · There is a kitchen, a bathroom and two bedrooms. · I share a bedroom with my sister. She's 15.
Form
adjectives go before the noun (a tiny house) or after be (the house is tiny); pronouns replace a noun already mentioned — it, she, we, they.
Pron. watch
comfortable /ˈkʌmftəbl/ — three syllables, not four · cosy /ˈkəʊzi/ · unusual /ʌnˈjuːʒuəl/ — stress on the second syllable.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«All three pictures were mentioned in the recording» — is that a mistake in the task? → no, that is how it is built. Only one of them ANSWERS the question.
«Tiny» and «small» — which is smaller? → tiny. It is size, not opinion, so it goes in the second column.
«The house is new and the house has two bedrooms» — what is wrong? → nothing grammatically, everything stylistically: the second the house should be it.

Assumptions: SB p.26–27, syllabus lesson 13. The home vocabulary from the last lesson is warm and this lesson depends on it. The five-column idea is new but intuitive; the pronoun rule is known passively and almost never used in writing. The listening format (three pictures, one answer) is met here for the first time this year.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
They tick a picture on the first word they recogniseState the rule before the first play and keep it: Listen to the end. The answer can be in the last sentence. And never give the key after the first play — the second one is where the marks are.
cold, cool, warm all land in the opinion columnOne question sorts it: is this what you THINK about it, or is it a fact you could measure? Temperature can be measured; cosy cannot.
The description turns into a list of roomsGive the shape of the model text out loud: where it is, what kind of home, who lives there, how many rooms, who you share with, one sentence of opinion. Six sentences, not a list.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
