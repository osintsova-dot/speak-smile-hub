🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Say prices, weights and measures out loud in English, and write a short email that answers every one of the three things it was asked.
What is genuinely hard
Numbers said aloud are a separate skill from numbers written down: £19.99 is «nineteen pounds ninety-nine», and 1.65 m is «one metre sixty-five». And in the writing, the marks are lost not on grammar but on omission — one of the three things simply never gets written about.
Typical error to expect
One of the three questions in the email is simply never answered. In the Russian writing task that alone drops the first criterion, and a zero there cancels the whole piece of work [exam report]
five pounds written five pound, and moneys — money is uncountable and the plural of a unit is not [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The third lesson of the unit is the skills lesson, and it is built back to front on purpose: everything in it feeds the twenty-two minutes of writing at the end. The reading opens it because pocket money is the one topic on which every teenager has both an opinion and a grievance, and because the figures in it are the reason the vocabulary stage exists. The measurement vocabulary is then taught with the numbers SAID rather than written — the recording exists precisely because nineteen pounds ninety-nine is a thing you have to hear before you can produce it. The listening is placed immediately after, because it is the same skill under time pressure: two plays, two different jobs, the second one entirely numbers. And the writing runs the book's four beats with the punctuation dictation as its own stage, because in 2026 punctuation is counted separately in the Russian writing paper and because an eight-minute dictation of symbols is the cheapest mark in the course.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Today, money — and the two things that actually lose marks. One: saying a price out loud, because £19.99 is four words, not two. Two: answering all three questions in an email. The best English on the page is worth nothing if it answers only two of them.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: the present perfect revised through food, and the topic of spending opened

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind, and it is the book's own warmer (TB p.161). Write down everything you have eaten today. Then in pairs ask each other present perfect questions to find out whether you have eaten the same things — Have you had any chocolate? Compare the lists: who is the healthiest and who has eaten the most?
  3. Two answers to the class, no more. It revises the tense and it opens the topic of spending in one move.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page eighty-two. Money. And by the end of the lesson you write an email that answers three questions — all three.
📺 On screen: the instruction written on the board before the lesson — chalk only, nothing to fail and no backup needed
👦 Students do: list, ask, compare. Expect the «who has eaten the most» question to produce the laugh that starts the lesson. Typical error: Have you eat… — repeat it back correctly and move on

8–20′
Reading · pocket money
solo → pairs
Why: An article about money in other countries, which is the one topic on which every teenager in the room has both an opinion and a grievance.

WHAT WE DO: the article on p.82: ex.1–3 (track 108)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. SB p.82: an article about pocket money round the world — how much teenagers get, where they get the most, what they have to do for it and what their parents want them to do with it. The photographs show teenagers saving money and doing housework.
  2. ex.1 — point at your own pocket and ask What is this? Then the questions in pairs, 3 minutes. Say clearly before they start that question 3 can be skipped.
  3. ex.2 — What are the teenagers in the photographs doing? (saving money, doing housework). Then match the five questions to the paragraphs, 4 minutes. Key: 1 e · 2 a · 3 b · 4 d · 5 c
  4. TB p.161: «How much pocket money does a Spanish teenager get every month?» (about €36 — €9 × 4) and «Why do teenagers buy clothes online?» (it's cheaper). Have them convert the figures into roubles as they read — that is what makes the numbers mean anything. ⚠️ Question 3 of ex.1 asks how much pocket money THEY get; TB p.161 warns that students may feel uncomfortable, so say out loud that it can be skipped.
  5. ex.3 — the six questions, 4 minutes, then checked with a partner. Key: 1 Girls don't get as much as boys. · 2 They get the most in the US. · 3 It is called an allowance. · 4 They have to do jobs around the house. · 5 They have part-time jobs. · 6 They want them to save it to spend on something big in the future, or not spend it at all.
  6. Play track 108 while they follow with a finger — the article is recorded and it carries the pronunciation of every currency word in it.
  7. Kagan Numbered Heads Together for the check: answer plus the line that proves it.
📺 On screen: track 108 in the whiteboard app (Backup with no sound: read the article aloud yourself while they follow — it is printed on SB p.82 and the stage does not depend on the recording)
👦 Students do: read, match, answer, follow the recording. Expect the conversion into roubles to be the moment the figures start meaning something. Typical error: answering question 1 from opinion rather than from the text

Talking points · pocket money
fours
Why: A question with a genuine disagreement in it — why boys often get more — and one that sends them home with something to ask their parents.

WHAT WE DO: the Talking points questions on p.82 — with a real disagreement in them

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three questions, in fours, 5 minutes: Why do you think boys often get more pocket money than girls? · Do you think your parents got pocket money? What did they spend it on? · Should teenagers have to do jobs at home to get pocket money?
  2. One rule that keeps it in English: every answer must contain a number.
  3. TB p.161: fast finishers think of three things they would like to save their pocket money for — a new phone, a holiday by the sea — and calculate how many weeks it would take them.
  4. Round-up: one sentence per group, and the second question becomes a homework question if nobody knows the answer — ask your parents and tell us next lesson. TB p.161 suggests exactly that.

👦 Students do: discuss in fours, then report. Expect the first question to produce genuine indignation, which is the best possible fuel for speaking. Typical problem: the numbers get said in Russian — the number rule is what stops it

28–40′
Vocabulary 2 · measurement and money
class → pairs
Why: Numbers said out loud are a skill of their own. Twelve symbols and four noun of noun phrases turn a page of figures into language.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–4 p.83: twelve symbols, the amounts said aloud (track 109), and «a slice of»

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed. Hold up a ruler: What is this? What is it for? How? (a ruler; for measuring things; in centimetres and millimetres). Ask for more words like these.
  2. Books open. ex.1 — complete the four sentences. Key: 1 kilograms or grams · 2 litres or millilitres · 3 dollars and cents, euros and cents, and pounds and pence · 4 centimetres, metres and kilometres
  3. TB p.161: American English writes meter, milliliter, kilometer with ‑er rather than ‑re, and kilogram is usually shortened to kilo. The British write and say p, not pence, in a price. Background worth thirty seconds: Britain still uses imperial units alongside metric — distances in miles (1 mile = 1.6 km) and weight in stones (1 stone = 6.4 kg) — and the pound is the oldest currency still in use, going back to the eighth century. There are about 180 currencies in the world and around 22 of them are called dollars.
  4. ex.2 — write four prices on the board and have them say them aloud BEFORE the recording. Then in pairs. Then play track 109 to check and repeat. Key: Two hundred and sixty grams · Fifteen euros and thirty-four cents · Seven hundred millilitres · Fifty-five centimetres · Two point five litres or two and a half litres · Thirty-seven pence · Nineteen pounds, ninety-nine pence · Six kilograms · Sixty-two cents · Twenty-seven dollars · One metre sixty-five centimetres or one point six five metres
  5. Then the question that makes it real (TB p.161): How much is a cinema ticket? How far is the nearest airport? How many litres of water do you drink a day? How much is a pound in our money?
  6. ex.3 — a loaf of bread, a pair of trousers, and then the four. Key: 1 a slice of pizza · 2 a pair of sunglasses · 3 a set of cups · 4 a variety of drinks. ex.4: 1 a pair of · 2 a variety of · 3 a slice of · 4 a set of
  7. TB p.162: ask weaker students whether each word is followed by a singular, a plural or an uncountable noun. slice (a thin section) + uncountable · bit (a small part) + uncountable · pair (two things, or something with two parts) + plural · set (a group of similar things) + plural · variety (different things) + plural. Then let them build more: set + books, clothes, friends · bit + time, bread, money · slice + bread, cheese · pair + trousers, shoes.
  8. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with symbol cards, 3 minutes: the symbol on one side, the word on the other.
📺 On screen: track 109 in the whiteboard app (Backup with no sound: say the eleven amounts yourself, twice — for numbers said aloud a live voice is better, because you can repeat any one of them on demand. The list is printed on SB p.83)
👦 Students do: say prices aloud before and after the recording, then sort the four phrases. Expect £19.99 and 1.65 m to be the two that need three attempts each. Typical error: nineteen point ninety-nine

42–54′
🎯 Listening out · the birthday picnic
solo → pairs
Why: The productive listening outcome: two plays and two different jobs — first what they have got, then how much of it. Nobody drowns and everybody has to hear a number.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2–3 p.83 (track 110): two plays, two different jobs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.162: line the class up by birthday, January on the left and December on the right, by asking one another When's your birthday? Then pairs discuss what they like to eat on their birthdays. Two minutes, and it puts the whole topic in the air.
  2. SB p.83, track 110: David and Lana are getting ready for a birthday picnic for Pia and going through what they have already bought and what they still need.
  3. TB p.162: on the first play they only TICK what David and Lana have already got — the ticks go to the left of the words, because the quantities are written on the right in the second play. Two plays, two jobs, and nobody drowns.
  4. Play track 110. ex.2 first — tick only. Key: They have already got pizzas, crisps, apples and a blanket. (David has got her present but Lana hasn't yet.)
  5. Play track 110 again. ex.3 — the quantities. Key: pizzas – 3 = 24 slices · crisps – 4 big bags · apples – 1.5 kilos · lemonade – 3 litres · fruit juice – 2 litres · water – 1 litre
  6. Kagan RallyCoach for the check: A gives a quantity, B coaches, then swap.
  7. ex.4 — read the conversation in pairs and answer the two questions. Key: 1 I could …, I can … if you like · 2 If not. The language point in ex.4 is small and genuinely useful: if not (and the positive if so) saves repeating a whole clause. We can meet at 6 p.m. If we can't meet at 6 p.m., 7 p.m. will be all right. → If not, 7 p.m. will be all right.
  8. ex.5 if there is a minute: pairs read the conversation aloud, then write two conversations of their own and practise them.
📺 On screen: track 110 in the whiteboard app (Backup with no sound: the full audioscript is on TB p.299 — read the conversation aloud yourself in two voices, twice, at natural speed. The two-job structure is what makes it work, not the recording)
👦 Students do: tick on the first play, write quantities on the second, then read the conversation. Expect 1.5 kilos and 24 slices to be the two that need the second play. Typical error: they try to do both jobs on the first play — say the two-plays rule before you start

54–62′
Writing · GET READY · punctuation
class → pairs
Why: Punctuation is dictated as symbols and then used to repair a real note. It is eight minutes that pays in both exams, because in 2026 the apostrophe is counted as punctuation in the Russian writing paper.

WHAT WE DO: the punctuation dictation and the repaired note on p.83

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. GET READY, and it is the stage that earns marks in both exams: dictate a list of punctuation marks as SYMBOLS — full stop, capital letter, apostrophe, question mark — and have them write the symbols down. Then they correct the unpunctuated note and compare with a partner before you check it.
  2. Dictate the marks as symbols: full stop, capital letter, apostrophe, question mark, comma, exclamation mark. They write the SYMBOL, not the word. Two minutes.
  3. Then the note on p.83 — correct it alone, 3 minutes, then compare with a partner before you check as a class. Key: Dear Dad, Lana and I need to get some things for Pia's party. Please can you take us to the supermarket this morning? Thanks! See you later. David (TB p.162)
  4. Point at the apostrophe in Pia's and say the one sentence that makes it matter: in the writing paper this counts as punctuation, not as grammar.
  5. Then read out the model, so the shape of the finished thing is heard before they plan: Model answer, TB p.162: Hi Sam, I have just bought a set of Lego for my sister because she loves it. I got it from the supermarket near my house. Best, Cecilia — note that it uses the grammar of this unit in its first sentence.
📺 On screen: the unpunctuated note on screen so the corrections can be made in front of them (Backup: it is printed on SB p.83 — they correct it in the book in pencil and one student reads the corrected version aloud)
👦 Students do: write the symbols, repair the note, compare. Expect the apostrophe and the question mark to be the two that go missing. Typical error: capital letters restored and commas ignored

62–84′
Writing · an email
solo → pairs
Why: Writing built on a model and a plan, started and finished in class, and checked against one question only: are all three things there?

WHAT WE DO: PLAN → WRITE → IMPROVE on p.83: an email of 25 words or more with three content points

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. A short email to a friend. You get a message with three things to write about, and you answer all three. 25 words or more. Read the task with them and underline the three things it asks for — underline them in the book, in pencil, before anything is written.
  2. PLAN: read the task and make notes — three notes, one per content point, and nothing else. 3 minutes, notes only.
  3. WRITE: expand the notes into sentences. TB p.162 gives the one instruction that prevents half the mistakes — look at the TENSES in the prompts, because the reply normally uses the same ones. 8 minutes of silent writing, timer visible. Remind them once to use the grammar of the unit — I have just bought… — as the model answer does.
  4. IMPROVE: swap papers and check two things only — are all three content points there, and is the punctuation right. Then look back at the email written on SB p.61 in block 5 and see whether this one is better. Kagan RallyCoach for the swap: the partner's only job is to put three ticks on the paper. If a tick is missing, the paper goes back for two minutes and nothing is said about the English.
  5. Then the second check, and only the second: punctuation. Full stops, capitals, apostrophes, question marks.
  6. Where marks go, and it is almost never grammar: one of the three points is simply not written about. In the Russian version that alone drops the first criterion, and a zero there cancels the whole piece of work.
  7. Log the errors on your correction sheet; the clinic is at the start of the next lesson — which is the review lesson, so it lands exactly where it is needed.
📺 On screen: the three ticks from 5–8′ still on the board and the model answer on screen (Backup: the model is on TB p.162 and can be read aloud, and the three ticks are chalk — this stage needs no technology at all)
👦 Students do: underline three things, plan, write, check a partner's. Expect two papers in the class to come back for a missing tick, and expect those two students to remember it for the rest of the year. Typical error: the third point answered in half a clause — the tick is only given for a sentence

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
How do you say £19.99? → nineteen pounds, ninety-nine. Four words, and the British usually leave out pence altogether.
A slice of — is the next word one thing or many? → uncountable: a slice of bread, of pizza, of cheese. A pair of takes a plural.
The email asks three things. You have written a beautiful answer to two of them. What is the mark? → the first criterion is gone, and in the Russian version a zero there cancels the whole piece of work.
Where does the apostrophe go in Pia's party? → before the s. It is Pia's — one person, one party, and in 2026 that mark is counted as punctuation.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
They read £19.99 as «nineteen point ninety-nine»Drill five prices before the recording and five after it. It is a spoken form, so it is learnt by saying it, not by being told.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
