🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Read five short authentic texts and decide why each one was written (B1 Reading Part 1), and match five people to eight short texts by finding all three of each person's requirements (B1 Reading Part 2).
Where it sits
The closing lesson of Block 1 (Units 1 «Going shopping» + 2 «Best friends forever»). The block test was last lesson and the papers come back today, so the block's language is revised in the warm-up and then the class moves to the first exam page of the year.
What is genuinely hard
Both parts are won and lost on the same reflex, and it is not a language reflex: the option that repeats a word from the text feels right and is usually the trap. In Part 2 the designed distractor is the text that satisfies two of the three requirements — which is exactly the one a fast reader chooses.
Typical error to expect
Choosing the option that shares a word with the text — text 1 says price and so does option B, and that is precisely why B is wrong. The Teacher's Book flags this trap by name.
Stopping at the second match — in Part 2 a text that covers two of the three points is designed to be attractive.
Why the lesson is built this way
Part 1 is taught as a question about PURPOSE, not vocabulary: what kind of text, who wrote it, who reads it. Part 2 is taught as a physical procedure — underline three things, then hunt for a text that has all three — demonstrated once by you on George, then done by them. The productive stage turns the task inside out: students write their own three-requirement profile and the class matches it, which is the fastest way to understand how the distractors are built.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Every sign, note and message on this page was written for a reason. The exam never asks what it says — it asks WHY it was written. And in the second task, a website that matches two of your three wishes is not your website. It's the trap.”

0–6′
Opening · papers back + block-1 retrieval sprint
door → desks
Why: The block test is behind them and the papers come back today. A short low-stakes recall of the block's language pulls Units 1–2 back into working memory the moment they sit — the block is revised, and today's exam input lands on something solid.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, marked papers back with one specific line of feedback, then a 3-minute recall race over the whole block

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 1.5 min — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what's the best thing about today so far? … Good. Sit down and open a clean page. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 1.5 min — the papers come back. Hand back the block test with the section totals written on each paper. Say one specific positive sentence about the class (The personality adjectives section was the strongest — twenty-six of you spelled «thoughtful» correctly) and one specific instruction for block 2.
  3. 3 min — block-1 retrieval sprint (books closed). A fast, no-marks recall over both units, mixing three kinds of question so every student can start somewhere: ① free recall — On your page, write four shopping words and four personality adjectives — sixty seconds, go. ② cued recall — Determiners: much or many? «___ discounts», «___ money». -ing form: «I don't mind ___ (wait).» ③ recognition — read three sentences, thumbs up if correct, thumbs down if not («There weren't much games.» / «I'm keen on making new friends.» / «He is very unreliable.»).
  4. Take three answers aloud, correct on the board in ten seconds each, then change gear: Books closed on the block. Page one hundred and twenty — the exam section at the back. Today, the two shortest tasks in the paper.
📺 On screen: the class results in two lines and the six recall prompts — no gadgets, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: look at their own paper with a job (find one error you can explain), then all write at once in the retrieval sprint — nobody waits, nobody watches. Every student produces block-1 language before the exam page opens
📚 Method note (retrieval-practice-generator, evidence: Roediger & Butler 2011; Rowland 2014): mixing free recall → cued recall → recognition lets weaker students enter on recognition while stronger ones free-recall — low stakes, everyone active, and it spaces Units 1–2 one lesson after the test.

9–20′
Part 1 · what is this text FOR?
class → pairs
Why: Part 1 is a PURPOSE question wearing a vocabulary disguise. If students first name the purpose of each text in one word, the three answer options stop being a language puzzle and become a simple check.

WHAT WE DO: the exam information, the example (0), and the purpose of each of the five texts

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the exam information box aloud: Reading Part 1 — five short texts (notes, messages, signs, postcards, emails, labels) with three options each. The question is always about PURPOSE: why was this written?
  2. ex.1: the example (0), Jenny & Max's text message. Think-Pair-Share: what kind of text is this, who are Jenny and Max, and — the exam's real question — why has Max sent it?
  3. Show how the wrong options are built, on the board: A (bring sports equipment) uses real words from the text; B (a new arrangement) contradicts «like we said». Only C survives → to remind Jenny about their plans.
  4. Now the purpose sweep, in pairs, three minutes: read texts 1–5 and write ONE word for the purpose of each — do not choose A, B or C yet.
  5. Collect the five purposes on the board (advert · competition info · a favour · holiday news · a delivery). This is the step students skip and the step that makes the options easy.
📺 On screen: the example text with its three options visible. Backup: the printed page works identically — mark the traps on the board instead
👦 Students do: read, decide the purpose in one word, and say why the other two options fail

20–34′
Part 1 · the task, then the autopsy
individual → pairs → class
Why: The first timed exam task of the year has to be done alone and against a clock — and then dissected, because a wrong option that shares a word with the text teaches more than a right one.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 under a clock, then every wrong option explained

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the exam tip aloud: for each text ask what kind of text it is, who wrote it, who will read it. A word that appears in both the text and an option (price in text 1) is a trap, not a clue.
  2. Five minutes, silent, individual. Clock on the board. Say it out loud: This is the first timed exam task of the year — five minutes is roughly what the real paper allows.
  3. Pairs compare and, where they disagree, they go back to the text rather than to each other's confidence.
  4. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together. For every item, two questions: which words gave you the answer, and why is the option next to it wrong?
  5. Spend the longest on text 1 (the sale): B repeats price from the text and is still wrong, because a shop that excludes designer bags does not reduce everything. Name the trap and write it on the board: SHARED WORD ≠ RIGHT ANSWER. (Key: 1 A · 2 C · 3 C · 4 B · 5 C.)
📺 On screen: the timer and the trap sentence on the board. Backup: none needed — book and board only
👦 Students do: work alone against the clock, then justify. Every student answers at least once because the check is by number

34–38′
Move break · read the room
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new skill. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer built out of the same skill — everybody on their feet

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Five short texts are stuck around the walls (a sign, a note, a text message, a poster, a label — take them from real English packaging or write them in two minutes before the lesson).
  2. Everyone walks. At each text they say ONE sentence to whoever is next to them: This was written to warn people / to sell something / to ask a favour.
  3. Ninety seconds of walking, then freeze and take three answers aloud.
  4. Sit them down on the last one: That's the whole of Part 1. Now the harder one.
📺 On screen: five short texts on the walls — printed or handwritten in advance, so nothing can fail
👦 Students do: walk, read, say a purpose sentence to a neighbour. Everyone speaks five times in four minutes

38–48′
Part 2 · underline three things
class → pairs
Why: Part 2 collapses if students read the eight texts first. Fixing the order of work — profiles first, three underlines each, then the texts — is the whole skill, so it is modelled in public once before anyone starts.

WHAT WE DO: the exam information, the tip, and the whole method demonstrated on George

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the exam information aloud: five people with three requirements each, eight short texts (here: eight music websites A–H). Three texts are not needed.
  2. Then the tip, which IS the method: underline the THREE things the person wants, then find the text that covers all three. A text that matches two of the three is the designed trap.
  3. Demonstrate on George in public, on the board. Underline his three requirements one at a time and number them 1, 2, 3: band members from the past talking about their work · albums that are hard to find · reliable reviews by experts.
  4. Then hunt: read website G (MUSIC FREAKS) aloud and match each underline to a phrase in it — reviews by the best music journalists (= experts), video interviews with musicians performing twenty years ago (= from the past), links to shops selling rare CDs (= hard to find). All three → G.
  5. Now show the trap on purpose: website C also has reviews and unusual music — two things — but no interviews with musicians from the past. Say the rule: Two out of three is the trap, not the answer.
  6. Pairs now underline three things in profiles 2–5 only (Ella · Bojing · Saanvi · Rob). Nothing else yet — just the underlining. Check it before they read a single website.
📺 On screen: George's profile enlarged with the three underlines added live. Backup: copy his three requirements onto the board and underline them there — same demonstration
👦 Students do: watch one worked example, then underline in four profiles and have the underlining checked before reading any website
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE task 10 (matching texts to headings / to people). Underlining three requirements and hunting for the text that covers all three is the same procedure, whichever exam prints it.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: after the book task, put up one Russian-format set (four short texts, five headings) for five minutes — it replaces two minutes of the strategy-card stage and three of the write-your-own stage.

48–66′
Part 2 · the task in exam conditions
individual → pairs
Why: The exam is done alone and under time; the evidence round afterwards is where the learning is — no answer counts without the three matching phrases, which kills the fast-and-wrong reflex.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 (2–5) timed, then checked with evidence for all three points

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Eight minutes, silent, individual, clock visible. Say the honest timing: In the real paper this takes about ten minutes. We're doing it in eight so we have time to check properly.
  2. Then the evidence round, in RallyCoach pairs, six minutes: for each answer, write the three phrases from the website that match the three underlines. No three phrases, no answer.
  3. Check as a class, one person per item, evidence first and letter second (key: 1 G · 2 A · 3 E · 4 F · 5 H). Ella (A) and Saanvi (F) are worth slowing down for — both have a tempting two-out-of-three neighbour.
  4. Ask the class which three websites (B, C, D) were never used and why they existed at all. That question teaches distractor logic better than any explanation.
📺 On screen: the clock on the board. Backup: none needed — book, pen and board only
👦 Students do: work alone under time, then prove every answer three times over with a partner

66–78′
🎯 Speaking out · write the trap yourself
pairs → class
Why: Building a distractor is the fastest way to stop falling for one. Writing a profile and two matching texts turns the reading skill into a production task where everyone speaks and writes.

WHAT WE DO: students write their own three-requirement profile; the class matches it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. In pairs, write a profile of yourselves in the exam's shape: … wants three things: X, Y and Z. Any topic — a phone, a summer camp, a café, a game.
  2. Then write TWO short texts to go with it: one that matches all three, and one that matches only two. That second text is the trap.
  3. Swap with another pair. Read their profile, choose the answer, and — the important part — say which point the trap text is missing.
  4. Take three of the best trap texts and read them to the whole class. Vote on the most convincing.
  5. Close the loop out loud: The person who writes your exam does exactly this, on purpose, every time.
📺 On screen: two or three student traps written up at the end — no gadgets, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write a profile and two texts, then solve another pair's. Everyone produces language and everyone reads for evidence

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
Thumbs up / thumbs down — the recognition round and the wrap-up: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: reading or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of the move break.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
