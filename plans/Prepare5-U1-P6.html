🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Read five short authentic texts and decide why each one was written (B1 Reading Part 1), and match five people to eight short texts by finding all three of each person's requirements (B1 Reading Part 2).
What is genuinely hard
Both parts are won and lost on the same reflex, and it is not a language reflex: the option that repeats a word from the text feels right and is usually the trap. In Part 2 the designed distractor is the text that satisfies two of the three requirements — which is exactly the one a fast reader chooses.
Typical error to expect
Choosing the option that shares a word with the text — text 1 says price and so does option B, and that is precisely why B is wrong. The Teacher's Book flags this trap by name.
Stopping at the second match — in Part 2 a text that covers two of the three points is designed to be attractive.
Why the lesson is built this way
The block test is behind them and the papers come back today, so the lesson opens with three minutes of real feedback and then changes gear completely. Part 1 is taught as a question about PURPOSE, not vocabulary: what kind of text, who wrote it, who reads it. Part 2 is taught as a physical procedure — underline three things, then hunt for a text that has all three — demonstrated once by you on George, then done by them. The productive stage turns the task inside out: students write their own three-requirement profile and the class matches it, which is the fastest way to understand how the distractors are built.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Every sign, note and message on this page was written for a reason. The exam never asks what it says — it asks WHY it was written. And in the second task, a website that matches two of your three wishes is not your website. It's the trap.”

WHAT WE DO: greeting, marked papers back with specific feedback, and the switch into the exam page

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what's the best thing about today so far? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — the papers come back. Hand back both tests with the section totals written on them. Say one specific positive sentence about the class (The adjectives section was the strongest — twenty-six out of twenty-seven of you spelled thoughtful correctly) and one specific instruction for the next block.
  3. Sixty seconds, in pairs: find ONE mistake on your paper that you can explain to your partner. Not all of them — one.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): change gear: Papers away. Page one hundred and twenty. This is the exam section at the back of your book, and today it's the two shortest tasks in the paper.
📺 On screen: the two class results summarised on the board in two lines — no gadgets, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: look at their own paper with a job to do rather than a mark to feel. Explaining one error out loud is worth more than reading twenty red marks

9–20′
Part 1 · what is this text FOR?
class → pairs
Why: Part 1 · what is this text FOR?

WHAT WE DO: the exam information, the example (0), and the purpose of each of the five texts

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the exam information box aloud: Reading Part 1 — five short texts (notes, messages, signs, postcards, emails, labels) with three options each. The question is always about PURPOSE: why was this written?
  2. ex.1: the example. Pairs answer the two questions on the page — what kind of text is this, and who are Jenny and Max? Then the third question, which is the exam's real one: why did Max send it?
  3. Show how the wrong options are built, on the board: option A (bring equipment for him) uses a real word from the text; option B (a new arrangement) contradicts «like we said». Only C survives.
  4. Now the purpose sweep, in pairs, three minutes: read texts 1–5 and write ONE word for the purpose of each — advert, information, favour, holiday news, delivery. Do not choose A, B or C yet.
  5. Collect the five purposes on the board. This is the step students skip and the step that makes the options easy.
📺 On screen: the example text on screen if you have the digital book, with the three options visible. Backup: the printed page works identically — mark the traps on the board instead
👦 Students do: read, decide the purpose, and say why the other two options fail

20–34′
Part 1 · the task, then the autopsy
individual → pairs → class
Why: Part 1 · the task, then the autopsy

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 under a clock, then every wrong option explained

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the exam tip aloud: Read each text and ask three questions: what kind of text is it? who wrote it? who will read it? A word that appears in both the text and an option (price in text 1) is a trap, not a clue.
  2. Five minutes, silent, individual. Clock on the board. This is the first timed exam task of the year — say that, and say that five minutes is roughly what the real paper allows.
  3. Pairs compare and, where they disagree, they go back to the text rather than to each other's confidence.
  4. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together. For every item, two questions: which words gave you the answer, and why is the option next to it wrong?
  5. Spend the longest on text 1 (the sale) — B repeats price from the text and is still wrong, because everything is not true of a shop that excludes designer bags. Name the trap out loud and write it on the board: SHARED WORD ≠ RIGHT ANSWER.
📺 On screen: the timer and the trap sentence on the board. Backup: none needed — book and board only
👦 Students do: work alone against the clock, then justify. Every student answers at least once because the check is by number

34–38′
Move break · read the room
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer built out of the same skill

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Five short texts are stuck around the walls (a sign, a note, a text message, a poster, a label — take them from any real English packaging or write them in two minutes before the lesson).
  2. Everyone walks. At each text they say ONE sentence to whoever is next to them: This was written to warn people / to sell something / to ask a favour.
  3. Ninety seconds of walking, then freeze and take three answers aloud.
  4. Sit them down on the last one: That's the whole of Part 1. Now the harder one.
📺 On screen: five short texts on the walls — printed or handwritten in advance, so nothing can fail
👦 Students do: walk, read, say a purpose sentence to a neighbour. Everyone speaks five times in four minutes

38–48′
Part 2 · underline three things
class → pairs
Why: Part 2 · underline three things

WHAT WE DO: the exam information, the tip, and the whole method demonstrated on George

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the exam information aloud: Reading Part 2 — five people with three requirements each, eight short texts (here: eight music websites). Three texts are not needed.
  2. Then the tip, which IS the method: Underline the THREE things the person wants. Then find the text that covers all three. A text that matches two of the three is the designed trap.
  3. Demonstrate on George in public, on the board. Underline his three requirements one at a time and number them 1, 2, 3.
  4. Then hunt: read website G aloud and match each of the three underlines to a phrase in it. George wants: band members from the past talking about their work · albums that are hard to find · reliable reviews by experts. Website G has all three — reviews by the best music journalists (= experts), video interviews with musicians from twenty years ago, links to shops selling rare CDs (= difficult to find)
  5. Now show the trap deliberately: website C also has reviews and unusual music — two things — but no interviews with musicians from the past. Say the rule: Two out of three is the trap, not the answer.
  6. Pairs now underline three things in profiles 2–5. Nothing else yet — just the underlining. Check it before they read the texts.
📺 On screen: George's profile enlarged on screen with the three underlines added live. Backup: copy his three requirements onto the board and underline them there — same demonstration
👦 Students do: watch one worked example, then underline in four profiles and have the underlining checked before reading a single website
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains EGE task 10 (matching texts to headings and to people). Underlining three requirements and hunting for a text that covers all three is the same procedure, whichever exam prints it.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: after the book task, put up one Russian-format set (four short texts, five headings) and give five minutes. It replaces two minutes of the strategy-card stage and three of the write-your-own stage.

Part 2 · the task in exam conditions
individual → pairs
Why: Part 2 · the task in exam conditions

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 (2–5) timed, then checked with evidence for all three points

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Eight minutes, silent, individual, clock visible. Say the honest timing: In the real paper this task takes about ten minutes. We're doing it in eight so we have time to check properly.
  2. Then the evidence round, in RallyCoach pairs, six minutes: for each answer, write the three phrases from the website that match the three underlines. No three phrases, no answer.
  3. Check as a class, one person per item, evidence first and letter second. Ella (A) and Saanvi (F) are the two worth slowing down for — both have a tempting two-out-of-three neighbour.
  4. Ask the class which three websites were never used and why they existed at all. That question teaches distractor logic better than any explanation.
📺 On screen: the clock on the board. Backup: none needed — book, pen and board only
👦 Students do: work alone under time, then prove every answer three times over with a partner

🎯 Speaking out · write the trap yourself
pairs → class
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · write the trap yourself

WHAT WE DO: students write their own three-requirement profile; the class matches it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. In pairs, write a profile of yourselves in the exam's shape: … wants three things: X, Y and Z. Any topic — a phone, a summer camp, a café, a game.
  2. Then write TWO short texts to go with it: one that matches all three, and one that matches only two. That second text is the trap, and building one is the fastest way to stop falling for one.
  3. Swap with another pair. Read their profile, choose their answer, and — the important part — say which point the trap text is missing.
  4. Take three of the best trap texts and read them to the whole class. Vote on the most convincing.
  5. Close the loop out loud: The person who writes your exam does exactly this, on purpose, every time.
📺 On screen: two or three student traps written up on the board at the end — no gadgets, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write a profile and two texts, then solve another pair's. Everyone produces language and everyone reads for evidence

WHAT WE DO: the two methods written into a card that survives the year

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone writes a strategy card — half an A5 sheet, kept in the book at page 120: PART 1 — what is it? who wrote it? who reads it? Shared word ≠ right answer. PART 2 — underline three. Two out of three is the trap.
  2. Add one line each in their own words: what I personally have to stop doing in these tasks.
  3. Two students read their personal line aloud. Do not comment on them — just let the room hear that everyone has one.
  4. Say where these tasks come back: Part 1 again in Unit 14, Part 2 in Unit 8. Same card, same two methods.
📺 On screen: the two methods still on the board from the frame stage — no gadgets, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write a card they will actually reuse, and name their own weak reflex in writing

Grammar Plus U1 + U2
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can identify the purpose of a short text and choose between three close options, and match people to texts using a three-point underlining method.
  • Subsidiary. Vocabulary of notices and adverts (sale, half price, entries, collect, deliver); reading at speed under a visible clock; justifying an answer with a line reference.
  • Personal. Hand the block test back at the start, with the section totals written on each paper. Say one specific positive sentence about the class result and one specific instruction for block 2.
By the end, students can…
  • say why a note, a sign or an email was written, in one sentence
  • explain why two of the three options are wrong
  • underline exactly three requirements in a person's profile
  • reject a text that matches only two of the three requirements

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Why has Max sent this message?» Am I looking for what he says or what he WANTS? → what he wants. Purpose, not content.
Option B repeats the word price from the text. Does that make it right? → no. Shared words are the trap; shared meaning is the answer.
George wants three things and website E has two of them. Is E the answer? → no. Two out of three is a designed distractor.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students answer from the picture or the first lineEvery answer in this lesson has to be justified by a line of the text, and the justification is asked for BEFORE the letter is accepted: Which words tell you that?
Part 2 turns into thirty minutes of reading eight textsThe method fixes the order of work: the PROFILES first, three underlines each, and only then the texts. Model it in public on George before anyone starts.
The fast readers finish Part 2 in five minutes and are wrongTheir answer sheet is not the end of the task: they have to write, next to each answer, which three phrases in the text match the three underlines. Speed without evidence gets sent back.
The class asks whether this is the Russian examAnswer honestly in one sentence and move on — it is the same skill, differently packaged, and the note at the foot of this plan is where the detail lives. Do not spend lesson time on exam comparison.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 10 (matching texts to headings) and task 11 (the gapped text) — and this is the closest thing in the whole year to a one-for-one match with a Cambridge task.

Reading Part 2 (matching five people to eight texts) and the Russian exam's matching tasks are the same skill wearing different clothes: underline the THREE things the person wants, then find the text that covers all three. The distractor logic is identical too — a text that matches two of the three points is the trap, not the answer.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
