🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name twelve shops — including the ones with an apostrophe, butcher's, chemist's, newsagent's — and say what you can and cannot buy in each.
What is genuinely hard
Two things. The apostrophe, which is invisible in speech and is exactly what the shop names are made of — the shop OF the butcher. And the fact that English divides shops where Russian does not: a chemist's is not a shop for chemicals, and a department store is not a store of departments.
Typical error to expect
butchers, chemists, newsagents — the shop name loses its apostrophe. In 2026 the apostrophe counts as PUNCTUATION in the writing paper, not as grammar [exam report]
five pounds written five pound, and moneys — money is uncountable and the plural of a unit is not [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
This is the first of three lessons on Unit 14, so it does one thing thoroughly instead of three things quickly: the shop vocabulary, and the reading that uses it. The vocabulary stage is split in two on purpose — first the twelve labels on twelve photographs, then a separate twelve minutes attaching things to them, because a shop name is useless until it has goods in it, and because ex.2 and ex.3 are where the class discovers that most things can be bought in three different places. The apostrophe gets its ten seconds inside the vocabulary stage rather than a grammar slot of its own: it is a spelling fact about four words, not a rule. The reading is scanning, not gist — five adverts with opening dates and offers, and finding a date in a text is a genuinely different skill that this course practises nowhere else. And the productive stage writes an advert for a shop our town does not have, which needs the words, a date and an offer, all three straight off the page.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Twelve shops — and four of them have an apostrophe in them. Butcher's means the shop of the butcher, and that little mark is worth a real mark in an exam. Then five adverts, and by the end you write one for a shop this town should have and doesn't.”

WHAT WE DO: the whole of Unit 13 revised standing up, in three minutes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last unit rewind, standing. Run the Teacher's Book warmer, because it revises the whole of Unit 13 in three minutes: TB p.157, and it revises the whole of Unit 13 in five minutes: write the verbs be, break, eat, fly, grow, have, make, meet, ride, send, swim on the board, well spaced out. Take a ball, or screw up some paper. Two teams line up facing the board. The first student in team A throws the ball at the board; the first student in team B has to ask a Have you ever …? question using the verb nearest to where the ball hit — Have you ever sent an email to the wrong person? — and one of team A answers. A point for a correct question and a point for a correct answer, then the teams swap roles.
  3. Keep it to three minutes and two rounds per team. It is a warmer, not a tournament.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the new unit: Books open, page eighty. New unit — shopping and money. Twelve shops, and four of them have a mark in them that costs a mark.

WHAT WE DO: the About you questions on p.80, from a real purchase

Vocabulary 1 · shops
pairs → class
Why: Twelve shops on twelve photographs. The useful thing is not the label but the phrase at the butcher's — which is why the apostrophe gets ten seconds of board time here and not in a grammar lesson.

WHAT WE DO: twelve shops on twelve photos: ex.1 p.80 (track 105) plus a Quiz-Quiz-Trade mingle

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Start from the pictures, not the words (TB p.157): What is in picture A? In picture B? Take three descriptions before anybody matches anything.
  2. ex.1 — match the twelve words to the photos, in pairs, 4 minutes. Key: A sweet shop · B chemist's · C department store · D café · E shoe shop · F supermarket · G bookshop · H clothes shop · I market · J butcher's · K newsagent's · L bakery
  3. Play track 105 — check and repeat chorally. Drill the three that are always mispronounced: BUTCHer's, CHEMist's with a /k/, BAKery.
  4. TB p.157 says it in one line and it is worth ten seconds of board time: butcher's and newsagent's carry an apostrophe and an s, and you will also see chemist's written that way. It is the shop OF the butcher — and in 2026 that apostrophe is counted as punctuation in the Russian writing paper.
  5. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with shop cards, 4 minutes: the photo on one side, the word on the other. Quiz your partner. Coach if he's wrong. Trade cards. Find a new partner!
  6. One question to close: Which of these twelve does our town NOT have?
📺 On screen: track 105 in the whiteboard app with the twelve photos on the screen (Backup if the board is down: read the twelve shops aloud twice yourself and use the photos in the paper book; the Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards are printed in advance and need no technology at all)
👦 Students do: describe, match, repeat, mingle. Expect 7–8 of 12 unaided. Typical error: chemist's pronounced with /tʃ/, and the apostrophe dropped the moment they write rather than speak

Vocabulary practice · what you buy where
pairs → class
Why: The words become usable only when they are attached to things. Two exercises and a mingle turn twelve labels into a working map of a high street.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2–3 p.80: attach real things to the twelve shops, then add two more each

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.2 — where can you buy each of the eleven things? In pairs, 4 minutes. Accept every reasonable answer, and there are usually three. apples – market, supermarket · bread – bakery, supermarket · burgers – butcher's, supermarket · chocolates – sweet shop, department store, supermarket · dictionary – bookshop · magazines – newsagent's, bookshop, supermarket · sandwich – café, bakery, supermarket · socks – clothes shop, department store, market, supermarket · sun cream – chemist's, department store, supermarket · tea towels – department store, market, supermarket · trainers – shoe shop, department store, market, supermarket
  2. Then the question that makes the stage worth doing (TB p.157): Do you prefer to buy things from supermarkets and department stores, or to go to smaller shops? Two minutes, and let them disagree.
  3. ex.3 — at least two more things you can buy at each shop, in pairs, 4 minutes. bakery: cakes, buns · bookshop: school books, reading books · butcher's: beef, chicken · café: tea, cakes · chemist's: face cream, medicine · clothes shop: T-shirts, jeans · department store: hats, furniture · market: fruit, vegetables · newsagent's: newspapers, pens · shoe shop: sandals, shoes · sweet shop: soft drinks, sweets · supermarket: flowers, food
  4. Kagan RallyCoach for the check: A names a shop, B names two things, then swap.
  5. Finish with the reverse task, and it is the one the block test uses: Name one thing you CANNOT buy at the baker's. Fast, round the room.
📺 On screen: the twelve shop names on the board as a running list (Backup: none needed — the exercises are printed on SB p.80 and the board simply keeps the twelve words visible)
👦 Students do: attach things to shops, argue about supermarkets, then reverse it. Expect department store to be the answer to everything — push for the specific shop. Typical problem: they answer in one word; the frame you can buy X at the Y is required

Reading · the York Times adverts
solo → pairs
Why: Five real-shaped advertisements with dates and offers in them. Scanning for a date is a different skill from reading for gist, and it is the one that actually gets used in life.

WHAT WE DO: the five adverts on p.80: what the shops are, then the questions and the dates (track 106)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. SB p.80: five advertisements from a local paper called the York Times — a bookshop with a café, a sweet shop, a shoe shop, a clothes shop and a chemist's. Each advert has an opening date and an offer in it, which is what the two comprehension exercises are built on.
  2. Pre-teach one word only, books closed (TB p.157): advertisement — and ask whether they have a favourite one.
  3. ex.4 — a race with a visible timer, three minutes: read the adverts and say what each shop is. Key: Reading Time – a bookshop with a café · Something Special – a sweet shop · One Foot After Another – a shoe shop · Shop and Try – a clothes shop · Brown's Chemist's – a chemist's
  4. One question before the detail work, from TB p.157: Where can you get something for your six-month-old sister? (Brown's Chemist's).
  5. ex.5 — five questions, alone, 4 minutes, then checked with a partner. Key: 1 Reading Time · 2 Brown's Chemist's · 3 One Foot After Another · 4 Something Special · 5 Shop and Try
  6. ex.6 — now the dates, and this is the scanning task proper. 3 minutes. Key: 1 21 March · 2 Reading Time – 6 March · Something Special – 20 March · One Foot After Another – 3 April · Shop and Try – 24 March · Brown's Chemist's – 19 March
  7. Play track 106 while they follow with a finger — the adverts are recorded, and it fixes the pronunciation of the shop words in context.
  8. Kagan Numbered Heads Together for the check on ex.6: the student called gives the date AND the advert it came from.
📺 On screen: track 106 in the whiteboard app (Backup with no sound: read the five adverts aloud yourself while they follow — they are printed on SB p.80 and the stage does not depend on the recording at all)
👦 Students do: race for the shop types, then scan for dates. Expect ex.6 to be faster than ex.5, which is a good thing to point out: finding a number is easier than finding a meaning. Typical error: they read the adverts from the top instead of scanning

Talking points · shops in our town
fours
Why: A short opinion exchange about something they can see out of the window, using the unit's words for a reason.

WHAT WE DO: a short opinion exchange that uses the twelve words for a reason

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three questions on the board, in fours, 6 minutes: Which shop in our town do you go to most? Which of the twelve shops does our town not have? What would you open here if you had the money?
  2. One rule that keeps it in English: every answer must contain one of the twelve words.
  3. Collect the answers to the third question on the board — that list is the material for the next stage, so do not skip the collecting.
  4. Two minutes of feedback with your ears only: read out three of their sentences and let the class vote on the best idea.
📺 On screen: the three questions and the collected list on the board (Backup: none needed — chalk and conversation, and the list has to be handwritten anyway)
👦 Students do: discuss in fours and produce a list of missing shops. Expect a bookshop with a café and a sweet shop to come up, because they are on the page. Typical problem: one confident student takes over — a numbered speaking order per group fixes it

🎯 Speaking out · our own advert
pairs → class
Why: The productive outcome: an advert for a shop that should exist in our town, written and presented. It needs the shop words, a date and an offer — all three from the page.

WHAT WE DO: write an advert for a shop this town should have, on the model of the five on p.80

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Give the frame on the board, taken straight from the adverts: NAME · what we sell · opening date · one offer · one line to make people come.
  2. In pairs, 7 minutes. It must contain a date written the way the book writes them and an offer of the buy two, get one free kind.
  3. Circulate with the correction sheet and log errors silently — the apostrophes above all.
  4. Adverts up on the wall or passed round. Each pair reads one other pair's advert and says whether they would go and why. 4 minutes.
  5. Class vote for the shop the town most needs.
  6. Close the loop with the errors you logged — three on the board, no names, thirty seconds to repair.
📺 On screen: the five-line frame on the board and the adverts on SB p.80 still open as models (Backup: the frame is dictated into notebooks — this stage needs no technology at all)
👦 Students do: write an advert, read somebody else's, vote. Expect the offers to be more imaginative than the English. Typical error: the date written as a number only — point at the book's own format

WB Unit 14 (p.56–59)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can name twelve shops and say what you can and cannot buy in each.
  • Subsidiary. Scanning five short adverts for dates and offers (SB p.80 ex.4–6, track 106); the apostrophe in shop names; writing a short advert.
  • Personal. Do not let the apostrophe become a five-minute grammar lecture. Ten seconds, four words, on the board — and then it is a spelling item that gets pointed at, not explained.
By the end, students can…
  • name twelve shops, spelling the four with an apostrophe correctly
  • say two things you can buy in each shop
  • say what you CANNOT buy in a given shop
  • find a date and an offer in a short advertisement

Structure
You can buy sun cream at the chemist's. · You can't buy a dictionary at the butcher's. · It opens on 21 March. · Buy two, get one free.
Form
the shop of the person takes 's: butcher → the butcher's, newsagent → the newsagent's, chemist → the chemist's. The other eight are plain compounds: bookshop, shoe shop, sweet shop, department store.
Pron. watch
BUTCHer's /ˈbʊtʃəz/ — no /r/. CHEMist's /ˈkemɪsts/ — /k/, not /tʃ/. BAKery, three syllables. These three are mispronounced by everybody on first meeting.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Where can you buy tablets and medicine?» → a chemist's, or a supermarket. Not a chemistry shop — there is no such thing.
Why does butcher's have an apostrophe and bookshop not? → because it is the shop OF the butcher. A bookshop is just a shop for books.
«Can you buy a sandwich in three different places on this page?» → yes — a café, a bakery, a supermarket. Most things can be bought in three places, and that is what ex.2 is really teaching.
«Five pound or five pounds?» → five pounds. The money word takes a plural even though money itself does not.

Assumptions: SB p.80, syllabus lesson 43. Shop, supermarket and café are known; chemist's, newsagent's, butcher's, department store and bakery are new. The apostrophe was met in Unit 6 with possession and will need reminding, not teaching. The present perfect of Unit 13 is three days old and gets recycled in the opening.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
butchers, chemists, newsagents without the apostropheFour words in a box on the board with the apostrophes in a different colour, up for the whole lesson. Point, do not explain. And say once why it matters: in the writing paper it counts as punctuation.
They think a chemist's sells chemistry equipmentOne picture and one sentence: it is where you buy medicine, plasters and sun cream. Fifteen seconds, and it is the single most useful shop word on the page for a traveller.
The advert stage turns into reading every wordSet it as a race with a timer: five adverts, five shop types, three minutes. Scanning has to feel different from reading or it is not being practised.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
