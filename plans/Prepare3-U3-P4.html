🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Describe an object precisely — twelve adjectives in the right order — and hear whose something is when the speaker changes their mind halfway through the sentence.
What is genuinely hard
Two different things. In listening, the speakers correct themselves, so the first name you hear is usually the wrong one. In writing, English orders adjectives by a rule nobody was ever taught, and a wooden small box sounds wrong for no reason a student can name.
Typical error to expect
They write down the first name they hear, but the speaker corrects themselves two seconds later (TB p.74). The answer is what is said LAST [exam report]
A word from the option was said in the recording → they tick it. A matching word is not a correct answer; meaning decides [exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
The reading goes first and does three jobs at once: it is the input, it is where the twelve adjectives live, and it contains the one text of the unit with a genuine surprise in it — a football that makes a noise so that a blind boy can play. Talking points follows immediately, while that is still working. The vocabulary stage ends with a guessing game rather than an exercise, because describing an object until somebody names it is the shortest possible proof that the words are owned. The listening is placed at the middle of the lesson, at 44′, when attention is highest and before the writing tires them; it also carries the one strategy of the day — wait for the end of the exchange, because the speaker will correct themselves. Adjective order gets its own short stage before the writing, with the seven-column table, so the writing stage has a tool rather than an instruction. And the drawing comes before the writing, as the Teacher's Book insists: you cannot describe an object you have not decided on.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“A blanket a grandmother made, and a football that makes a noise. Both of them are somebody's favourite thing. By the end of today you can describe an object so exactly that the person opposite you can draw it — and you send one to a museum.”

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim: describe an object so exactly that it can be drawn

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hold up an object and describe it badly on purpose: It's a thing. It's nice. Then again, properly: It's a small, smooth, wooden box.

WHAT WE DO: «Special Memories» on p.38: which present each of them got, then eight questions

Talking points · your earliest memory
small groups → class
Why: A personal question and a real one about how objects can be designed for people who cannot see — the kind of talk that makes a language lesson worth sitting through.

WHAT WE DO: the two questions on p.38: your earliest memory, and things that help people who cannot see

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Tell them YOUR earliest memory first, briefly — TB p.73 asks for this and it unlocks the stage.
  2. Small groups, 3 minutes: what is your earliest memory, and how old were you?
  3. Then the second question, and it is the better one: What other things could help someone like Pete? Collect ideas on the board — objects and services both count.

Paralympic five-a-side football uses a ball with a noise-making device inside, which is exactly Pete's ball. Other everyday examples worth naming: Braille displays on computers, over a hundred radio-reading services, tactile paving on pavements, and banknotes — the Canadian dollar among them — with Braille-like dots for the value.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 p.38: twelve adjectives on six photographs, then a guessing game (track 46)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Concept-check first, TB p.73: TB p.73, concept-check in this order: «The shape of an apple or the sun» (round) · «An elephant is this» (large, heavy) · «How a baby's skin feels» (smooth).

🎯 Listening out · whose is it
solo → pairs
Why: The productive listening outcome: five items, eight options, and speakers who correct themselves. The strategy — wait for the end of the exchange — is the point of the stage.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.39 (track 47): five things, eight people, three options left over

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the format in one sentence: Carmen and Murat go through things they have found in her grandparents' house. Five things have to be matched to eight people — three options are left over. The recording is played TWICE.
  2. Before you play it, TB p.74: TB p.74, before you play it: describe the photo — «What are they doing?» (looking in boxes) · «Who are speaking?» (Carmen and Murat) · «Who do all these things belong to?» (her relatives).
  3. State the strategy BEFORE the first play, because it is the whole stage: TB p.74, and this is the trap of the whole task: do not write down the first thing you hear — the speakers correct themselves. Listen to the end of the exchange, and use the second play to check rather than to answer.
  4. First play — answers in pencil, nothing said out loud. Do not compare. Do not speak.
  5. Second play — change what you want to change. Kagan Showdown to collect: every team reveals its five letters at once. Key (TB p.74): 1 A · 2 H · 3 B · 4 D · 5 F (the example 0 is E)
  6. Then, and only then, ask the question that teaches: Which one did you change after the second listening? What made you change it?
📺 On screen: track 47 in the whiteboard app, played twice, with the two lists on the screen (Backup if the board is down: read the conversation aloud from the audioscript on TB p.290, twice, taking both voices — the two lists are in the book on p.39)
👦 Students do: listen twice and match five of eight. Expect 3–4 of 5 after the first play and 4–5 after the second. Typical error: they take the first person named in the same sentence as the object and miss the correction that follows

Adjective order
class → pairs
Why: Native speakers order adjectives without knowing why. Seven columns make the invisible rule visible, and the writing stage needs it ten minutes later.

WHAT WE DO: the Get ready block on p.39: Ben's email and the seven-column table

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed, the question from TB p.74: what museums do you know, and what is inside them? Then open the book and read the notice: THE CITY MUSEUM · «Have you got a favourite thing or something from the past you want to tell us about? Send us an email. Describe the object and say why it's special for you, and attach a photo if you have one.»
  2. Read Ben's email and answer the one question on it — what is his favourite thing? The model on the page: I've got a beautiful, old, silver ring. It was my uncle's. I keep it in a small, red, wooden box as it's very special. He gave it to me when I was ten. I never wear it, but I like to look at it. From Ben
  3. Then the question that opens the rule (TB p.74): Why do we say a big wooden box and not a wooden big box? Put the seven columns on the board: Opinion (pretty, beautiful, lovely) → Size (big, small, little) → Physical quality (hard, smooth) → Shape (square, round) → Age (new, old) → Colour (blue, red) → Material (gold, silver, wooden, wool, metal)
  4. Students find the adjectives in Ben's email and put them in the table, then find three more pairs in the texts on p.38, 4 minutes. Key: Opinion: beautiful, lovely · Size: small, little · Physical quality: smooth · Shape: round · Age: old · Colour: red · Material: silver, wooden, wool, metal
  5. Say the honest version of the rule: nobody uses all seven at once — two or three is the whole job.
📺 On screen: the seven-column table on the screen, filled in live (Backup: draw the seven columns in chalk with one example word in each — the table must stay on the board until the end of the lesson either way)
👦 Students do: read the model, then sort the adjectives into columns. Expect smooth to be put under opinion. Typical error: they try to memorise all seven columns — say clearly that two or three is all anybody ever uses

Writing · an email to the museum
solo → pairs
Why: Writing built on a model, a table and a plan, started and finished in class — and the drawing first, because you cannot describe what you have not decided on.

WHAT WE DO: the PLAN / WRITE / IMPROVE block on p.39: about 35 words, with the drawing first

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. PLAN, 3 minutes of notes: PLAN — what is it, where or who did it come from, what does it look like; and which groups of adjectives you will need, in which order;
  2. WRITE, 9 minutes, and the first minute of it is the drawing: WRITE — about 35 words, on Ben's model, and DRAW the object first: TB p.74 says the drawing is what makes the description possible;
  3. Two conditions on the board: About 35 words. At least two adjectives before one noun, in the right order.
  4. Students who need support (TB p.74) get the spoilt version of Ben's email on the board and repair it instead of composing from nothing: for the writing, put a spoilt version of Ben's email on the board and let them repair it instead of composing from scratch.
  5. IMPROVE, 4 minutes: IMPROVE — swap, underline every adjective and check the order; then read all the emails as if you were the museum director and choose one object for the exhibition.
  6. Show the model answer only at the end: Model answer, TB p.74: I've got an interesting object for you. It's a 1966 paper poster of The Beatles, a fantastic old pop group. It's from my uncle's house and I found it in his bedroom. From Ben
  7. Log the errors on your correction sheet; the clinic is at the start of the next lesson — which is the review lesson, so it lands exactly where it is needed.
📺 On screen: Ben's email and the seven-column table stay on the screen while they write (Backup: both are printed in the book on p.39 and the table is on the board — this stage needs no technology at all)
👦 Students do: draw, then write about 35 words, then check a partner's adjective order. Expect the drawing to make the descriptions twice as concrete. Typical error: three adjectives in the wrong order — the partner check with the table catches it

Structure
It was beautiful, colourful and soft. · I've got a beautiful, old, silver ring. · I keep it in a small, red, wooden box. · It felt round, hard and smooth.
Form
adjective order before a noun: opinion → size → physical quality → shape → age → colour → material. After be they are simply joined with and.
Pron. watch
smooth /smuːð/ — voiced th at the end · colourful /ˈkʌləfl/ — three syllables · heavy /ˈhevi/, not /ˈhiːvi/.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«A wooden small box» — what's wrong? → the order. Size comes before material: a small wooden box.
«The speaker said «my brother» and then «no, my uncle» — which do I write?» → the second one. The answer is what is said LAST.
«Little» and «small» — which column? → both are size. English has two words there and one of them, little, often carries a feeling as well.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
They write down the first person they hear and stop listeningState it before the first play and repeat it before the second: the speakers change their minds. The answer is what is said last, not first.
The seven columns look like a table to be memorisedIt is not memorised, it is used: they only ever need two or three of the seven at a time. Do the ordering as the Cooler line-up game if there is any doubt — bodies remember what lists do not.
Pete's text raises real questions about blindness and the lesson driftsDo not shut it down — it is the best thing in the unit. Give it the three minutes of Talking points, use the background note from TB p.73, and come back on the bell.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
