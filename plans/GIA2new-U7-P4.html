🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Why (for the teacher): Character adjectives are harder for new students than appearance: you cannot see them. So we go through the book's own dictionary definitions (ex.1), then through a “positive — negative” table, then through real people from the podcast. The subskill (listening for numbers) is practised literally: we say all eight numbers out loud BEFORE listening, otherwise the ear will not catch them.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two teenagers today. Easton was fourteen when he built a robot hand out of Lego. Amineh was thirteen when she won a poetry prize — in a language she started speaking one year ago. To talk about them you need words that describe PEOPLE, not faces: generous, talented, lazy, kind, careful. Today we learn thirteen of them and their opposites. At the end you write six lines about a person you're proud of. Let's start with MY person…” (and I show the model straight away)
The gist, if you need to restate it: two teenagers from the podcast — Easton with his robot hand and Amineh with her poetry prize. To talk about them you need character words: generous, talented, lazy, kind, careful. We learn thirteen of them and their opposites, and at the end we write six lines about a person we are proud of.

WHAT WE DO: model before the task: my person on the board → ex.1 three definitions → ex.2 five definitions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. MODEL: I tell them about MY person: My grandmother is very kind. She's generous — she gives her time to everybody. She isn't lazy at all.
  2. ex.1 from the book's dictionary definitions (TB key: A lazy · B talented · C generous). Every word with a gesture: lazy — I slide down my chair; talented — a two-handed “brilliant”; generous — I hand over something imaginary.
  3. ex.2 in writing, with the word bank (TB key: 1 polite · 2 special · 3 careful · 4 friendly · 5 kind). Question out loud: Positive or negative? All five — positive.
  4. Kagan RallyRobin: partners take turns, one word each with an example about themselves.
📺 On screen: SB p.89 ex.1–2 · word bank on screen (Backup: the word bank written large on the board — not to be rubbed off before the end of these two lessons)
👦 Students do: they repeat the model and write the words in. Expect: the gestures pull them in. Typical problem: “talent” instead of talented — I point at the ending on the board

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 pairs of opposites + a “positive / negative” table + the prefixes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 with support (TB key: careless – careful · normal – special · rude – polite · unfriendly – friendly · unkind – kind).

  5. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade on 13 cards: word ↔ opposite.

WHAT WE DO: written first: six lines about a person on the frame, then read in pairs and to the class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

WB Unit 7 · the adjectives word bank stays up to the end of the unit
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, learners can use 13 adjectives to describe people with their opposites and write six lines about a person they admire.
  • Subsidiary. ex.1 three dictionary definitions; ex.2 five definitions; ex.3 the table of opposites; ex.4 six people; ex.5 the photos; ex.6–7 the podcast (track 46) for numbers; the prefixes un- / im- / il- / ir- as support.
  • Personal. The numbers are said out loud BEFORE listening — I don't start the stage without that. The adjectives word bank stays up all lesson and comes down only for the mini-test.
By the end, students can…
  • describe a person's character, not appearance
  • give the opposite of an adjective (kind → unkind)
  • catch a number in a recording and write it down
  • write six lines about a person I admire

Form
the adjective goes after be (She IS kind) or before a noun (a kind teacher); opposites are made with the prefixes un- / im- / ir- or with the suffix -less (careful → careless).
Pron. watch
from the Wordlist: generous /ˈdʒenərəs/ · talented /ˈtæləntɪd/ · polite /pəˈlaɪt/ · careless /ˈkeə(r)ləs/ · unkind /ʌnˈkaɪnd/
Concept checks (CCQs) · Assumptions
“He is very talent” — what is wrong? → we need the adjective: TALENTED.
“careful” and “careless” — what does each suffix mean? → -ful is “full of”, -less is “without”.
“£1,000” — how do you say it? → one thousand pounds: the sign is written first and said last.

Assumptions: SB p.89, syllabus pair 57. Appearance adjectives are familiar from the Starter unit; character is new. Big numbers and currencies are hard by ear — the subskill of the unit is exactly about that.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
“Good” and “bad” for everythingGently but firmly: “good” is only accepted together with a precise word. The word bank is always in sight and I point at it.
Numbers slip past the earTB technique: we say all eight numbers OUT LOUD before listening, including £1,000 = one thousand pounds. The ear only catches what the mouth has already said.
The podcast feels fastWe listen three times: 1) who is who, 2) the numbers, 3) checking. Between the runs there is a pause for writing.
Classroom management — signals
«Eyes up in 3-2-1» — count down calmly → all eyes on you at «1». No shouting over noise, ever.
Visible timer — timer on screen = we work; timer stops = pens/hands down. The timer is the boss, not you.
Volume dial — arm up/down = louder/quieter; «radio whisper» for rehearsals.
«Boards up!» — mini-whiteboard rounds: everyone commits, all boards up together — nobody hides, nobody shouts first.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, class answers with two and freezes — instant reset mid-game.
English-first — ideas in Russian are welcome, POINTS only for the English version — recast and repeat.
