🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Say and hear large numbers accurately, including the ‑teen / ‑ty pairs, and write a 50–70 word paragraph about your own English class with every capital letter right.
What is genuinely hard
One beat. Thirteen and thirty differ by nothing except which syllable is stressed, and in a recording that is the only clue there is. And in the writing: Russian capitalises none of the things English capitalises — not languages, not nationalities, not days, not I — so there is nothing at all to transfer.
Typical error to expect
thirteen read as thirty — the stress is the only difference (thirˈTEEN / ˈTHIRty) and a wrong number in the listening paper is a lost mark [exam report]
on monday, i speak english — days, languages, nationalities, countries and I are always capitalised in English and none of them are in Russian. This is criterion K4 of the exam email [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The exam speaking questions open the lesson rather than closing it: four questions about themselves, answered properly, set the standard for everything that follows and cost four minutes. Numbers are taught through arithmetic out loud, because that is the only way a number becomes automatic rather than translated. The ‑teen / ‑ty pair gets its own stage immediately afterwards, while the numbers are still in the air, and the rule is worked out by the class rather than given. The listening is placed before the writing so that the lesson's last half hour is uninterrupted: five predictions, then five checks. The writing runs the full four-stage cycle with the draft started in class — nobody takes an empty page home — and the IMPROVE stage has exactly one criterion, capital letters, because a peer check with one criterion gets done and a peer check with five does not.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Thirteen. Thirty. Say them again. One of those has the beat at the end and one at the beginning, and that is the entire difference — no other sound changes. In an exam recording, that beat is worth a mark. And then you are going to write fifty words about this class, and I am going to mark exactly one thing: the capital letters.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, one fast trading round on the two tenses, and the bridge into the last page of the unit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — the tense contrast, one round only. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with cards that each carry one time expression: yesterday · since 2019 · three days ago · already · when I was younger · never. The partner says PERFECT or SIMPLE and then a whole sentence.
  3. Two trades each. Yesterday's grammar is the hardest thing in the block and this is the only revision it gets before the test — keep it fast and keep it moving.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page ninety-three. Last page of the unit — and it starts with four questions about you.
📺 On screen: printed time-expression cards — the cards in their hands are the medium, so no backup is needed; if the set has gone missing, read the expressions out and the class calls the tense back in one voice
👦 Students do: trade cards and produce a sentence each time. Expect the sorting to be quick and the full sentences to be where the errors are. Typical error: I have finished it yesterday — the exact sentence from yesterday's clinic, and worth pointing at without stopping

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the two halves and the one thing that will be marked

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write two numbers on the board, nothing else: 13 and 30. Say one of them. Which one did I say? Do it three times. Half the room will be wrong at least once, and that is the demonstration.
  2. Then the two halves: 1 Numbers — saying them and hearing them. 2 Fifty words about this class.
  3. Name what will be marked, now and only this: In the writing today I am marking one thing. Capital letters. That is all.
  4. And say why in one sentence, because it makes it worth doing: In your language, «English» and «Russian» and «I» are not capitals. In English all three are. There is nothing to copy across — you just have to know it.
📺 On screen: the two numbers and the two halves of the lesson in chalk — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: guess which number was said, three times. Expect a genuine split, which is the point — nobody argues with a demonstration. Typical problem: one student always hears it correctly; make them the one who says the numbers next time

WHAT WE DO: the Prepare for the exam box p.93: four questions, answered properly

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The four questions are read aloud once: Where do you come from? What languages do people in your country speak? Do you enjoy studying English? Tell me something about what you do in your English lessons.
  2. Then the one demonstration that changes everything (TB p.182): TB p.182, the one sentence that turns the exam task into a language task: write Where do you come from? on the board, then under it Segovia. crossed out and I live in Segovia, a very old town not far from Madrid. next to it. Same question, same student, two completely different amounts of English heard.
  3. Set the standard in one line: Question four begins «Tell me something about». That means three sentences, not one.
  4. Kagan Timed Pair Share: ninety seconds each way, all four questions.
  5. Two pairs demonstrate at the front. Take a show of hands: Did anybody answer question one with just the name of a town? That is the honest self-check.
📺 On screen: the crossed-out one-word answer and the full-sentence version side by side in chalk — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: answer four personal questions in pairs. Expect question 4 to be the one that collapses into one sentence. Typical error: I come from Norilsk. and full stop — the board demonstration is what makes the longer version obvious

Vocabulary · large numbers
class → pairs
Why: Numbers are the one piece of vocabulary that is never guessable from context. They are taught through arithmetic, out loud, because that is the only way they become automatic.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3 p.93 (track 119): match four numbers to their words, then find and say the numbers in the map text

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.182, the way in: teach the four signs first — + plus, − minus, × multiplied by, ÷ divided by — and read calculations out for the class to answer aloud: 17 + 44 (61) · 11 × 8 (88) · 90 ÷ 6 (15). It takes two minutes and it turns numbers from a spelling task into a listening task.
  2. ex.1, 2 minutes: four numbers, four sets of words. Key: 176 — one hundred and seventy-six · 7,468 — seven thousand, four hundred and sixty-eight · 76,000,000 — seventy-six million · 7,000,000,000 — seven billion.
  3. Two form points, both worth thirty seconds each: the and before the last part (one hundred and seventy-six), and no ‑s on hundred, thousand, million after a number.
  4. ex.2: back to the map on p.92 — underline every large number and write it in words. Pairs, 5 minutes. Key: the numbers underlined in the text, in the order they appear: seven thousand · two hundred and eighty-four · three hundred and thirty-five million · five hundred and five million · two thousand three hundred and three · one thousand and sixty · four hundred and six million · two thousand one hundred and forty-six · one thousand three hundred and eleven · eight million · eight hundred and thirty-two.
  5. Play track 119 to check, then pairs practise saying them.
  6. ex.6 as the game version, 2 minutes: Student A says a number from the text, Student B closes the book and says what it refers to. Two hundred and eighty-four. — The number of languages in Europe.
📺 On screen: track 119 in the board app with the numbers on the screen (Backup: the numbers are printed on SB p.92–93 — say each one aloud yourself, twice, and have the class repeat chorally; the arithmetic warm-up needs no technology at all)
👦 Students do: match, then write eleven numbers out in words. Expect the writing to be slower than the saying. Typical errors: three hundred thirty-five million with no and, and two millions

28–38′
Pronunciation · teen or ty
class → pairs
Why: One beat is the whole difference between thirteen and thirty. It looks like a curiosity and it is a mark in every listening paper they will ever sit.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4–5 p.93 (track 120): the eight numbers, the stress, and the rule

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ask the orientation question first (TB p.182): How many syllables in «language»? Is the stress on the first or the second? The vocabulary of stress has to exist before it can be used.
  2. Play track 120. Students underline the stressed syllable in all eight. Key: thirTEEN · THIRty · fifTEEN · FIFty · eighTEEN · EIGHty · nineTEEN · NINEty.
  3. Let them state the rule themselves before you do. The rule the class works out for itself: in 13–19 the stress is on ‑teen, at the end of the word; in 20, 30, 40 … the stress is on the first syllable. That single beat is the only difference between thirteen and thirty, and in a listening paper it is a mark.
  4. Drill it with your hand: clap on the stressed syllable, four pairs, twice each. No explanation during the clapping.
  5. Kagan Showdown: you say one number of each pair, they write 13 or 30 on the mini-whiteboard, boards up together. Eight rounds, fast.
  6. For anybody still missing it: TB p.182, for anyone who cannot hear it: read out more calculations, but rig them so that every answer is either 13–19 or a round ten — 32 − 18 (14) · 8 × 5 (40) · 9 + 6 (15) · 30 × 3 (90). Then they do the same in pairs.
  7. ex.5 in pairs, 2 minutes: they say the numbers to each other and the partner writes the digits.
📺 On screen: track 120 in the board app (Backup: say the eight numbers yourself, twice each, and clap the stressed syllable — this is one of the few stages where the teacher's voice is genuinely better than the recording, because you can exaggerate)
👦 Students do: underline the stress, state the rule, then discriminate eight pairs. Expect 6 of 8 on the Showdown and near-perfect after the clapping. Typical error: they hear correctly but write the wrong digit under pressure — slow the last two down
SB p.93 ex.4–5 · track 120 · mini-whiteboards
38–45′
Listening · what languages do differently
pairs → class
Why: Five predictions made in pairs, then checked. The content is strange enough to hold attention, and the strategy — predict, then listen for five things — is the transferable part.

WHAT WE DO: the Listening box p.93 (track 121): five questions predicted, then checked

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.182, the way in: draw a rainbow on the board and get the colours in English (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet). Then ask for all the colour words in their own language and count them. The number will not match — and that is the whole listening in one board drawing.
  2. The five questions are read aloud, then discussed in pairs BEFORE anything is played, 3 minutes. The pair discussion before the recording is not a warmer, it is the strategy: five predictions mean five things to listen FOR, instead of one long stream to survive.
  3. Take one guess per question from around the room. Do not confirm any of them.
  4. Play track 121 once. Key: 1 twelve colour words in Russian and Greek, eleven in English · 2 three (one, two, many) — the Pirahã language of Brazil · 3 they use one set with their family and the other when they need to be polite · 4 they use north, south, east and west instead of left, right, in front of, behind · 5 there are talking dictionaries and smartphone apps that help young people learn the language
  5. Then name the trap: Where marks go: question 4. The idea that a language can have no word for left is so strange that students stop listening to argue about it, and miss question 5.
  6. One closing question, thirty seconds: Which of those five is the strangest to you?
📺 On screen: track 121 in the board app with the five questions on the screen (Backup: the audioscript is on TB p.302 — read the talk aloud yourself, twice if needed; the five questions are printed in the students' own book)
👦 Students do: predict five answers, then listen once to check. Expect the colour-word question to be guessed and the direction question to astonish them. Typical problem: they argue about question 4 during the recording and miss question 5 — warn them in advance

Prepare to write · get ready
class → pairs
Why: The model is not a text to copy but a source of one thing: where the capital letters go. Six uses, all six inside seven sentences.

WHAT WE DO: the GET READY stage p.93: Maria's paragraph and the six uses of the capital letter

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.182, the thirty-second opener: write i am an english teacher on the board and ask What's the problem? Three things are wrong — the I, the English and the full stop — and every one of them is a mark in the exam email.
  2. Read the model aloud once, then leave it on the page: SB p.93, the model by a Spanish student: «My name is Maria and I come from Spain. Most of the people in my English class are from Spain too, but one of my classmates is from China and two are from Ecuador. The languages people speak in my class are Spanish, Catalan, Chinese and English. I like speaking in English, but I find listening difficult. I plan to spend more time learning vocabulary in the future.»
  3. GET READY: find in Maria's text an example of each use of the capital letter — after a full stop · for people's names · for countries · for nationalities · for languages · for the pronoun I. Six uses, all six present in seven sentences.
  4. Pairs, 4 minutes, hunting for the six uses. Kagan RallyCoach: one use each, in turn, with the example from the text.
  5. Collect the six onto the board as a permanent checklist: after a full stop · people's names · countries · nationalities · languages · I.
  6. Then the second thing to notice, and it costs one instruction: Look at how she starts each sentence. Count how many begin the same way. (they do not — and that is the shape to copy).
📺 On screen: the sentence i am an english teacher written wrongly in chalk, then repaired in a second colour, with the six-use checklist underneath — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: find six capital-letter uses in seven sentences. Expect nationalities to be the one they miss. Typical error: they think English is capitalised because it is the name of the subject — it is capitalised because it is a language, which matters for the next sentence
SB p.93 Writing · board
56–64′
Prepare to write · plan
solo
Why: Notes before sentences. Students who plan write 60 words in ten minutes; students who do not write 25 words in twenty.

WHAT WE DO: the PLAN stage p.93: four lines of notes, no sentences yet

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. PLAN: notes only, four lines — people's nationalities · languages people speak · languages people in the class are learning · your own likes, dislikes and plans.
  2. Say the rule of the stage and enforce it: Notes. Not sentences. If I see a full stop in the next six minutes I will take the pen.
  3. Give them the four headings on the board and let them fill in under each. The data is in the room — they can look round and count.
  4. Two minutes in, feed in the words the notes need and that nobody has: nationality, and the country adjectives they will want.
  5. Kagan RallyCoach for ninety seconds at the end: read your notes to your partner and the partner asks for one thing that is missing.
📺 On screen: the four note headings in chalk — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed; the students write in their own exercise books
👦 Students do: make four lines of notes about the real class. Expect the languages line to be longer than they expected, after last lesson's count. Typical problem: they write full sentences anyway — take one pen theatrically and the rest stop
SB p.93 Writing · board · exercise books
64–78′
🎯 Writing · your English class
solo, silent
Why: The productive outcome, started in class so that nobody takes an empty page home. Fifty to seventy words about the room they are sitting in.

WHAT WE DO: the WRITE stage p.93: 50–70 words from the notes, in class, against the clock

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Silent mode, 12 minutes, alone. The instruction is three lines and then nothing: Fifty to seventy words. All four notes used. Every sentence starting a different way.
  2. Write the target on the board and leave it: 50–70. Write the time remaining at 6 minutes and at 2 minutes, in chalk, never out loud.
  3. Walk the rows. Answer nothing about content; to any question say Look at Maria's paragraph.
  4. At 10 minutes: Two minutes. Count your words and write the number at the bottom. Counting is part of the task, and it is a habit worth building now.
  5. For your own reference, the Teacher's Book model: TB p.182 model answer: «I'm Artur and I'm from Sopot in Poland. Almost everyone in this class is Polish but there are two girls from Ukraine and a Lithuanian guy. We all speak Polish, and some English of course, but the Ukrainian and Lithuanian students know Russian too. I love listening to American rock music and one day I want to be in a band.»
📺 On screen: the word target and the minutes remaining written on the board (Backup: use the wall clock and write the times in chalk — the writing stage needs no technology whatsoever)
👦 Students do: draft 50–70 words in silence. Expect 40–60 words from most and a full 70 from three or four. Typical error: the first sentence is My class is good — the instruction about sentence openings is what prevents the next four being the same

78–86′
Improve · the capital letter check
pairs
Why: One criterion only. A peer check with one criterion is done properly; a peer check with five is not done at all.

WHAT WE DO: the IMPROVE stage p.93: swap, and check two things only

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. IMPROVE: swap and check two things only — is all the information there, and is every capital letter right? Nothing else is marked at this stage.
  2. Swap papers. The checklist is the six uses on the board and nothing else. Kagan RallyCoach: the partner circles every capital letter that is missing or wrong, and writes the total in the margin.
  3. The rule of the stage, and it is absolute: You may circle. You may not rewrite your partner's paragraph.
  4. Second check, thirty seconds: Is all four of the notes actually in there? Nationalities, languages, what they are learning, what you think.
  5. Name where the marks go: Where marks go: the nationality adjectives. polish, ukrainian, lithuanian in lower case is one error each, and in the exam email they all count against the same two-mark criterion.
  6. Students correct their own paper in a different colour. That colour is the evidence of the improvement, and it is what you look at when you collect them.
📺 On screen: the six-use checklist still in chalk from the GET READY stage — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: check a partner's capital letters and repair their own. Expect two to four circles per paragraph, mostly nationalities and languages. Typical error: they also correct the grammar and the spelling — remind them that today one thing is being marked

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can say and understand large numbers and the ‑teen / ‑ty contrast and have drafted a 50–70 word paragraph about their English class.
  • Subsidiary. Answering four personal questions in full sentences; listening for five specific facts after predicting them; the six English uses of the capital letter.
  • Personal. Mark one thing in the IMPROVE stage and say which thing it is before they start writing. A draft returned with six kinds of correction on it is a draft nobody rewrites.
By the end, students can…
  • say a four-digit and a seven-digit number out loud
  • hear the difference between thirteen and thirty
  • answer four questions about their school life in full sentences
  • write 50–70 words about their English class with correct capital letters

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Half the class cannot hear the ‑teen / ‑ty difference at allStop using words and use your hand: clap on the stressed syllable. Say the pair four times with the clap and no explanation. Then the TB p.182 fix — read out calculations rigged so that every answer is 13–19 or a round ten, and let them answer with the number rather than analyse it.
The paragraph comes out as five sentences beginning «My class is»The model on the page is the cure and it has to be looked at properly. Give one instruction before they write: Look at how Maria starts each sentence. No two are the same. Copy the shapes, not the words.
Nobody finishes 50 words in the timeThat is expected and it is why the WRITE stage is a draft. The target in class is the first four sentences, properly planned; the workbook task on p.67 is where it is finished. Say so at the start so that nobody panics at 78 minutes.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

The same skill is tested by: Task 35 (an email to a friend) — the capital-letter work on SB p.93 is criterion K4, orthography and punctuation, which is worth 2 of the 10 marks.

Languages, nationalities, countries, days and the pronoun I are the four capital letters Russian-speaking students lose most often, because Russian capitalises none of them. Two marks of the exam email live in exactly this habit.
