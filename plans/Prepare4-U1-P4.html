🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Build adverbs from fifteen adjectives (+ly, y→ily and the irregulars fast / hard / well / early), catch specific details in a two-part listening, and hold a two-way interview about your own style with the Prepare to speak phrases.
What is genuinely hard
Adverbs look free (add -ly) until healthily, luckily and the irregulars arrive — fastly and goodly are honest products of the rule. In the listening, the details sit in fast natural speech; in the speaking, the fight is turning «Jeans.» into a sentence with a reason.
Typical error to expect
He drives very careful instead of carefully; and goodly / fastly — irregular adverbs regularised [A2/B1]
A one-word reply («Fifteen.», «Jeans.») does not count as an answer at all: an answer needs a verb in it [national exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
Adverbs open the lesson because the listening and the speaking both need them (loose-fitting clothes worn everywhere, I usually buy…). The mime break welds each adverb to a movement while the set is minutes old. The Beatles photo buys attention for a listening that then runs three passes at three depths — gist, choices, exact words — because that is how detail-listening is actually trained. Harry's recorded answer is placed BEFORE the students' own interviews: the model sets the bar, the Prepare to speak box gives the bricks, and the interview at 66′ is where both get spent. The error clinic lands straight after, while the conversation is still warm in their ears.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“1965. Your grandma is sixteen. What is she wearing? Today you hear a real answer — a grandma, her photos, The Beatles on the TV. And then the interview turns on YOU: what do you wear, where do you buy it, how much do you spend — with answers longer than one word.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a clinic on lesson 3's three errors, an irregular ping-pong, and the bridge

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. A clinic on the correction sheet: three sentences with no names — Did you went shopping? · I choosed a T-shirt. · What you did on Sunday? Thirty seconds — fix all three on your board.
  3. Check together; then a 30-second irregular ping-pong, chorally: wear! — wore! · choose! — chose! · tear! — tore! · go! — went!
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Yesterday: WHAT people wore. Today: HOW people do things — quickly, politely, amazingly — and a trip to 1965.
📺 On screen: the three sentences written on the board before the lesson — the board is the medium, no backup needed
👦 Students do: correct on mini-whiteboards, then ping-pong the past forms chorally. Expect the auxiliary error (Did you went) to be fixed by most now — lesson 3's drill working

Vocabulary · adverbs
pairs → class
Why: Fifteen adjectives become adverbs through three visible patterns (+ly, y→ily, irregular). Sorting them into columns IS the rule — no rule needs announcing.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–3: fifteen adjectives become adverbs in three visible patterns

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books-closed intro (TB): write slowly · quickly · beautifully and the verbs sing · speak · walk — pairs make three true sentences about people they know.
  2. ex.1: internationally / angrily / hard → what are the adjectives? Key: international · angry · hard.
  3. ex.2 in pairs: sort the fifteen adjectives into the three board columns as adverbs, 5 minutes. Key: +ly: amazingly, badly, carefully, honestly, politely, quickly, rudely, seriously · y→ily: healthily, heavily, lazily, luckily · irregular: early, fast, well.
  4. The irregular corner gets a 20-second chant: fast, hard, well, early — no -ly!
  5. ex.3 — complete the eight sentences, Kagan Showdown per item, spelling counts. Key: 1 happily · 2 fast · 3 loudly · 4 slowly · 5 quietly · 6 hard · 7 well · 8 easily.
📺 On screen: the ex.2 word box on the screen while pairs sort (Backup: the fifteen words are in the book box — sort straight from the page into the chalk columns)
👦 Students do: sort fifteen words into three patterns and write eight under pressure. Expect healthily and luckily to be misspelled first try (y kept) and item 7 (well) to catch a third of the class. Typical error: fastly — the chant answers it
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare4_Unit2_Vocabulary2_Adverbs_A5
A5 glue-in sheet — ADVERBS: HOW DOES IT HAPPEN?: the adjective → adverb pairs (amazing–amazingly · careful–carefully · honest–honestly · healthy–healthily · polite–politely · quick–quickly · serious–seriously · fast–fast) with the Russian, a definition and a model sentence, the FORMATION box (usual +-ly · -y → -ily · -le → -ly · fast stays the same) and the CONTRAST She is careful (what is she like?) / She chooses carefully (how does she choose?)
  • Glue the sheet in and draw the arrow adjective → adverb in each pair.
  • Highlight fast — it is the one that does not change.
  • Answer the frame How did he do it? with three different adverbs.
  • Test yourself: a person or a thing → adjective; an action → adverb.

Adverbs in action
standing
Why: A ninety-second mime stirrer that welds each adverb to a movement: walk slowly, speak politely, write carefully. Cheap, fast, and the words stop being abstract.

WHAT WE DO: a mime stirrer that welds adverbs to movement

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. Walk — SLOWLY. Walk — FAST. Wave — POLITELY. Sit down — CAREFULLY. Now in pairs: one calls a verb + adverb, the other acts it. Three each. Rudest mime wins nothing — this is a polite classroom!
  2. Close with Kagan Stand Up–Hand Up–Pair Up: find a new partner, tell them one TRUE sentence with an adverb — My dad drives really carefully.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: mime, command, and produce one true adverb sentence with a new partner. Expect full engagement — this is the lesson's pressure valve before 20 minutes of listening

Listening · Sara and her grandma
solo → pairs
Why: Three passes, three depths: gist (what do they talk about), choice (facts of the 1950s–60s), and exact words into gaps — the same recording trains three sub-skills.

WHAT WE DO: tracks 07–09: three passes — gist, four choices, nine gap words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pass 1, gist (ex.2, track 07), books closed: What do they talk about — a, b or c? Key: b — they talk about music and fashion in the 1960s.
  2. Pass 2 (ex.3, track 08) — first half again, four choices. Pair check, then Kagan Numbered Heads Together to report. Key: 1 didn't watch (no colour TV in the 1950s) · 2 listened to pop in the 1960s · 3 saw The Beatles on TV · 4 dad plays Abbey Road.

  4. Pass 3 (ex.4, track 09) — second half, gaps 1–4. Key: 1 blue, jeans · 2 dresses, tights · 3 hair, beards, loose-fitting · 4 jacket (grandma made her own).
  5. Check the predictions from the hook: which guess survived?

👦 Students do: listen three times at three depths. Expect pass 1 to be easy and gap 3 (loose-fitting) to need the second play of pass 3 — that is the check habit being built. Typical error: writing jacket in every gap that mentions clothes — the board list disciplines it

Speaking prep · the questions
pairs
Why: The four interview questions are built by the students before they are asked: whoever has assembled a question reads it with the right stress and owns it.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: build the four interview questions and own them

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 warm-up in pairs, 90 seconds: Do you enjoy shopping for clothes? Who do you go with? What do you buy?
  2. ex.2 — choose the words to build the four questions. Key: 1 do you like (wearing) · 2 (How often) do you buy · 3 (Where) do you usually (buy) · 4 (How much) do (you spend).
  3. Kagan RallyCoach: partners read the questions to each other with FALLING intonation at the end; the coach listens for the melody, not just the words.
  4. The four questions go on the board — they are the interview script for 66′.
📺 On screen: the four question frames on the screen (Backup: the frames are in ex.2 in the book; the finished questions go on the board in chalk either way)
👦 Students do: build the questions and rehearse their intonation. Expect «How much do you spend» to feel rude to some — note that in England it is a normal survey question and the answer can be vague: about £30

Why: A recorded model answer shows the bar before students have to reach it: notes, then the exact phrases (I usually…, because…, I think…) that turn one word into an answer.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–5 (track 10): a recorded answer that shows the bar, then the phrase box

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Play track 10, ex.3: complete Harry's notes. Key: Harry's notes: 1 jeans · 2 two or three times a month · 3 the market · 4 about £30 a month.
  2. ex.4 — what EXACTLY does Harry say? Four gaps. Key: 1 think (jeans always look good) · 2 every week · 3 don't like (department stores — too expensive) · 4 always (spend more when I get birthday money).
  3. Name what makes Harry's answers good: a verb + a reason + a frequency word. Never one word.
  4. Walk the Prepare to speak box: Prepare to speak: present simple + adverbs of frequency (I usually / always / often…) · add reasons (because…) · likes and dislikes (I like… / I don't like… / I really like…) · opinion (I think… / I don't think…).
  5. Choral drill of the frames, 40 seconds: I usually… · I think… · because… · I really like…
📺 On screen: track 10 in the whiteboard app (Backup: read Harry's answers from the audioscript, TB p.262 — the notes task works by voice)
👦 Students do: catch the notes, then the exact phrases, then name the recipe. Expect the recipe to feel obvious once named — that is the point: obvious and named beats obvious and vague

66–80′
🎯 Speaking out · your style
pairs, rotating
Why: The productive outcome: a two-way interview about their own clothes and shopping, with the Prepare to speak phrases compulsory. Logged errors feed the next stage.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6: the two-way interview about clothes and shopping — the productive outcome

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the frame: Four questions each way. Every answer: a verb, a reason, a frequency word. Interviewer — follow up anything short: Really? Why?
  2. Kagan Timed Pair Share: A interviews B for 90 seconds on the timer, swap on the bell. Then EVERYONE moves — new pairs, second round.
  3. Second round target: same questions, better answers — longer, with because.
  4. Log errors silently on the correction sheet: adverb slips (dress good), one-word answers, frequency words missing.
  5. Close with two volunteers performing 30 seconds of their interview for the class — applause is the full stop.
📺 On screen: the four questions and a 90-second timer on the screen (Backup: questions in chalk from 48′, wall clock and your bell for the swaps)
👦 Students do: interview and be interviewed, twice, with a partner change. Expect round two to be audibly better — say it out loud, it is the lesson's proof. Typical error: I buy in the market usually — word order noted for the clinic

Structure
He sings amazingly. · I usually wear jeans because they always look good. · I don't go shopping every week. · How much do you spend on clothes? · I think… / I really like… / I always…
Form
adverbs: adjective + ly · consonant + y → ily (healthily, luckily) · irregular: fast, hard, well, early. Interview questions in present simple with frequency adverbs.
Pron. watch
question intonation falls at the end of wh-questions; luckily /ˈlʌkɪli/ · seriously /ˈsɪəriəsli/ — keep the syllable count.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«He runs very fastly» — fix it. → fast is its own adverb: he runs very fast.
«She sings good» — fix it. → good is the adjective; the adverb is well.
«I buy trendy clothes» — a full answer? → not yet: add how often and why — I usually buy trendy clothes because…

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
fastly / goodly produced by the fresh ruleExpected and welcome — the irregular four get their own corner of the board (fast · hard · well · early) and a 20-second chant before the listening.
Panic at natural-speed speech in the listeningProtect the first play: gist only, one question, books closed. Details come on the second play, and the gap words (ex.4) are on the board BEFORE play two — students listen FOR something, not just listen.
In the interview the stronger student takes all the air timeFix the roles hard: A interviews B for 90 seconds on the timer, then swap on the bell. Timed Pair Share keeps the turns honest.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
