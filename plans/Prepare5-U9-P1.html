🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use eight verbs of communication (apologise, complain, disagree, joke, promise, remind, warn, wonder) to say what a message is FOR, and report a question somebody asked: she asked me whether I had seen it.
What is genuinely hard
A reported question asks for two changes in the same breath. The word order goes back to a statement — subject, then verb — and the tense steps one move into the past. Russian does neither: it keeps the question order and the original tense, so the English version has to be BUILT, not translated. The auxiliary do is the third casualty and the one nobody expects: it simply disappears.
Typical error to expect
«He asked me where was I», «She asked what is the time» — a reported question is not a question any more. The word order goes back to subject + verb, and the question mark disappears with it. Russian keeps the inversion, so this one is produced by every single group until it is drilled aloud.
«They asked me did I enjoy the film» — the auxiliary do NEVER survives into a reported question. The Student's Book plants this trap in exercise 3, item 8, and it is worth stopping on.
«He asked that I want to come» — a yes/no question is reported with if or whether, never with that. A wh-question keeps its wh-word and nothing else changes about the opening.
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary arrives as PURPOSE, not as a list: eight situations on the page, each one describing why a person opens their phone, and the verb is the answer. That framing is what makes the reading work five minutes later, because Reading Part 1 asks exactly this question about five real messages — why has Max sent this text? The grammar then falls out of the unit's own reading text: many people wanted to know why Steve wouldn't open the bottle. The tense is derived from a sentence they have already met, not introduced cold, and the productive stage is a live three-way chain — ask, answer, report — in which the reporter has to listen to a real question and rebuild it on the spot.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“A message in a bottle was thrown into the Pacific in 1906. It was found on a Canadian beach a hundred and seven years later — and the man who found it refuses to open it. Today: the eight verbs that say what a message is for, and how to report a question somebody asked you.”

WHAT WE DO: About you + ex.1–2: the photos, then the eight situations

Answers: 1 disagree, 2 warn, 3 remind, 4 complain, 5 apologise, 6 joke, 7 promise, 8 wonder.

standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer that forces the eight verbs into the body

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Four corners of the room, one card each: APOLOGISE · COMPLAIN · REMIND · WARN.
  2. You read a situation — Your friend is walking towards a wet floor. You are forty minutes late. Your sister forgot her keys. The food arrived cold. Everybody walks to a corner.
  3. One person from each corner says the sentence they would actually send, in one line, using the verb. Six rounds, six walks, twenty-four sentences.
  4. Freeze and sit: Page ninety-nine. One message was thrown into the sea in 1906. It has never been opened.
📺 On screen: four corner cards only — chalk and paper, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone commits with their body six times and a quarter of the room produces a sentence aloud each round

Reading · a message in a bottle
individual → pairs
Why: Reading · a message in a bottle

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: the article read for the story, then the question nobody can answer

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed, thirty seconds: what unusual ways of sending a message can you think of? Smoke signals, drumming — and, if nobody says it, a message in a bottle.
  2. Background in two sentences and it earns its place: the oldest known message in a bottle was found in British Columbia in 2013. It had been thrown into the sea in 1906 — one hundred and seven years earlier.
  3. Four minutes, silent: read the article and answer the four questions. Answers: Earl Willard sent it; it is very old; the content is unknown; and Steve Thurber refuses to open it — probably to avoid damaging the bottle, destroying the paper, or reducing its value.
  4. Mixed-ability pairs compare before the class check. Check the one vocabulary item that blocks the text: genuine — really what it seems to be.
  5. Take the argument to the floor for ninety seconds: Should he open it? A lot of people online were angry that he wouldn't. Both sides, one sentence each, and stop on time.
📺 On screen: the 1906 date and the 2013 date written on the board with an arrow and «107 years» between them — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: read for the story, answer four questions alone, then argue one side in one sentence

individual → pairs → class
Why: Reading · five real messages (Reading Part 1)

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4: what kind of text, then the five exam questions

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.3 first, and it is not a warm-up — it is the skill. Elicit the text types (a phone text, a handwritten note, a notice, an email), then two minutes to match. Answers: 1 and 2 phone texts, 3 a handwritten note, 4 a typed notice, 5 an email. Then the follow-up the Teacher's Book asks for: who is each one FOR?
  2. Name the format before they start: Five short texts, five questions, three options each. The question is always about the PURPOSE of the message, not about a detail in it.
  3. The one strategy that changes the mark, and it goes on the board: underline the key words in the QUESTION first — 1 Why, Max, sent, text · 2 Dolores, says · 3 note, Anna, mentions · 4 giving, seeing, asking · 5 is keen, recommend, ask.
  4. Check the three words that block the options: recommend (say that something is good), delivery (someone brings a parcel and hands it over), souvenir (something you keep to remember an event).
  5. ex.4 individually, six minutes, clock visible. Then mixed-ability pairs compare and must AGREE before the class check.
  6. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: 1 C, 2 A, 3 C, 4 B, 5 B. For every answer, one student reads the line of the message that proves it — and one says why the nearest wrong option is wrong. That second half is the whole lesson.
  7. Talking points if the clock allows: If you sent a message in a bottle, what would you write about? Will we still be writing messages like these in the future? What other methods have people used in the past to communicate? — pairs first, then the floor
📺 On screen: the five answers with the proof line beside each — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail. Backup: the five texts are recorded as track 67 if a weaker reader wants them read aloud
👦 Students do: underline key words in the questions, work alone against a clock, then defend both the right answer and the rejection of the wrong one

Grammar · reporting the question
class → individual
Why: Grammar · reporting the question

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–4: the tense derived from the text they have just read, then produced twenty times

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Warmer, books closed, and it takes ninety seconds. Write on the board: She asked him if he often wrote emails. Ask for the DIRECT question. Then put both lines one under the other and let the class name the four differences: the pronoun, the tense, the added if, and the word order.
  2. Then do it live: ask one student How often do you write emails? and nominate another to report it. Write their sentence on the board next to the frame.
  3. ex.1: the four examples matched to the direct questions — and one of them is the sentence from the reading they did twenty minutes ago (many people wanted to know why Steve wouldn't open the bottle). Answers 1 b, 2 d, 3 a, 4 c. Elicit what happened to every tense: one step back.
  4. ex.2 in pairs, the rules: a question, b positive, c whether, d never, e which. Stronger students explain each rule using an example from ex.1 — the Teacher's Book asks for this and it works.
  5. ex.3, five minutes: eight sentences, correct the mistake. Warn them there may be MORE THAN ONE error in a sentence, and that item 7 is present, not past. Kagan RallyCoach in pairs — one writes, one coaches, then swap.
  6. Check ex.3 in writing on the board, all eight, and make the class name which rule each correction belongs to.
  7. ex.4 individually, four minutes: five reported questions with the pronoun, possessive and tense changes to make. Answers: 1 if / whether there would be · 2 if / whether I had ever lost my · 3 why I hadn't bought myself · 4 when I had last seen my · 5 how many songs I had downloaded for my. Weaker students work in pairs here (the Teacher's Book says so and it is right); stronger students work alone.
  8. Grammar reference is SB p.163 for anyone who wants it in writing.
📺 On screen: the frame ASKED + IF/WH + SUBJECT + VERB with the crossed-out do, and the eight corrections written up as they are checked — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: derive the tense from a text they have read, coach a partner through eight corrections, then produce five reports alone

🎯 Speaking out · the three-way chain
groups of three
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · the three-way chain

WHAT WE DO: SB p.139 ex.5: ask, answer, report — live, with a real question in the room

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of three, roles named aloud and written on the board: A asks · B answers · C reports both.
  2. The question bank is SB p.139: How much time do you spend online each day? Have you ever chatted with someone in another country? What is the most interesting fact you have found online? Which music website can you recommend? What is the strangest thing you have ever done?
  3. Demonstrate once with three strong students so the whole room hears the shape: A asks, B answers, C says She asked him how much time he spent online, and he said…
  4. 🎯 Kagan Three-Step Interview, ten minutes: three questions each, then rotate the roles so everybody reports twice. C is the demanding job and everybody has to do it.
  5. Monitor for ONE thing and note names: the word order after the reporting verb. Nothing else gets corrected today.
  6. Delayed correction, three minutes: five sentences you heard, written on the board with the names removed. The class repairs them together, pointing at the frame each time.
  7. Close with the count: Every person in this room has just reported six real questions asked by a real person. That is the item most candidates lose, and you have it.
📺 On screen: the three roles and the frame on the board, side by side — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone asks, everyone answers and everyone reports, twice, in a group where nobody can hide

Structure
She asked me whether I had seen the message. · Many people wanted to know why Steve wouldn't open the bottle. · He wondered how many messages we send each day.
Form
reported question = ask / want to know / wonder + (if or whether for yes/no · wh-word for the rest) + SUBJECT + VERB, with the tense one step back and no auxiliary do.
Pron. watch
apologise /əˈpɒlədʒaɪz/ — the stress is on the SECOND syllable and the class will put it on the first · disagree /ˌdɪsəˈɡriː/ carries its stress right at the end · in a reported question the reporting verb is stressed and the rest of the line flattens: she ASKED me where I LIVED · and quite is /kwaɪt/, one syllable, not the two the spelling suggests
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«She asked me where I lived.» Is that a question? → no. So does it take a question mark? → no. And does the verb come before the subject? → no. Three answers, one rule.
«They asked me did I enjoy it» — what is wrong? → do. It never survives. They asked me IF I HAD ENJOYED it.
«Do you often use email?» reported — which tense? → past simple: whether they often USED email. One step back, every time.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The question word order survives into the reportDo not correct it in words. Point at the board frame — ASKED + IF/WH + SUBJECT + VERB — and wait. Three seconds of silence self-corrects more than thirty seconds of explanation.
Reported speech from Unit 16 has gone coldSpend ninety seconds, not ten minutes: one direct statement on the board, one reported version underneath, both underlined. Then move. The backshift is the same machine; only the opening changes.
They cannot hear the difference between the eight verbsGive the situation, not the word. Every check goes «You want someone to know about a danger — which verb?» rather than «What does warn mean?» Meaning first, label second.
Fast finishers in the vocabulary stageThe Teacher's Book task: find the OTHER communication verbs hiding in the questionnaire on p.139 — post, tweet, chat, comment, communicate, give someone a call. They report them to the class.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 10–18 (the reading section — the short-message task is the closest Cambridge cousin of the Russian heading-matching item) and tasks 19–25 (reported questions are a named line of the grammar codifier).

A reported question is the single most reliable trap in the Russian grammar section, because it asks for TWO changes at once: the word order goes back to a statement, and the tense steps one move into the past. Russian does neither — it keeps the question word order and the original tense — so a candidate who has not been drilled writes «He asked me where was I» and loses the mark. The eight communication verbs that arrive today (apologise, complain, disagree, joke, promise, remind, warn, wonder) are the reporting verbs the reading section uses to signal WHY a message was written.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
