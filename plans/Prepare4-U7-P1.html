🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name and use the twelve moods-and-feelings adjectives (amazed, brave, confused, disappointed, embarrassed, exhausted, grateful, hopeful, proud, relaxed, scared, stressed) in full sentences with a reason attached — and read a research article the way researchers read: claim, method, result.
What is genuinely hard
The words are long, the stresses wander (disapPOINTed, emBARrassed) and three of them hide sound traps: exhausted starts with a /ɡz/, scared is ONE syllable and demands OF, and the -ed endings say /t/ or /d/, not /ɪd/ — except exhausted and disappointed, which DO say /ɪd/.
Typical error to expect
I'm scared spiders · afraid from dogs — the preposition dropped or borrowed from Russian; the frame is scared OF + noun [A2/B1]
An opinion with no reason attached — I think reality shows are bad full stop — scores as no opinion at all in the speaking [national exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
An extended block: Unit 13 gets THREE lessons, so today is pure vocabulary and reading — the grammar hiding in the text waits for lesson 2, unnamed. The adjectives arrive inside a social-media feed (every word attached to a person and a story), get a second spoken context in six recorded mini-dramas, and go physical in the statues break. The reading is the block's showpiece — real research on ten million sentences — and it is run with the TB's method for the inserted-sentences task: first gap together, sentences sorted by what they describe, neighbours of every gap read aloud. The productive stage then repeats the research ON THE CLASS: everyone rates the days of the week, the chart goes up, and the class compares its own data with the professors'. Opinions close the loop with the book's sentence starters and the reason rule.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two professors read TEN MILLION sentences to find the worst day of the week — and it is NOT Monday. Today you learn the twelve words English uses for feelings, you read the research, and then we run the same experiment on this class. Your own worst day, measured. With evidence.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts. The rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a mood round with two partners, and the bridge into the unit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — the mood round. Kagan Mix-Pair-Share, one question, two partners: How are you feeling today — and what are you going to do about it? Thirty seconds each way, swap once — last block's going to wakes up inside the new topic.
  3. Collect two answers and stretch them: Tired? After the test, I believe it. English has a stronger word — you'll have it in ten minutes.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the unit: Books open, page seventy-six. Unit thirteen — Mixed feelings. Twelve words for what's inside you… and one scientific surprise about your week.
📺 On screen: questions by voice — the voice is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: mingle, answer, hear that today is about feelings. Expect Russian fragments — accept, reformulate, move; the point is warm mouths, not accuracy

Vocabulary · the feelings feed

Why: Twelve adjectives arrive inside six social-media posts — every word lands attached to a person and a story, not to a list. The definitions task then covers the six words the feed left out, so the set closes itself.

WHAT WE DO: About you + ex.1: six posts, choose the feeling; ex.2: define the six words left over

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Think-Pair-Share on the two About-you questions: About you (video 09): Are you usually in a good mood? · What kind of things put you in a bad mood? — twenty seconds to think, tell your partner, two pairs report.
  2. ex.1 in pairs, 4 minutes: Ellen's feed, six friends, six posts — choose the adjective that fits each story. Key: the friends' feed, choose the adjective: 1 Alfie, home alone with a horror film — scared · 2 Evie, «Incredible game, Brighton!» — proud · 3 Lily, film night on the sofa — relaxed · 4 Ollie, the ending of Sherlock — confused · 5 Jack, «Sydney is the capital of Australia» — embarrassed · 6 Grace, 92% in the practice test — hopeful.
  3. Check with stories, not letters: Why is Alfie scared? … Home alone, horror film. Perfect.
  4. ex.2, fast: the six unused words against their definitions. Key: definitions for the six unused adjectives: a grateful (feeling or showing thanks) · b disappointed (not as good as you hoped) · c amazed (very surprised) · d exhausted (very tired) · e stressed (worried, can't relax) · f brave (no fear of danger).
  5. Drill the traps by ear, three choral repetitions: exhausted /ɪɡˈzɔːstɪd/ — the x says gz and -ed says id · embarrassed /ɪmˈbærəst/ — -ed says t, and the spelling has TWO r and TWO s · scared /skeəd/ — ONE syllable, and it is followed by OF · stressed /strest/ — one syllable · disappointed /ˌdɪsəˈpɔɪntɪd/ — stress on POINT. Anchor gestures: amazed = WOW eyes, embarrassed = red cheeks, exhausted = empty battery.
  6. Words go into the two mood columns as they are confirmed — the class decides which column each joins (confused starts an argument; let it, in English).
📺 On screen: the feed is on SB p.76 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: choose by story, define, drill, sort into moods. Expect hopeful/grateful to be the two genuinely new words — both get an extra example from you. Typical error: scared spiders — the OF goes up on the wall in red

Reading · the worst day
solo → pairs
Why: Gist first, then the inserted sentences with a method, then the word harvest — the CELTA order. The research text also quietly carries next lesson's grammar in a dozen natural examples; today nobody names it.

WHAT WE DO: the research article: hook vote → gist → the five inserted sentences (with the method) → the word harvest

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hook, books closed: hands up — Whose worst day is Monday? Tuesday? … Now: two professors read TEN MILLION sentences to answer this properly. Let's see who's right.
  2. Gist race, 3 minutes — ex.1: Find the research answer: worst day, happiest day. Go. Key: gist: the research says Wednesday is the worst day and Sunday the happiest — most students expect Monday, which is the fun of it.
  3. ex.2, the five missing sentences — the method first: do gap 1 together (G), sort the eight sentences into about-the-METHOD vs about-the-RESULTS, and say the rule: Read the sentence before the gap and after the gap. The answer lives in the neighbours. Pairs, 6 minutes. Key: the inserted-sentences task (B1 Reading Part 4 shape: five sentences out, three distractors): 1 G · 2 D · 3 C · 4 A · 5 F — not used: B, E, H. TB method: do the first gap together (G — the everyone-back-at-school sentence fits the Monday paragraph); sort the sentences into about-the-method (C, D, E) vs about-the-results (A, B, F, H); always read the sentence BEFORE and AFTER the gap.
  4. ex.3, pairs, 3 minutes — the six highlighted words. Key: highlighted words: 1 came up with (thought of) · 2 analysed (examined carefully) · 3 surprising (not expected) · 4 put off (decide to do later) · 5 dreadful (terrible) · 6 according to (as said by). Put off and dreadful go on the wall — the comments used them about days.
  5. Close with the bonus fact: Happiest people online: 45 to 60. Most miserable: teenagers. Do we protest? Ten seconds of noise, cap it.
📺 On screen: track 65 is the recording of the article — optional (Backup: skip the audio entirely; the stage is built on silent reading)
👦 Students do: vote, read for the answer, place the sentences by their neighbours, harvest the words. Expect surprise at Wednesday and delight at Monday being second-happiest — that IS the gist check. Typical problem: placing sentences by topic words alone — the neighbours rule catches it

WHAT WE DO: 90 seconds: the wall becomes the week

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. The left wall is Monday, the right wall is Sunday — the whole room is a week. Walk to your WORST day. Look around — you are the data.
  2. Now walk to your BEST day… and sit down where you are. That picture you just made? That's a survey. Now we do it properly.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: walk their answers, see the class spread. Expect a Wednesday cluster after the article — priming is real, and it is worth one laugh

🎯 Speaking out · the class survey
pairs → class
Why: The productive outcome: the class repeats the professors' research on itself — everyone rates the days, the chart goes up, and every judgement must carry an adjective and a reason. Data beats opinion, and the grammar of reasons is today's vocabulary.

WHAT WE DO: repeat the professors' research on the class: rate, chart, compare — every judgement with an adjective and a reason

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Frame on the board: On …day I usually feel [adjective] because … — plus a seven-day grid for tallies.
  2. One minute solo: everyone writes their worst day and best day with the frame — two full sentences.
  3. Kagan Timed Pair Share, 90 seconds each way: say both sentences, partner asks ONE follow-up (Why? What happens?).
  4. Collect the tallies fast — hands up per day, a student marks the grid. The class chart appears.
  5. Compare with the research, in sentences: According to OUR research, the worst day is… The professors said Wednesday. Are we normal? Why not? Two or three theories, adjectives compulsory.
  6. You walk with a paper during pair talk: log dropped OF, one-word answers and stress slips SILENTLY — they open lesson 2.
📺 On screen: the sentence frame and the tally grid in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: rate their week, interview each other, build the chart, explain the difference from the research. Expect the comparison to produce the best language of the lesson — school tests and training schedules are real evidence. Typical error: I stressed — the missing am is logged, not corrected

WHAT WE DO: Quiz-Quiz-Trade with the twelve adjectives + a spelling round on the traps

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

WB Unit 13 (p.52–55)

Structure

Form
be/feel/get + adjective; scared OF + noun; proud OF; grateful TO someone FOR something; the -ed forms here are adjectives, not verbs.
Pron. watch
exhausted /ɪɡˈzɔːstɪd/ — the x says gz and -ed says id · embarrassed /ɪmˈbærəst/ — -ed says t, and the spelling has TWO r and TWO s · scared /skeəd/ — ONE syllable, and it is followed by OF · stressed /strest/ — one syllable · disappointed /ˌdɪsəˈpɔɪntɪd/ — stress on POINT
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I'm scared» — of WHAT? The frame wants scared OF + the thing: scared of spiders, scared of the dark. Can I drop the of? → not when the thing is named.
«exhausted» or «tired» — which is stronger? → exhausted: completely empty, after a marathon, not after one lesson.
«grateful» — do I feel good or bad? → good: someone helped me and I want to say thank you.

Assumptions: SB p.76–77, syllabus lesson 40 — the block after the Unit 11–12 test: expect the class warm, not rusty. Relaxed, scared and proud are usually known by sight; amazed/embarrassed/exhausted blur together and grateful/hopeful are effectively new. The present perfect is owned from Units 9–10 — which is why next lesson's just/already/yet can be discovered rather than taught.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
amazed, embarrassed and exhausted melt into one wordThree anchors, said and gestured: amazed = WOW eyes; embarrassed = red cheeks; exhausted = empty battery. Return to the anchors every time one is misused — the gesture recalls faster than the translation.
«I'm boring» is offered for «мне скучно»Do NOT open the -ed/-ing story today (it is lesson 3's aim). Recast quietly — you're BORED, yes? — and move on; the full rule gets its own lesson.
The inserted-sentences task collapses into guessingThe TB method is in the plan: first gap together, sentences sorted into method vs results, and the rule said out loud — read the sentence BEFORE and AFTER every gap. Guessing stops when the method starts.
The survey stage produces one-word answersThe bar is on the board: adjective + because. Wednesday, stressed, BECAUSE we have two tests. Point at the bar, wait, get the full sentence — every time.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
