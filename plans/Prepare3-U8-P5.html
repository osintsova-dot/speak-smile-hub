🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Choose between the present perfect and the past simple by reading the time expression in the sentence, and use both of them inside one conversation.
What is genuinely hard
Nothing here is new. Both tenses have been taught, both are known, and that is exactly the difficulty: this is the first time they are put side by side and made to compete. The decision is not about meaning in the abstract, it is about one word in the sentence — yesterday, since, already, ago — and students who translate instead of looking for that word get it wrong every time.
Typical error to expect
I have seen this film yesterday — the present perfect with a finished time marker. yesterday, ago, last year, in 2019 always take the past simple [high priority, A2/B1]
Did you ever be to Spain? instead of Have you ever been to Spain? — life experience takes the present perfect [A2/B1]
I have goed / I have writed / I have swimmed — the third form invented by analogy. The list on SB p.167 is not optional [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The whole lesson is one decision, so the discovery is built on one pair of board sentences and four questions about them — no new form is presented at all. The rules are completed by the students and then used immediately to sort nine sentences, because a rule that is only read has gone by the next stage. The Susanna text is the biggest single piece of controlled practice in the block and it is deliberately a connected text rather than ten separate sentences: that is the shape the exam uses and it is the shape where time markers actually live. The reference page gets three minutes at the midpoint, purely for the two-column time-expression table, which is the fastest diagnostic a student can carry into a test. The productive stage requires both tenses in the same dialogue, because separately they are easy and together they are the point. The error clinic is at the end and not at the start, so that the errors are today's.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“You know both of these tenses. You have known them for months. Today is not about learning a new one — it is about the half-second where you have to choose. And the choice is never in your head. It is one word in the sentence, and I am going to show you where to look for it.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a three-round rewind of the language map, and the bridge into the tense contrast

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — yesterday's numbers, said properly. Three rounds: One — how many languages in the world? Two — which country has eight hundred and thirty-two? Three — how many does Europe have?
  3. Insist on the whole number said as words, not read as digits. It is thirty seconds of pronunciation and it is the vocabulary of the next lesson arriving early.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page ninety-one. Today there is nothing new. That is the problem.
📺 On screen: the three questions in chalk on the board — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: answer three factual questions with full numbers. Expect the facts to have stuck and the numbers to be shaky. Typical error: seven thousand said as seven-zero-zero-zero — say it back correctly and move on

Lesson frame

Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: put the whole lesson on the board as two sentences and one question

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The two sentences from TB p.178, one under the other, with the verbs underlined: I have known Alex for two years. and He was a first-year student when I met him.
  2. Name the tenses. They can, instantly — present perfect and past simple. Confirm it and then say the honest thing: Right. You know both. So why is this the hardest lesson of the block?
  3. Answer it yourself in one line: because from today they are in the same sentence and you have to choose in half a second.
  4. Set the standard for the whole lesson, and it is the only one: Every answer today comes with the word that made you choose it. Not «it sounds right». The word.
📺 On screen: the two model sentences in chalk with the verbs underlined — the board is the medium here and they stay up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: name both tenses and hear why the lesson exists. Expect confidence at the start and a visible drop at the first mixed exercise, which is normal and worth predicting out loud. Typical problem: they think it will be easy — the frame sentence about half a second is what prepares them

Grammar · the two tenses
class → solo
Why: Both tenses are known separately; this is the first time they are contrasted. The whole lesson is one decision, so the discovery is built around one pair of board sentences.

WHAT WE DO: the board questions from TB p.178, then ex.1 p.91: six sentences sorted by tense

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stay on the two frame sentences and ask the four questions in this order (TB p.178): Do I still know Alex? (yes) So can the present perfect mean something not finished? (yes) Is Alex a first-year student now? (no) So can the past simple mean something in the present? (no).
  2. That is the entire concept and it took ninety seconds. Write the two conclusions on the board under the sentences.
  3. Books open, ex.1: six sentences taken from the recordings on p.90, marked present perfect or past simple. Solo, 3 minutes. Key: 1 past simple · 2 past simple · 3 present perfect · 4 past simple · 5 present perfect · 6 past simple.
  4. Check with Kagan Showdown — PP or PS on the mini-whiteboard, all six in a row, boards up together.
  5. For each one, the question of the day: Which word made you choose it? Items 1, 2 and 6 all contain a finished time word; items 3 and 5 contain since and already.
📺 On screen: the six sentences on the screen so the class marks the same one at the same time (Backup: they are printed on SB p.91 — uncover them on the page with a ruler and keep the two conclusions in chalk on the board)
👦 Students do: answer four concept questions, then sort six sentences. Expect 6 of 6 — this stage should be easy and its job is confidence. Typical problem: item 4 is a question, and questions hide their time word at the end

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 p.91: complete the six rules, then ex.3: match nine sentences to the four uses

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed first, elicit the rules rather than reading them (TB p.178): Which tense goes with «yesterday» and «ago»? Which goes with «never» and «yet»? Which goes with something that started and finished in the past? Which goes with something that finished but is still important now?
  2. Books open, ex.2 in pairs, 3 minutes. Key: 1 a past simple, b present perfect · 2 a present perfect, b present perfect, c past simple, d present perfect.
  3. Then use the rules immediately — ex.3, nine sentences matched to uses a–d, 4 minutes. This is the stage that turns a completed rule box into something operable. Key: 1 d · 2 a · 3 c · 4 a · 5 b · 6 a · 7 d · 8 d · 9 c.
  4. Kagan RallyCoach: the partner may only accept an answer with the letter AND the reason.
  5. Draw the FINISHED box on the board now and leave it up: a line with NOW at the right, a box round the past labelled FINISHED, yesterday · ago · last year inside it, since · already · yet · ever outside it.
📺 On screen: the rule box and the nine sentences on the screen, matched live as the class decides (Backup: both are printed on SB p.91 — draw the four uses as four columns on the board and let students come up and write the sentence numbers into them)
👦 Students do: complete six rules, then apply them nine times. Expect ex.3 to be harder than ex.2, which is the right way round. Typical error: uses a and d confused (both are present perfect) — that distinction is not worth a fight today

Grammar · Susanna's blog
solo → pairs
Why: Ten gaps in one connected text — the exam shape as well as the coursebook shape. The text carries its own time markers, and finding them is the skill.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 p.91: ten gaps in one connected text about a woman who speaks eight languages

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed, thirty seconds: Write down «hello» in as many languages as you can. Then open the book and read the text fast to see whether Susanna speaks any of them.
  2. SB p.91 ex.4: Susanna Zaraysky has studied eleven languages (English, Russian, French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Croatian, Ladino, Hebrew, Arabic and Hungarian) and speaks eight of them. She was born in Russia, learned English when the family moved to California, studied French and Spanish at school, then heard tourists speaking Italian and decided to learn that too. She has written several books, has been on television many times and has had a special interest in language and music for years.
  3. Now the ten gaps, solo, 6 minutes, verb in brackets. Key: 1 has studied · 2 was · 3 learned · 4 moved · 5 studied · 6 heard · 7 decided · 8 has written · 9 has been · 10 has had.
  4. Kagan RallyCoach to check, and the rule of the check is the rule of the lesson: the partner asks Which word made you choose it? for every gap, including the right ones.
  5. For anyone still guessing, use the TB p.178 walk-through: TB p.178, for the tense contrast: go through every gap of the Susanna text and make students say WHY, in their own language if necessary, using the rules of ex.2. Gap 1 is present perfect because she probably hasn't stopped learning these languages; gaps 2, 3 and 4 are past simple because they are facts that finished.
  6. Two closing questions that are worth the minute (TB p.178): Which language do you think was hardest for her? What would you want to read on her blog?
📺 On screen: the Susanna text on the screen with the ten gaps, filled in live during the check (Backup: it is printed on SB p.91 — work from the paper book and write only the ten answers on the board, numbered)
👦 Students do: complete ten gaps in a connected text and justify each. Expect 7 of 10 solo and 9 of 10 after the pair check. Typical errors: gap 1 (present perfect, because she has not stopped) and gap 8 (has written, no date given) — both are the same distinction

Why: The reference page separates the two tenses by time expression, which is the fastest diagnostic a student can carry into a test.

WHAT WE DO: three minutes on the one table that is worth carrying into a test

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Send them to SB p.162 and go straight to the practice table at the top of the second half — the sorting task, not the rules.
  2. Read the thirteen expressions out and have the class call back PERFECT or SIMPLE in one voice: already · ever · for 18 weeks · in 2008 · last week · never · recently · since 5th May · three days ago · when I was younger · yet.
  3. The two that always split the room are recently and when I was younger. Do those twice.
  4. Set the instruction that makes the page useful later: Copy that table into the back of your exercise book. Before the block test, that is the only page you need to look at.
📺 On screen: the two columns drawn on the board as the class calls out — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed; the reference page is printed in the students' own book
👦 Students do: sort thirteen expressions by calling out. Expect it to be fast and about 90 per cent accurate, which is a good place to leave it. Typical error: recently — it has no marker feel to it at all and has to be learnt as a list item

Why: Six items where both forms look possible until the time expression is read. This is the stage that shows whether the rule has actually landed.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5 p.91: six sentences where both forms look possible until you read the time word

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. One demonstration on the board first (TB p.178): He finished / has finished, so it is my turn now. Which one and why? (has finished — the result matters now, rule 2d).
  2. Solo, 4 minutes, six items. Key: 1 watched · 2 have already bought · 3 have seen · 4 watched · 5 arrived · 6 danced, ate.
  3. Kagan Showdown to check: the team agrees, writes on the mini-whiteboard, all boards up at once.
  4. Item 6 has two verbs in one sentence and both are past simple — it is the one that catches people who have started over-using the present perfect. Point at it.
  5. For every item, one team says the word that decided it. Not the rule number — the word.
📺 On screen: the six sentences one at a time on the screen so the whole class decides the same one (Backup: they are printed on SB p.91 — write the six pairs on the board before the lesson and circle the winner as the class calls it)
👦 Students do: choose six forms and justify each. Expect 5 of 6. Typical error: item 3 (I have seen the beach) — students hear a holiday and reach for the past simple, but there is no finished time word anywhere in it

Grammar · in conversation
pairs
Why: The two tenses in the same exchange: a general question in the present perfect, a definite occasion in the past simple. That pattern is how the tenses really live together.

WHAT WE DO: ex.6 p.91: four short conversations where the question and the answer use different tenses

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Explain the pattern before the exercise, because it is the single most useful thing in the lesson (TB p.178). On the board: Have you ever been to Rome? / Yes, I have. I went there last summer.
  2. Question — general, no date, present perfect. Answer — one particular time, past simple. Say it once, point at it, leave it up.
  3. Four conversations, pairs, 5 minutes. Key: 1 A: Have you done — B: finished · 2 A: did you go — B: went, has lived · 3 A: Have you ever eaten — B: had · 4 A: Did you enjoy — B: have never seen, liked.
  4. Kagan Numbered Heads Together to collect: Conversation two — number ones, boards up!
  5. Conversation 2 is the richest: it contains a past simple question, a past simple answer and a present perfect with since all in three lines. Read it aloud in two voices before moving on.
📺 On screen: the four conversations on the screen, revealed one at a time (Backup: they are printed on SB p.91 — work from the paper book and write only the answers on the board, numbered by conversation)
👦 Students do: complete four dialogues in pairs. Expect conversation 4 to be the slowest — it contains have never seen next to liked. Typical error: they put the answer in the present perfect too, matching the question instead of contrasting it

🎯 Your own conversations
pairs → class
Why: The productive outcome: four dialogues written and performed, each of which must contain both tenses. The constraint is what makes it a grammar task rather than a chat.

WHAT WE DO: ex.7 p.91: write and perform four conversations of your own, both tenses in each

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The task and its one constraint: Four conversations, like the ones in exercise six. Every conversation must contain the word «ever» and the word «ago». Those two words make both tenses unavoidable.
  2. Model the shape with a strong student for fifteen seconds, then let them write. Pairs, 6 minutes.
  3. Walk and monitor for the tense choice specifically. Fix the questions now — a broken question survives into the performance and infects the answer.
  4. Kagan Timed Pair Share: pairs join into fours and perform two conversations each way, 4 minutes.
  5. Three pairs perform at the front. The audience has one job: Put your hand up when you hear the past simple.
  6. Log errors silently for the clinic — it is six minutes away.
📺 On screen: the two constraint words ever and ago written large in chalk, plus the model exchange from the previous stage still on the board — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write and perform four dialogues containing both tenses. Expect the two constraint words to do all the work. Typical error: Have you ever been there ago? — the constraint forced both words into one clause; that is exactly the sentence the clinic wants

80–86′
Error clinic
class
Why: Four errors, named and repaired publicly and anonymously, while there is still a lesson left to use them in. Feedback is only useful when there is time to act on it.

WHAT WE DO: four errors from today, anonymous, ninety seconds each

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Four sentences on the board, exactly as they were said or written today, no names. Choose them deliberately: one have + yesterday, one missing participle, one Did you ever …?, one answer that matched the question's tense instead of contrasting it.
  2. Ninety seconds each. The class repairs it chorally, you write the correct version next to it in a second colour, and then one student says the WORD that decides it.
  3. Four. Not twenty. These four cost this class more marks today than everything else together.
  4. Everybody copies the four repaired sentences into the back of the exercise book, under the table they copied from p.162. That page is now the revision plan for the block test.
  5. Finish with the one-line diagnostic they can use alone: Find the time word. Is it inside the finished box or outside?
📺 On screen: the four wrong sentences in chalk with space beside each for the repair — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: repair four sentences and copy them down. Expect the Did you ever one to be the hardest to see, because it sounds completely natural. Typical problem: somebody recognises their own sentence and says so — thank them out loud, it makes the next clinic easier

Structure
She lived in Sweden three years ago. · She learned Russian last year. · She's spoken Japanese since she was a baby. · Did you go to Spain in the holidays? · I've already finished mine. · The teacher gave it to us three days ago.
Form
past simple with a finished time (yesterday, ago, last year, in the holidays, when I met him) · present perfect with an unfinished one (since, for, already, yet, just, ever, never, this term). Question in the present perfect, answer with the occasion in the past simple.
Pron. watch
the contracted auxiliary is what students miss: I've studied is /aɪv/, not /aɪ hæv/. And the ‑ed endings from block 7 still apply — decided /ɪd/, moved /d/, studied /d/.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I have known Alex for two years.» Do I still know Alex? → yes.
«He was a first-year student when I met him.» Is he a first-year student now? → no. So the past simple cannot mean now.
«I have seen this film yesterday» — what is wrong? → yesterday is a finished time, so it takes the past simple.
«Have you ever been to Spain? — Yes, I've been there last summer.» Where is the mistake? → the second sentence names the occasion, so it is I went there last summer.
Which of these two is about the result now: He finished or He has finished, so it is my turn? → the second.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«I have seen this film yesterday» survives every explanationStop explaining and make it visual. Draw a line on the board with NOW at the right end and a box round everything before it labelled FINISHED. Put yesterday, ago, last year inside the box and since, already, yet outside it. Then: If your time word is in the box, the tense is past simple. Always.
Weaker students choose correctly but cannot say whyThat is the TB p.178 mixed-ability note and it matters: go through the Susanna text gap by gap and make them justify each one against the rules of ex.2, in Russian if necessary. A choice they can justify survives the test; a choice they guessed does not.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
