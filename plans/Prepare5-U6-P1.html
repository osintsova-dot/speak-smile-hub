🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use twelve words for school and education (primary, secondary, attend, qualifications, degree, undergraduates, boarding school, break up, do well / badly) and use the past perfect to show that one past action happened BEFORE another: when I got up, my brother had already gone.
What is genuinely hard
The past perfect is a choice Russian never has to make. Russian orders events with words — сначала, потом, до этого — and English orders them with a tense, so the form is easy and the DECISION to use it is not. The second difficulty is mechanical and expensive: had takes the third form, and half a group will write had went for a year unless the irregular list is on the desk.
Typical error to expect
«When I arrived, the train left» for an event that had already happened — English marks the earlier action with the past perfect: the train had left. Russian orders events with words, not with tenses, so the past perfect has to be chosen consciously every single time.
«had went», «had drank», «had wrote» — the past perfect takes the THIRD form, not the second, and the irregular list is where the marks actually go on the block test.
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary arrives through three photographs of genuinely hard journeys to school and a photographer describing them, so education is not an abstract topic for ninety seconds. The reading is three teachers describing three classrooms — one of them inside a cave in China, built by the village fifty years ago — and it does the job a reading should do in a grammar lesson: it hands over the example sentence the grammar comes out of. The past perfect is then built on a timeline drawn on the board rather than explained, because it is a tense about ORDER and order is spatial. The productive stage is personal and unavoidably past-perfect: what had already happened in your life before you started school.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“One of today's classrooms is inside a cave. The village built the school themselves, fifty years ago, carrying the materials up the mountain. Today: the English words for school — and the tense that says which of two past things happened first.”

class → individual
Why: Grammar · the past perfect, on the timeline

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–5: the tense derived from the text, then produced eight times

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Back to the timeline on the board. Write two events on it: 7.30 am my brother went to school · 8.00 am I got up. Then ask for one sentence containing both.
  2. Give the model if nobody produces it: When I got up, my brother had gone to school. Underline both forms and put an arrow from the earlier dot to the later one.
  3. ex.1–2: the rule out of the reading text — the school had been built before he went there. Elicit the form: had + past participle, for the action that happened FIRST.
  4. Put the irregular past participles on the desks that need them now, before the exercise. Weaker students should not be spending their thinking on gone against went.
  5. ex.3, two minutes: which action came first, a or b? Answers 1 b, 2 b, 3 a, 4 b. Fast and oral — it is a comprehension check, not a production task.
  6. ex.4 individually, three minutes: six items. Check on the board in WRITING, with all three verb forms said aloud each time.
  7. ex.5, four minutes: the eight-gap text about a school in Norway. Check and take the two facts the class notices — every child has a computer, and at sixteen they leave the island for the mainland.
  8. Grammar reference is SB p.157 for anyone who wants it in writing.
📺 On screen: the timeline with two dots, two events and an arrow — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: derive the tense from a text they have read, then produce fourteen forms in writing

🎯 Speaking out · before I started school
pairs → class
Why: 🎯 Speaking out · before I started school

WHAT WE DO: a personal task that cannot be done without the past perfect

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write three stems on the board: Before I started school, I had already … · By the time I was ten, I had … · When I arrived at this school, I had never …
  2. Four minutes, silent, individually: finish all three in writing. Rule — every sentence contains had and a third form, and the third form is underlined.
  3. 🎯 Kagan Timed Pair Share, two minutes each way: read your three. The listener asks one follow-up question about each — How old were you? Who taught you?
  4. Then the guessing round, five minutes: collect the papers, redistribute them, and read three sentences aloud without the name. The class guesses who wrote them. It is the best two minutes of the lesson and it makes everybody listen to the grammar.
  5. Correct only the participle and the tense choice today. Nothing else.
  6. Close with the count: Everybody in this room has just written three past perfects about their own life. That is the tense that separates B1 from B2, and you have it.
📺 On screen: the three stems on the board with the timeline still underneath — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: write three personal past perfects, defend them in pairs, then hear them read anonymously

Form
past perfect: had / hadn't + past participle, for the action that happened FIRST · past simple for the later one · common signposts: before, by the time, when, already, never … before.
Pron. watch
the stress is the whole difficulty in this set and it is worth four minutes: qualifiCAtions · BOARDing school · eduCAtion · underGRADuates · and compound nouns take the stress on the FIRST part (HEADteacher, TIMEtable, BLACKboard), which is what makes them sound like one word even when they are written as two
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«When I got up, my brother had gone to school.» Who left first? → my brother. That is the whole tense in one question.
«had went» — possible? → no. Had takes the third form: had gone. Say the three forms aloud: go, went, gone.
Do I always need the past perfect for two past actions? → no, only when the ORDER matters and is not obvious.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
