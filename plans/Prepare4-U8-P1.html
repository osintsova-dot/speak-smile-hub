🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use the eight computer collocations (delete a file, do a search, have a virus, install an app, share links, upload photos, download a podcast, + password) in full questions and answers about their own digital lives — and survive a real five-people-eight-apps matching text with a method.
What is genuinely hard
The verbs are easy; the COLLOCATIONS are the point — Russian speakers say make a search and put a photo. Add two sound traps: virus is /ˈvaɪərəs/ (not «вирус») and upload stresses the LOAD.
Typical error to expect
«My best friend is call Sean» — the passive without its past participle (is callED) is one of the most-flagged lines in Russian candidates' scripts; the Corpus task of this very unit hunts it.
Also expect I searched about my name for I did a search for my name — the collocation wall exists to kill exactly this.
Why the lesson is built this way
An extended block: Unit 15 gets THREE lessons, so today is pure vocabulary and reading — the passive hiding in the app texts waits for lesson 2, unnamed. The collocations arrive inside a quiz about THEIR phones, get a second spoken context in Jack's recorded interview, and go physical in the mime break. The reading is the block's exam showpiece — B1 Reading Part 2 exactly — and it is run with the underline-the-needs method on Emre together before pairs work alone. The productive stage turns the quiz back on the class as a real interview with follow-ups, and the app-builders stage converts the reading's language into a four-line pitch of their own.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Have you ever deleted something important by mistake? Searched for your own name online? Today English gets its hands on your phone: eight phrases for everything you do every day, five teenagers hunting for the perfect app — and by the end YOU pitch an app of your own.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a phone poll with two partners, and the bridge into the unit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — the phone poll. Kagan Mix-Pair-Share, one question, two partners: What did you use your phone for today — before this lesson? Thirty seconds each way, swap once.
  3. Collect three answers and stretch them toward the topic: Photos, music, a game… English has exact phrases for all of this. Eight of them, today.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the unit: Books open, page eighty-six. Unit fifteen — Digital life. First: a quiz about YOU.
📺 On screen: questions by voice — the voice is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: mingle, answer, hear that today is about their digital lives. Expect Russian fragments — accept, reformulate, move on

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim and put up the collocation wall

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two colours on the board: verbs in one column (delete · do · have · install · share · upload · download), nouns in the other (a file · a search · a virus · an app · links · photos · a podcast) — empty lines between them, to be joined by minute 20.
  2. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: eight phrases, one exam-style reading, and by the end — an interview and an app pitch of their own.
  3. Set the bar: A verb without its noun is half a phrase. We collect PAIRS today.

👦 Students do: listen; one student repeats the pairs-rule in their own words

Vocabulary · the digital quiz
pairs → class
Why: Eight computer phrases arrive inside a quiz about THEIR digital lives — every collocation lands attached to a question they genuinely want to answer, not to a list. The verb+noun pairs go up as pairs; the verb alone is worth nothing.

WHAT WE DO: About you + ex.1: match the quiz halves; drill; join the wall pairs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Kagan Think-Pair-Share on About you: What do you use your phone or computer for? Make a list — you have sixty seconds. Lists compared, longest list read out.
  2. ex.1 in pairs, 4 minutes: eight quiz beginnings, eight ends — match by story sense. Key: 1 c · 2 f · 3 b · 4 h · 5 e · 6 a · 7 g · 8 d. Track 73 confirms (or you read the full questions aloud — works identically).
  3. Check by meaning, not letters: Why does number one end with by mistake? … Because nobody deletes an important file on purpose. Exactly.
  4. Drill the traps by ear, three choral repetitions: upload /ʌpˈləʊd/ (stress on LOAD) · virus /ˈvaɪərəs/ (not «вирус»!) · delete /dɪˈliːt/ · password with a full /ɑː/.
  5. Students come up and join verb to noun with a chalk line — the wall is now the unit's dictionary.
📺 On screen: track 73 in the board app is a luxury here (Backup: read the eight full quiz questions from SB p.86 yourself — the matching works identically by ear)
👦 Students do: match, justify, drill, build the wall. Expect have a virus and do a search to be the two genuinely new pairs — both get an extra example from you

Listening · Jack's digital week
solo → pairs
Why: The recorded interview gives every phrase a second, spoken context — and the follow-up questions of ex.2 are exactly the interview skill the speaking stage needs twenty minutes later.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2–3 (track 74): Alex interviews Jack — catch the follow-up questions, then Jack's facts

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the scene: Alex asks Jack the quiz. But a good interviewer never stops at the answer — catch the follow-up questions.
  2. First pass — complete the follow-ups of ex.2. Key: 2 get it (back) · 3 remember them · 4 do you share · 5 you find · 6 to share them · 7 have altogether · 8 listen to.
  3. Second pass — pairs complete Jack's facts in ex.3, Kagan RallyCoach, one pen. Key: 2 virus · 3 password · 4 shares links · 5 done a search · 6 uploads, photos · 7 installed, apps · 8 downloads podcasts.
  4. Quick harvest: Which of Jack's facts is also true for you? Tell your partner in a full sentence.

Move break
standing
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: 90 seconds of collocation mimes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. I say the phrase — you do the action. Upload photos! Delete a file! Do a search! Have a virus! (The virus mime is the best thirty seconds of the lesson.) Speed up mercilessly.
  2. Now the trap round: I do the action — you shout the phrase. And… freeze. Sit down. Reading time: five teenagers need the perfect app.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: mime, shout, sit. The collocations are now attached to bodies, not pages

32–48′
Reading · five teenagers, eight apps
solo → pairs
Why: This is B1 Reading Part 2 in its natural habitat: underline what each person NEEDS, scan the apps for what each one OFFERS, and let the traps fail. The method is taught on Emre together before pairs fly solo.

WHAT WE DO: the B1 Part 2 method: needs first on Emre together, then pairs match all five; the traps fail

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hook, books closed: Eight apps for learning English. Three of them are traps — nobody needs them. Let's find out who survives.
  2. Method on Emre together (ex.1–2): underline his three needs (difficult practice · listening · short bus journeys), scan apps A–C only. Key: 1 B, C · 2 B, C · 3 A, C — and C (Newscast) is the best app for Emre. Say the rule: Needs first. Apps second. Never the other way.
  3. ex.3–4 in pairs, 8 minutes: underline the needs of Anna, Luis, Evie, Filipe, then match all five from A–H. Key: Emre → C (Newscast) · Anna → H (Themez) · Luis → B (EnglishScene) · Evie → A (WordPowr) · Filipe → E (LanguageLab); D, F and G are the three extras.
  4. Feedback with justification: Evie → A. Prove it from the text. … play online against users — she enjoys competing. Point. Fish any pair out of traps D, F, G publicly and kindly.
  5. Fast finishers: synonyms in the texts for awesome, attractive, fairly, allow, huge.
📺 On screen: track 75 is the recording of the app texts — optional (Backup: skip the audio entirely; the stage is built on silent reading)
👦 Students do: underline needs, scan offers, match, justify from the line. Expect Anna→H vs Anna→A to split the room — H wins on quick activities through topics; let pairs argue it in English first

Reset · the app vote

Why: Ninety seconds of movement that pre-loads the speaking: the room becomes a spectrum, their feet give the first opinion, and the mouths only have to explain what the feet already said.

WHAT WE DO: 90 seconds: the room becomes a spectrum

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand up. Left wall — I'd pay for a good app. Right wall — never, free or nothing. Walk to your opinion. Look around — you are the data.
  2. One sentence from each end — why are you standing there? … And sit down where you are. Now the quiz comes back — for YOU.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: walk their opinion, hear both ends. The Talking point about paying for apps is now pre-loaded for the pitch stage

🎯 Speaking out · the quiz interview
pairs → class
Why: The productive outcome: the book's quiz run as a real interview with follow-ups, then reported to the class. Nobody can play without the collocations, and the reporting line forces third-person accuracy.

WHAT WE DO: the book's ex.4–5: run the quiz as a real interview with follow-ups, then report the best fact

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Frame on the board: Have you ever…? — Yes, I have. Once I… / No, never, because… plus one follow-up from ex.2 per question.
  2. Kagan Timed Pair Share, 3 minutes each way: A interviews B with any four quiz questions plus follow-ups; swap.
  3. ex.5 — the report: Tell the class the most interesting thing about your partner. Third person, full sentence: Sara got a virus on her dad's computer and they had to buy a new one. Five or six reports.
  4. You walk with a paper during pair talk: log dropped collocations and third-person slips SILENTLY — they open lesson 2.
📺 On screen: the interview frame in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: interview, follow up, report in third person. Expect the deleted-homework stories to be gold — let the best one run a little long

64–76′
App builders
groups of 3–4
Why: A four-line app pitch built from the reading's own language — the fastest route from receptive to productive, and the Talking points ride along for free.

WHAT WE DO: invent an app for learning ANYTHING in four lines, pitch it, vote — the Talking points ride along

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of three or four. The four-line skeleton on the board: Our app is called… · It teaches… · It's perfect for people who… · It costs… / It's free because…
  2. Six minutes to build. Steal shamelessly from the reading's language — awesome reviews, games last a few minutes, play against your friends.
  3. Kagan Numbered Heads Together: you call a number, that member pitches. Class votes: Which app would you actually install?
  4. Close with the Talking points as a whole-class minute: What apps do you use to help you with studying? Do you mind paying for apps? — pairs first, then open the floor; every opinion carries a because
📺 On screen: the four-line skeleton in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: build, pitch, vote, discuss paying. Expect at least one app for learning nothing (an app that does your homework) — allow it, the language still works

76–86′
Word exchange · collocation cards
mingling
Why: Retrieval disguised as movement: verb cards hunt their noun cards until every collocation has passed through everyone's mouth — and the spelling round catches password and virus while the test is still two weeks away.

WHAT WE DO: Quiz-Quiz-Trade with split collocations + a spelling round on the traps

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Sixteen cards: eight verbs, eight nouns, one per student (duplicates fine). Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade: verb-holders hunt noun-holders, say the full collocation together, then trade cards and hunt again. Three minutes, maximum trades.
  2. Sit on a clap. Mini-whiteboard spelling round, the traps: Boards up — password! … virus! … download!
  3. Fix the casualties with a smile: one s in virus, double s in password. These words are in the test in three weeks. Now they're yours.
📺 On screen: printed collocation cards — the cards in their hands are the medium; if the set has gone missing, call the verbs by voice and the class answers with the noun chorally
👦 Students do: hunt, pair, trade, then spell the monsters. Expect password to lose an s somewhere — that is why the round exists

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I made a search» — better verb? → DID a search. English does searches, it doesn't make them.
«upload» or «download» — which direction? → up = from you to the internet; down = from the internet to you. Gesture it every time.
«a virus» — do you install it or have it? → you HAVE it, nobody installs a virus on purpose.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students answer the quiz with yes/no and stopThe frame on the board is non-negotiable: Yes, I have — I deleted my project in Year 5. Point at the frame, wait, get the story. The interview stage dies without this bar.
«make a search», «put photos» — Russian collocations in English clothesThe wall is two colours on purpose: verbs in purple, nouns in yellow, drawn together with a chalk line. Wrong verb → walk to the wall, tap the pair, repeat the true collocation. No lecture.
The matching task collapses into keyword-huntingThe method is in the plan: underline Emre's three needs FIRST, then scan the apps for offers. The traps (D, F, G) all contain shiny keywords — let one pair fall in, fish them out publicly, name the lesson.
Fast finishers in the reading stageTB's own extension: find synonyms in the app texts for awesome, attractive, fairly, allow, huge — they exist and they are exam words.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
