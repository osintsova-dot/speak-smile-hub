🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use about fifty words for the body, illness and medical care — the parts (thigh, waist, forehead, ribs, kidney), what goes wrong (fracture, dislocate, sprain, fever, exhaustion, infectious) and what is done about it (A and E, GP, chemist, prescription, painkiller, physiotherapy, injection) — and read a long article by proving every answer with a line number.
What is genuinely hard
Two different difficulties in one lesson. The vocabulary looks easy and collocates badly: students know «leg» and «pain» but produce «I have a pain in my stomach ache» and «my leg is broken in the ankle». Half the set is also a pronunciation problem rather than a meaning problem — the long and short i (heel / hip, sneeze / sick) and the silent letters in muscle and chemist. The reading is harder than it looks: True/False on a dense text is where a confident class guesses from memory of the topic instead of finding the line, and the book asks for the line number precisely to stop that.
Typical error to expect
«I have a pain in my stomach ache», «my leg is broken in the ankle» — the body and illness set collocates tightly: break a bone, sprain an ankle, dislocate a shoulder, have a headache, run a temperature. The words are easy; the partners are the lesson.
Why the lesson is built this way
The vocabulary is never a list. Parts of the body arrive through the Teacher's Book drawing game — one student draws, the group names — so the word is produced before it is read. Medical care arrives inside two real texts, a health-site page about a dislocated shoulder and a page about flu, so every item is met in a sentence. Then the reading is genuinely worth reading: how you would get clean water and basic medicine if civilisation stopped — boiling, sunlight in a plastic bottle, willow bark, lavender, menthol, and the fact that willow bark became aspirin. The exam task on it is done with a rule that changes behaviour: no line number, no answer.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two and a half thousand years ago Hippocrates told people to bite the bark of a willow tree when they were in pain. He was right — that bark became aspirin. Today: the English for everything that can go wrong with a body and everything a doctor does about it, and then an article about what you would actually do if the hospitals closed.”

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 with track 27: sixteen parts of the body, produced before they are read

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Groups of four. One student from each group comes to the board in turn and draws a part of the body from the box on p.70; the rest of the class name it. The point goes to the group that names it with the correct English term — not to the artist. Sixty seconds per turn, timer visible.
  2. Run six or seven turns, no more: the aim is to surface what they already have, not to cover the list.
  3. Play track 27 once, books open, and drill the four the group will get wrong: heel against hip (long i against short i), muscle with a silent c, forehead, thigh.
  4. Then the sixty-second pronunciation sort from the Teacher's Book, in pairs: fever, liver, ribs, sick, sneeze, lips, treat, knee, kidney, disease — long /iː/ or short /ɪ/? Answers: long — fever, sneeze, treat, knee; short — liver, ribs, sick, lips; kidney and disease carry one of each.
  5. Everything confirmed goes into column one of the wall, written by the student who said it.

standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Seventeen-year-olds who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies quietly.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer on the Teacher's Book question — the room decides with its feet

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two ends of the room: A AND E at one end, THE GP at the other. The middle exists and is discouraged.
  2. You call out a situation — a dislocated shoulder · flu for a week with no improvement · a deep cut that will not stop bleeding · a sore throat since yesterday · a fever of 39 for three days · an ankle you cannot stand on — and everybody walks.
  3. After each walk, one person from each end gives a one-sentence reason using a wall word. Six situations, six walks, twelve sentences, nobody sitting.
  4. Finish with the ex.4 questions as a quick standing Quiz-Quiz-Trade: Have you ever injured yourself playing sport? Have you ever been to A and E? When did you last have a fever?
  5. Freeze and sit: Page seventy-one. Now imagine there is no A and E, and no GP, and no chemist.
📺 On screen: the six situations are called out, not shown — nothing to prepare and nothing to fail
👦 Students do: commit physically to six decisions and produce twelve reasoned sentences with the new lexis

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: what you would actually do without hospitals, then a fast read to test the guesses

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Book closed for thirty seconds. Write dystopia on the board and ask what it means. Take the films and the series they name — there will be several — and get one sentence on why those worlds are frightening.
  2. ex.1 in pairs, three minutes: how and where would you get clean water and basic medicine in a dystopian future? Collect four or five predictions on the board, in their words, without judging any of them.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 with track 30: six statements, and every answer proved by a line number

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Name the rule before anything else, and mean it: Six statements. For each one I want T or F AND the line number. An answer without a line is not an answer today.
  2. Tell them to read all six statements BEFORE they go back to the text, so they know what they are hunting for. Thirty seconds for that alone.
  3. ex.3 individually, seven minutes, clock visible. Weaker readers get the Teacher's Book support written on the board: 1 is in paragraph 2, 2 and 3 in paragraph 3, 4 and 5 in paragraph 4, 6 in paragraph 5. Stronger readers write two extra statements of their own.
  4. Pairs compare — line numbers first, letters second. Two minutes.
  5. Check with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: 1 True (line 15) · 2 False (26–28) · 3 True (33–34) · 4 True (39–41) · 5 False (40–45) · 6 False (50–54). The nominated student reads the line aloud, then says the letter.
📺 On screen: track 30 is the recorded article — play it under the second read for weaker readers if you want it. Backup: the task works entirely on the printed page; if the audio fails, simply give ninety extra seconds of silent reading
👦 Students do: read six statements first, hunt the text alone, then read the proof aloud instead of the answer

WHAT WE DO: ex.4: the underlined words worked out from the sentence, not from a dictionary

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

Why: 🎯 Critical thinkers · which knowledge would you keep

WHAT WE DO: ex.5: the article's practical advice ranked, defended and argued in the unit's vocabulary

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three minutes, silent, individually: go back through the article and list every piece of PRACTICAL advice it gives — wash your hands regularly, a litre of water with a tablespoon of salt and three of sugar for food poisoning, boil or sun-purify water, willow bark for pain and fever, lavender oil as an antiseptic, menthol for pain.
  2. Each student picks the one they would most want to know tomorrow and the one they think is just a good story, and writes one sentence for each with a reason. Rule: every sentence contains a word from the wall.
  3. 🎯 Kagan Timed Pair Share, two minutes each way: read your two sentences; the listener asks one follow-up question — Why that one? Who would it help first?
  4. Then take the disagreement to the floor for four minutes: Of everything in the article, which piece of knowledge would you most want to have if the hospitals closed tomorrow — and which one is just a good story? What makes you say that? Insist on the rule the book keeps repeating — an opinion without a reason does not count.
  5. Correct only two things today: the six collocations and the long and short i. Everything else waits.
📺 On screen: the list of practical advice built on the board by the class as they call it out — chalk only
👦 Students do: rank six pieces of advice, write two justified sentences each, then defend a choice against a partner and against the room

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«A GP» — is that a hospital doctor? → no, the family doctor you see first. And when do you skip the GP and go straight to A and E? → when it is an emergency: a dislocated shoulder, not a week of flu.
«Infectious» — can I catch it from you? → yes. Is a broken ankle infectious? → no. Which words in the flu text tell us it spreads?
«The statement is False. Which line proves it?» → that question, asked six times, IS the exam task. An answer without a line number is not accepted today.

Assumptions: SB p.70–71, syllabus lesson 34, the first lesson of Unit 6 and the first lesson after the winter holidays. Unit 5 (Financial advice) is finished: the modals of obligation and advice — should, must, have to, ought to — are recent and are recycled in the opening on today's topic, not re-taught. Students know some of this vocabulary passively from films; the collocations and the vowel lengths are new. True/False reading tasks were met earlier in the course, so the format is not new — the discipline of quoting the line is. Everyone has the Student's Book; the word wall is on paper.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 10–18 (the reading section — health, sport and lifestyle is one of the four topics that print every single year) and tasks 32–38 (lexical choice).

Health vocabulary is not decoration in the Russian paper: it is the answer bank for the headings task, for the lexical cloze and for the oral card about a healthy lifestyle. What today's lesson adds that a word list never does is the habit the True/False task on the dystopian text enforces — every answer has to be pinned to a line number. That is exactly how EGE task 12–18 is marked: the candidate who cannot point at the line is guessing, and guessing costs four marks in ten minutes.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
