🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Consolidate everything Units 19–20 own — the ten things you read, say/speak/talk/tell, reported speech, the seven thinking collocations, look/see/watch and the past simple passive — and sit the two official Cambridge unit tests in the second half.
What is genuinely hard
Not the language — the mixing. Each piece works in its home lesson; the test shuffles them, and reported speech next to the passive is where forms collide (said it was built · told me it had been…no, was). Retrieval in shuffled order is the skill of the day.
Typical error to expect
Katy said she likes reading · Cosmo said the board will be full — the tense left unshifted after a past reporting verb; Russian keeps the original tense, so the interference is systematic [A2/B1]
He was died in London · The medicine was tasted terrible — was/were glued onto verbs that must stay active; and the mirror error, the film called Now You See It with the was dropped [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
The first half is retrieval, not re-teaching: two stations pull words and grammar out of memory in short bursts, and the official Crazy Sentences! game (Units 17–20 pack, today only the U19–20-skeleton cards) hides revision inside laughter. The briefing names the morning's errors while the test is still ahead — feedback still has time to be used. A real two-minute break protects attention. The test itself is two official Cambridge papers, 63 items in 40 minutes, every format done in class — it measures learning, not surprise.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Everything from the last six lessons, one room, one morning. First we play it back out of your heads — stations, and the silliest writing game Cambridge ever published. Then the official papers. Nothing on them will surprise you; I can name the lesson for every line.”

Why: Crazy Sentences!

WHAT WE DO: the official review game — U19–20 cards only today

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The game, from the publisher's own sheet: Groups of three or more, one cut set of sentence cards per group, a blank sheet per player. A card is read aloud up to its first blank («The man with the colourful ___»); everyone completes the blank IN SECRET at the top of their sheet, folds the paper back and passes it left. Three blanks per card, three folds — then the sheets are unfolded and the crazy sentences are read out. The group votes for the funniest; the teacher may award points for originality, humour, vocabulary, spelling or grammar.
  2. Today's deck: ⚠️ The card set covers Units 17–20. For the BLOCK review pull out the cards whose skeleton is Unit 19–20 language and use only those: the graphic-novel card · «Don't tell me to…» said the… (say/tell!) · the advert-persuaded card · the second-thoughts card · the lose-her-concentration card · the lady who WAS ASKED to leave the cinema (the passive!) · the sticker card. The rest of the pack is saved for the Review 5 lesson, where every card is legal — say so up front.
  3. Eight minutes on the visible timer, four cards per group. One crazy sentence per group read to the class at the end; points for grammar that SURVIVED the silliness.
  4. Slower groups: Slower groups or extra writing: the Dictation version from the teacher's notes — players write the whole sentence, not just the blank.
📺 On screen: the cut card sets on desks; visible timer (Backup: no timer — call the minutes aloud; the game needs only paper and the cards)
👦 Students do: write in secret, fold, pass, howl at the reveal. Expect the «Don't tell me to…» card to produce the best material — it drills say/tell while nobody notices

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Nina said she couldn't imagine life without her phone» — her exact words? → «I can't imagine…»: the shift, backwards.
«The lady was asked to leave» — who asked? → we don't know and it doesn't matter: that is WHY the passive.
«Watch my hands and tell me what you see» — why two different verbs for eyes in one sentence? → moving+long vs simply noticing.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
