🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use the seven talking-about-films words (character, clip, plot, review, series, soundtrack, trailer) in real opinions, follow six recorded conversations for attitude and detail, and reach agreement in a negotiation — offer, reason, counter-offer, deal.
What is genuinely hard
Plot, review and trailer look like international words but are not (сюжет, рецензия, трейлер only half-match); series is singular AND plural; and in the speaking, the hard part is cultural: refusing an offer WITHOUT killing the conversation — no, it's stupid ends a negotiation that it sounds too scary for me — what about…? keeps alive.
Typical error to expect
An opinion with no reason attached — I think reality shows are bad full stop — scores as no opinion at all in the speaking [national exam report]
A one-word reply («Comedy.», «Yes.») does not count as an answer at all: an answer needs a verb in it [national exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
The lesson is one mission announced at minute 5: by minute 85 the class will have chosen ONE film everyone accepts — in English, with reasons. Everything feeds it: the seven words arrive through definitions that are THEMSELVES relative clauses (Thursday's grammar working overtime), the listening trains catching attitudes — the exact skill a negotiator needs — with the strategy pass (underline key words first) taught explicitly, and the recorded model shows two friends doing the task before anyone must. Then the negotiation runs twice: pairs agree on one film, pairs merge into fours and agree AGAIN — double difficulty, same phrases — and the class vote crowns the film night. Delayed error feedback stays after the fluency stages, per the КТП's own note.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Tonight, four films. Your class watches ONE. Who decides? Everyone — and that's the problem. Today you learn the seven words critics use, you listen to six people arguing about screens, and then you negotiate a real film night: offer, reason, counter-offer, deal. No fights. All English.”

Vocabulary · seven film words
pairs → class
Why: The film words arrive through definitions that are THEMSELVES relative clauses — last lesson's grammar earns its keep inside this lesson's vocabulary. The completion task then puts all seven into real sentences.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 match halves (the definitions ARE relative clauses) + ex.2 completions + drill

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1 in pairs, 3 minutes: match the seven words to their definition halves. Key: match halves — and every definition IS a relative clause: 1 e a character is a part which an actor plays · 2 a a clip is a small part taken from a longer film · 3 c a plot is the story · 4 b a review is a report with an opinion · 5 g a series is programmes that use the same characters · 6 f a soundtrack is the music · 7 d a trailer is a short video advertising a new film.
  2. Point at Thursday's glue card: Look at these definitions. A part WHICH an actor plays… programmes THAT use the same characters… You read seven relative clauses and didn't even notice. That's fluency.
  3. Drill the traps: genre /ˈʒɒnrə/ — the g says zh · series /ˈsɪəriːz/ — singular AND plural look the same · character /ˈkærəktə/ — the ch says k · review /rɪˈvjuː/ · documentary /ˌdɒkjəˈmentəri/ — stress on MEN · soap opera — two words — series gets the singular-and-plural warning with one example each way.
  4. ex.2 solo, 4 minutes, pair check. Key: complete: 1 character · 2 series, clips · 3 plot · 4 reviews, trailer · 5 soundtrack.
  5. Kagan RallyCoach micro-round: partner names a real film, you say one sentence about its plot, soundtrack OR characters; swap. Words go onto the wall as they prove themselves.
📺 On screen: the matching is on SB p.82 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: match, notice the grammar inside, complete, apply to real films. Expect plot vs story to need one line (plot = the events as built); expect soundtrack to be everyone's favourite word. Typical error: a review about — of, one choral fix

Listening · six conversations
solo → pairs
Why: Three passes, exam shape, ordinary run: predict the genres, underline the key words, choose — then justify. The justification pass is where listening is actually taught rather than tested.

WHAT WE DO: three passes: genres → strategy → the six questions → justification

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pass 1 — ex.1: seven genres listed, six conversations, one genre left over. Play track 68. Key: track 68 — genres to conversations: 1 horror · 2 reality show · 3 documentary · 4 science fiction · 5 the news · 6 comedy; period drama is the genre NOT used.
  2. Pass 2 setup — ex.2, the STRATEGY stage, 2 full minutes: read the six questions and options, underline key words. Pre-teach: rather · behave · unlikely · creature · set off · showing.
  3. Pass 2 — the six questions, solo answers. Then pairs compare WITHOUT changing answers yet.
  4. Key: track 68 again — the six exam questions: 1 B · 2 C · 3 B · 4 C · 5 C · 6 A. Third pass with pauses: why are the wrong options wrong? Audioscript: TB p.272–273.
  5. Pass 3 — pause after each conversation: Why is A wrong? Who heard the line that kills C? Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together — number twos answer for their pair.
  6. Name the transfer: Conversation 2 — the girl finds reality stars boring to listen to. That's an ATTITUDE. Catching attitudes is tonight's negotiation superpower.
📺 On screen: track 68 in the whiteboard app (Backup: read the six conversations from the audioscript, TB p.272–273, in two voices — the task works identically by ear, one conversation at a time)
👦 Students do: predict genres, underline, choose, justify. Expect items 4 and 5 to need the third pass — agreement questions are the subtlest; that is why they sit next to today's speaking. Typical problem: changing answers during pair compare — the rule says discuss, decide only on replay

🎯 Speaking out · reaching agreement
pairs → fours
Why: The productive outcome: pick three, defend them, drop two — a real negotiation where every offer needs a reason and every refusal a counter-offer. The box phrases are compulsory; the film is theirs.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 grown into a two-round negotiation

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Round 0, solo (1 minute): everyone lists THREE films or shows they'd genuinely watch tonight — the p.80 genre wall helps; real titles allowed and encouraged.
  2. Round 1 — pairs, Kagan Timed Pair Share shape, 4 minutes: agree on ONE of your six. Box phrases compulsory; the deal line must be TRUE when said: I think we're both happy with that.
  3. Round 2 — pairs merge into fours, 5 minutes: two winning films enter, ONE leaves. Deadlock rule on the board: two minutes stuck → each pair drops one film, vote on the rest.
  4. You walk with the log: phrase-dodging, Russian leaks and bulldozing go down SILENTLY.
  5. Each four writes its winner and ONE reason on a slip — the finale needs them.
📺 On screen: the phrases board carries the stage — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: negotiate twice, at rising difficulty, phrases compulsory. Expect round 2 to be genuinely hard — that is the design; the deadlock rule turns failure into procedure. Typical error: No, stupid film — point at the refusal rule, wait for the two-part version

74–84′
Film night final
class
Why: Pairs merge into fours and the negotiation runs again at double difficulty — then the class votes one film for a (real or imaginary) film night. Agreement reached in public is agreement rehearsed.

WHAT WE DO: the fours pitch, the class decides, the deal line closes it

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Each four pitches its film in 30 seconds — the slip's reason plus one film word: Great plot, better soundtrack — and nobody has seen it twice.
  2. One round of challenges: any team may ask any team ONE question (Isn't it too scary for half of us?) — answered with a reason, not a shrug.
  3. The vote — but the winning side must close it properly, all together: Let's decide. I think we're ALL happy with that.
  4. If a real film night is possible, book it; if not, the winner goes on the wall as «the film this class agreed on» — the agreement was the point.
📺 On screen: pitches and votes live in the room — voice is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: pitch, challenge, vote, close the deal in chorus. Expect losing teams to accept the vote gracefully IF the challenge round let them speak — that round is the pressure valve. Typical problem: a pitch that lists no film words — one point off, smilingly

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«a trailer» — do I watch it before or after the film comes out? → before: it advertises. And «a clip»? → any short piece cut from something longer.
«the plot was great, the characters were flat» — did I like the film? → half: the story yes, the people in it no. Two words, two separate opinions.
«No, that's a stupid film» — is the negotiation still alive? → barely: English refuses with a reason and a counter-offer — it sounds too scary FOR ME; what about…?

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The six conversations run too fast for weaker earsThe strategy stage is the seatbelt: key words underlined in advance turn listening into confirmation. And the third pass pauses after every conversation — nobody leaves a question unexplained.
Negotiations flip into Russian when they heat upThe phrases board is the lifeline: point at it silently. The English-first rule pays the point only for English versions — and the performance round makes the phrases the star.
One partner bulldozes the agreementThe rule is built in: a deal needs BOTH voices — I think we're BOTH happy with that must be true to be said. The listener checks: did both speakers offer at least once?
Fours cannot agree at allThat is the good problem — cap it: two minutes of deadlock, then the coin rule (each pair drops one film, vote on the rest). Deadlock handled in English is the skill succeeding, not failing.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
