🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Collect a real speaking sample from every pair in the group — the complete A2 Key Speaking paper — while the rest of the class revises the whole year without needing a teacher.
What is genuinely hard
Running two things at once for an hour. Not the speaking: the room. Who is where, when their turn is, how loud the quiz may be, and what a pair does the moment they come back from your table.
Typical error to expect
A one-word reply («Karate.», «Yes.») does not count as an answer at all: an answer needs a verb in it [national exam report]
«I don't know» / «I haven't got a favourite» and full stop — a bare negative is marked as an unanswered point; it needs development: …but I like chess [exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
The A2 Key Speaking paper is eight to ten minutes per PAIR with an examiner sitting opposite. A group of twelve needs an hour of one-to-one time, and there is no lesson left in the year to give up to it — so it has to run underneath something else. Lesson 71 is the only lesson whose structure survives half the room being busy: an open-book quiz and a speaking carousel both work perfectly well with two people missing at a time, and neither needs a teacher standing over it. The order of pairs is on the board from minute five, so nobody spends the hour waiting to find out when their turn is. Feedback is sixty seconds at the table, immediately, while it is still true — one thing that worked and one thing to fix, and the mark written down after they have gone. This is also the reserve lesson of the year, and the order in which its parts are dropped is fixed in advance so that the decision is never taken in the doorway.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Two things at once today, and you all do both. At my table, in pairs, you do the speaking paper — eight minutes, you talk to me and to each other, and you get sixty seconds of honest feedback the moment you finish. The order is on the board, so you know exactly when you are up. Everyone else: the quiz of the year, books open, ten questions from twenty units, and the team that finds all ten first wins. Then we all talk about the twenty things this year was actually about.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls back the one thing that is needed today rather than everything that was ever taught.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a thirty-second check that the error log survived the weekend, and the bridge into a lesson with two tracks

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and leave the desk empty. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — one line from the log. Open your exercise book at your three lines. Which one did you actually work on? Kagan Timed Pair Share, thirty seconds each way.
  3. Take three answers from three different students and write nothing down — this is a check that the log survived the weekend, not a stage.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Today two things happen at once, and you all do both of them.
📺 On screen: nothing on display and nothing playing — the exercise books on the desks are the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: report on one line of their own log. Expect about two thirds to have done something. Typical problem: someone left the book at home — they still have the three lines in their head, and that is the honest test of whether the lines were any good

How the room works today
board
Why: Two things run at once for an hour, and the only way that survives is if every student knows in advance when their turn is and what they do until then. Five minutes here saves the whole lesson.

WHAT WE DO: the two tracks, the order of pairs, and the three rules that keep them apart

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The order of pairs is already on the board, written before they came in. Find your name. That is when you are up. Nobody is going to surprise you.
  2. The two tracks in two columns: At my table — the speaking paper, eight minutes, in pairs. Everywhere else — the quiz of the year, then the carousel.
  3. Three rules, and they are the whole lesson: the quiz is written, not shouted, and only the runner speaks · a pair coming back from the table joins their team and says nothing about the questions · if you are next, you sit at the front so no time is lost calling you.
  4. Say what the speaking paper is in one sentence, without dressing it up: Eight minutes. You talk to me a little and to each other a lot. The part where you talk to each other is the part that counts most.
  5. And say what the feedback is: Sixty seconds at the table, immediately. One thing that worked, one thing to fix. No number.
📺 On screen: the order of pairs and the two-column plan in chalk, written before the lesson and left up for the whole hour — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: find their name in the order and settle into a team. Expect the visible order to remove almost all of the nerves. Typical problem: somebody wants to swap to be with a friend — the list was made before the lesson and is not negotiable, said once

10–40′
Speaking paper + open-book quiz
teacher's table + teams
Why: The speaking paper needs one pair at a time; the quiz needs no teacher at all. Putting them in the same hour is the only way the speaking sample of the year gets collected without giving up a lesson to it.

WHAT WE DO: pairs 1–4 sit the speaking paper while the rest race through the year with books open

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. At your table. Your table is at the far corner, turned so that the pair sits with their backs to the room. Pairs are called in a fixed order written on the board, so nobody is waiting to find out when their turn is. Sixty-two minutes of lesson time gives six or seven pairs at eight to ten minutes each — enough for a group of twelve to fourteen.
  2. Part 1 first, three to four minutes: Part 1, 3 to 4 minutes: hello, name, age, where you live — with the back-up prompts printed in the frame for anyone who freezes — then the two topic sets. In this test they are Your family and Watching TV, two questions each per candidate, plus one extended response: the oldest person in your family to candidate A, the most interesting programme you have watched this week to candidate B.
  3. Then Part 2, five to six: Part 2, 5 to 6 minutes: the picture page of different places to meet friends goes between the two candidates and they talk to each other for a minimum of one minute and a maximum of two. Then the «Do you think…?» prompts — cinemas fun, shopping centres boring, cafés expensive, city centres dangerous, sport stadiums exciting — then which they like best. Then up to two minutes of follow-up: is it better to meet friends at home or go out, and do you prefer one friend or a group.
  4. Then, before they stand up: Sixty seconds at the table, immediately, while it is still true: one thing that worked and one thing to fix, in that order, and nothing else. Not a mark. Write the mark down for yourself after they have gone. What to note as you listen, because it is the only speaking sample of the year you will have on paper: did they answer with a verb in it, did they ask their partner anything at all in Part 2, and did they keep going after a mistake. Those three predict next year better than accuracy does.
  5. Everywhere else. TB p.234 runs it as an open-book race: the questions go on the board, teams look back through the Student's Book to find the answers, and the first team with all ten correct wins. That is exactly right for today — it sends them through twenty units at speed and it needs no preparation from you beyond writing ten questions on the board. The ten questions are written on the board before the lesson, with the answers kept in your own copy of this plan.
  6. Teams of three or four, one answer sheet per team, one runner who is the only person allowed to speak to you — and you are busy, so the runner writes the team's answers and hands the sheet in at the end.
  7. Collect the quiz at 40 minutes with Kagan Numbered Heads Together once everybody is back together — not now, while the table is still running.
📺 On screen: the ten quiz questions in chalk on the board and the printed speaking paper and picture page on your table — paper and the board are the media here, so no backup is needed; if the picture page has not been printed, the same pictures are in the Student's Book on p.135
👦 Students do: four pairs sit the paper; everybody else hunts through twenty units at speed. Expect the quiz to take about twenty minutes for a fast team and the whole slot for a slow one — both are fine. Typical error at the table: a one-word answer in Part 1, which is the single thing to name in the sixty seconds of feedback

Move break
standing
Why: A stirrer between two heavy stages. Teenagers who have been marking their own mistakes for half an hour need to stand up before they are asked to look at four more.

WHAT WE DO: two minutes to reset between the quiz half and the carousel half

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone up — including the pair at my table, we pause for two minutes.
  2. Kagan Stand Up–Hand Up–Pair Up: one sentence to the person you meet, and it has to start This year I learned how to…
  3. Back down on a clap. The order of pairs continues from where it stopped.
📺 On screen: instructions by voice, nothing to display and no backup required
👦 Students do: stand, move and produce one full sentence about the year. Expect the sentence frame to give better answers than a free question would. Typical problem: the pair at the table wants to keep going — pause anyway, they get their full eight minutes either way

Speaking paper + twenty topics
teacher's table + carousel
Why: The carousel is revision that sounds like conversation: every topic of the year comes back once, spoken rather than read, while the pairs keep coming to the examiner's table.

WHAT WE DO: the remaining pairs sit the paper while the rest run the speaking carousel of the year

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. At your table. The remaining pairs, same frame, same eight to ten minutes, same sixty seconds of feedback. Keep to the clock: the last pair must start by 62 minutes.
  2. Everywhere else. The carousel: two concentric circles, inner faces outer, one topic per rotation, ninety seconds each way, then the outer circle moves one place. Kagan Timed Pair Share is the engine and RallyRobin is what happens when a topic runs dry.
  3. The twenty topics are one per unit and they are on cards, one card per rotation: a challenge you have taken on · a place in nature you would protect · the best holiday you have had · the home you would like to live in · how you would change your school · an object you would never throw away · an adventure holiday you would book · life in twenty years' time · a sport you would defend to somebody who calls it boring · a website you would recommend and why.
  4. And the second ten: the city you would live in · a film everybody should see · something you have never done and want to · what you spend your money on · a hobby you have had for years · the language you would learn next · what you do to stay healthy · a book you would give to a friend · a dish you can cook · the biggest change in your life so far.
  5. One rule for the carousel and it is the language aim of the whole lesson: Every answer has a reason in it. «Because» or sit down.
  6. A pair coming back from the table slots into the outer circle at the nearest gap and says nothing about the questions.
📺 On screen: the topic cards in the students' hands and the printed speaking paper on your table — paper is the medium here, so no backup is needed; if the cards were not cut, the twenty topics go on the board and the circles rotate down the list
👦 Students do: the rest of the pairs sit the paper; everybody else speaks for thirty minutes without a teacher. Expect the carousel to be the loudest and best stage of the term. Typical error: an answer with no reason in it — the «because or sit down» rule is enforced by the partner, not by you, because you are at the table

72–84′
The year in ten questions
whole class
Why: The whole class back together for the finale, so the lesson ends as a group rather than as two half-rooms. Ten questions across twenty units is the fastest possible tour of what the year contained.

WHAT WE DO: everybody back together: the quiz answers, the winning team, and what the year contained

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Collect the quiz sheets and run the answers with Kagan Numbered Heads Together — one question per team, the number called at random so it is not the same three students every time.
  2. The ten questions and where they came from: How many parts are there to the Duke of Edinburgh's Award? (four, SB p.10, Unit 1) · Do most pandas live in zoos or in the wild? (in the wild, SB p.16, Unit 2) · What unusual house do Paula and Gary have? (a lorry, SB p.24, Unit 4) · What exams do sixteen-year-olds in England do? (GCSEs, SB p.40, Unit 5) · Which football team does eSport star Koen Weijland play for? (Ajax, SB p.56, Unit 9).
  3. And the second five: How old was Catherine Cook when she started myYearbook.com? (15, SB p.61, Unit 10) · What is pocket money called in the US? (an allowance, SB p.82, Unit 14) · What language group is English in? (Germanic, SB p.92, Unit 16) · Who is the main character in the book Wonder? (August, SB p.106) · What sport did Kevin Pearce do before his accident? (snowboarding, SB p.114, Unit 20).
  4. Winning team named, and nothing more than named — no points, no wall chart, it is the last week of the year.
  5. Then Kagan Team Statements, one per team, thirty seconds to agree it: One thing this class can do now that it could not do in September. One sentence, and the whole team has to agree with it.
  6. Read the four or five statements out and leave them on the board. They are the closing image of the year and they cost two minutes.
📺 On screen: the ten questions already on the board from the first half, with the answers written in beside them as they come — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: answer the quiz as teams and agree one team statement. Expect the pandas and the lorry to be the two nobody found. Typical problem: an argument about whether an answer counts — the page number is printed next to every question in this plan, so it is settled in five seconds

Wrap-up + homework
circle
Why: Self-assessment against the aim they heard at the start, so the lesson closes the loop it opened. Homework comes out of each student's own paper, not from a common list.

WHAT WE DO: what the speaking showed, the homework for the last test, and the shape of next lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. General feedback on the speaking, and it is genuinely general — no names, no marks, three sentences: what the group did well, the one thing that cost it most, and the one thing that will matter in Prepare 4.
  2. For most groups those three are: full answers with a reason, one-word replies in Part 1, and asking the partner a question back.
  3. Homework named and kept small, because the last test is next: twenty minutes on whichever of your three lines is still open, plus one read-through of the vocabulary list at the back of the book for the last block.
  4. The shape of next lesson, so nobody arrives guessing: Forty-five minutes of fast revision, then the last paper of the year — forty minutes, eleven tasks, everything we did. You have seen every format on it.
  5. And the honest last line: The speaking is done. That was the hardest thing on the list and you have finished it.
📺 On screen: the homework and the shape of next lesson in chalk, copied into diaries — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: hear group feedback and write the homework down. Expect relief that the speaking is behind them. Typical problem: individual «how did I do?» — the answer is that they already had their sixty seconds, and that is the whole feedback

Structure
Do you like these different places to visit? Say why or why not. · Do you think going to a museum is boring? · What about you? · Is it better to meet friends at home or go out with them?
Form
Full answers with a verb in them, and a reason after every opinion. That is the entire language aim of the speaking paper at this level.
Pron. watch
Nothing drilled at the table — it is an exam, not a lesson. In the carousel, one reminder only: the voice goes up at the end of a question, and a question asked back is worth marks.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«Do you like these places?» Is «yes» an answer? → no. It needs a reason after it.
In Part 2, who do you talk to — me or your partner? → your partner. That is what is being marked.
How long do you talk together about the pictures? → at least one minute, at most two.
If you make a mistake in the middle of a sentence, do you stop? → no. Keep going. Stopping costs more than the mistake did.

The reserve ruleThis is also the reserve lesson of the year. Norilsk loses days to weather, and the syllabus has exactly one slot built in to absorb that: this one. If nothing was lost, the freed time goes to the twenty-topic carousel, which is the stage the group will enjoy most and the one that most obviously shows them what a year of language bought.
Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The quiz gets loud enough to drown the speaking tableTwo fixes, both set up before the first pair sits down: the table is at the far corner with the pair's backs to the room, and the quiz is written, not shouted — teams write answers on one sheet and only the runner speaks. If it still climbs, one clap and the count of three.
A pair freezes in Part 1The back-up prompts are printed in the interlocutor frame for exactly this — use them, they are part of the test and not a concession. If it is still stuck, move to the second topic and come back.
Two students refuse to be pairedPair by the list on the board, which was made before the lesson and is not negotiable, and say so once. If it is genuinely impossible, one of them goes with you as the second candidate — the frame allows it and it costs nothing.
A day was lost to weather earlier in the year and something has to giveIf a lesson has been lost earlier in the year, this is where it is made up, and the order in which today's parts are dropped is fixed in advance so the decision is not taken in the doorway: first the speaking carousel on the twenty topics goes (the speaking paper itself does not — it is the only speaking sample of the year), then the open-book quiz shrinks from ten questions to five, then the final whole-class round goes. The speaking paper and the sixty-second feedback are the last things standing.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the OGE — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Speaking tasks 1–3: read a text aloud (2 marks), answer six interview questions (6 marks), give a monologue from a plan (7 marks). 15 marks, about 15 minutes.

Today's speaking paper has a partner in the room and a live examiner. The Russian one has neither: the questions come from a recording and the answers go to a computer. Say that difference out loud to the two or three students it concerns, and to nobody else — for the rest of the group a partner is exactly what makes speaking worth doing.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the OGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
