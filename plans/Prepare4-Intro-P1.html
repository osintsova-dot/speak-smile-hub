🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Hold a one-minute conversation about your summer — the past simple for July, at least one present perfect sentence for experience — and ask a partner three questions without dropping the auxiliary.
What is genuinely hard
Not the grammar — the rust. After ten weeks of silence the group that talked in full sentences in May answers Sochi and stops. The second hard part is subtler: year-two boredom. If lesson 1 replays last September word for word, the strong students check out — so every ritual returns one level up.
Typical error to expect
I go to Sochi for a finished July; Where you went? for a question; and the new one to listen for — I have seen it last week, the present perfect glued to a finished time. Nothing is corrected aloud today; all of it goes on the correction sheet and opens lesson 2.
Why the lesson is built this way
Built backwards from one fact: this is a YEAR-TWO group. The contract is renewed, not rebuilt — the class recalls its own rules and votes one upgrade (two facts per answer). The score and the four structures get two minutes, not ten: one demonstration round tells you whether the choreography survived. The freed time goes where year two needs it — a longer speaking line-up with a present perfect round, and the map hunt with a real destination: the ✓ icon printed in their book, which p.6 names as B1 Preliminary for Schools. That line is said once, calmly, because the syllabus asks for the course path and the book itself prints it. No measurement today; it starts next lesson and saying so out loud is part of the design.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Same room, same rules — one level up. Today you talk to six people about your summer, we renew the four rules this room runs on — with one upgrade — and you find out where this book is actually taking you. Spoiler: page six names a Cambridge exam, and by May you will be closer to it than you think. Nothing is measured today. Next lesson, forty questions — and even those don't go in the register.”

0–5′
Opening · the door and the first sentence
door → desks
Why: The first minute of the year sets the language of the room for nine months. This group already lived one year by the English-first rule — the doorway sentence tells you in sixty seconds how much of that habit survived the summer.

WHAT WE DO: meet them at the door, one English sentence each before anybody sits down

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Stand in the doorway. Nobody comes in silently: Hello! Say one thing you did this summer — one sentence.
  2. Accept anything with a verb in it. One-word answers get the sentence back: Swimming. Good — «I went swimming». Say it.
  3. On the board while they sit down, in your own handwriting: Welcome back. Year two. 72 lessons. 20 units. One exam icon to find.
  4. Bridge (30 seconds): Everybody in this room has just spoken English — after ten weeks off. It is all still there. Today we switch it back on.
📺 On screen: the four lines written up in chalk as they come in — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: produce one sentence each at the door. Expect stronger sentences than a year-one group gives — and expect I go to my grandmother anyway; log it, do not correct it

5–8′
Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the year-two standard

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the old standard and the upgrade next to it: An answer has a verb in it → and TWO facts.
  2. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: today you talk to six people about your summer, we renew our four rules, and you find out exactly where this book is going.
  3. Say what today is NOT: Nothing is measured today. Next lesson there is a paper — forty questions — and it does not go in the register either.
  4. Set the bar: One minute about your summer, without stopping, with one «I have never / I have already» sentence inside. That is the standard by the end of today.
📺 On screen: the standard sentence and its upgrade in chalk — they stay up all lesson and everything is measured against them, so the board is the right medium and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: listen and read the board. Expect somebody to ask about marks — answer now. Expect somebody to announce they forgot everything; agree cheerfully and move on

8–20′
🎯 Line-up · how was your summer
two rows, standing
Why: Two rows facing each other, one question, thirty seconds, then everybody moves one place. In twelve minutes each student has spoken to six different people — and because this is year two, round three quietly adds a present perfect question, which is the first new muscle of the year.

WHAT WE DO: Kagan Mix-Pair-Share in a moving line: six partners, thirty seconds each way — round three upgrades to Have you ever

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two rows facing each other. Kagan Mix-Pair-Share: thirty seconds each way, then the left row moves one place, the last person runs to the front.
  2. Rounds 1–2, the frame on the board: How was your summer? — Last summer I ___. I went with ___. The best thing was ___.
  3. Rounds 3–4, the year-two upgrade: Now you also ask: Have you ever…? Answer with «Yes, I have — I…» or «No, never, but I want to». Two example questions on the board: Have you ever slept in a tent? Have you ever flown alone?
  4. Rounds 5–6, the interview: Three questions each: Where did you go? What did you do? Who did you go with?
  5. Stand at the end of the row so the last pair is always with you. Listen for three things: the missing did, the one-word answer, and the present perfect glued to a finished time.
📺 On screen: the frames and the questions for each pair of rounds on the board, added as the rounds change — the board is the medium here and stays up, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: speak to six different people, twelve turns in twelve minutes. Expect rounds 1–2 slow, 5–6 fast and loud. Typical errors: Where you go? · I have seen it last week — both onto the correction sheet, neither corrected aloud today

20–29′
Who is in this room · year two
threes
Why: The group mostly knows itself, but the summer changed people and there may be a new face. An interview in threes with a «nobody knows this about me» question re-bonds the group in nine minutes instead of a lesson — and gives any new student a scripted, safe way in.

WHAT WE DO: Kagan Three-Step Interview: the summer edition, with a «nobody knows» question

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Threes — mix them yourself so friends are split. Kagan Three-Step Interview: A interviews B while C takes notes; rotate twice.
  2. Three questions on the board, summer edition: What was the best day of your summer — and why? · What is one thing you did for the first time? · What is one thing nobody in this room knows about you?
  3. Then Kagan Team Statements: each three builds ONE sentence true of all three: All three of us ___.
  4. Each three reads its sentence; the best five go on the board.
  5. Keep the note sheets — a class list with something human on it, worth more in October than the register. If there is a new student, this stage is their official arrival: their three is introduced by its OTHER two members.
📺 On screen: the three interview questions written on the board, note sheets are plain paper — nothing here can break down and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: interview, take notes, agree one sentence in threes. Expect the «first time» question to smuggle in perfect-adjacent language — that is the point. Typical problem: the team statement arrives as three separate sentences; push for one

29–39′
The contract · renewed, not rebuilt
class → teams
Why: Rules that a class rebuilt from zero would feel like a lie — they already have rules and know they work. What earns the ten minutes is the renewal: which rules survived, and the one upgrade of the year (two facts per answer). A bar the class raised itself is a bar it will defend.

WHAT WE DO: the class recalls its own four rules, then votes the year-two upgrade in

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Do not show the rules. Ask for them: You lived a whole year by four rules in this room. In your threes — write down as many as you remember. Two minutes.
  2. Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together. Expect all four to come back, worded roughly; write them up as they land:
    • English first — An idea may start in Russian; the point is given for the English version. Last year's rule, unchanged — the room already knows it works.
    • Nobody answers alone — Everything goes to the board through a pair or a team first. The rule that made the room safe last September stays word for word.
    • An answer has a verb in it — and this year, two facts — «Football» was never an answer. This year the bar moves up: an answer is a verb plus two facts. One sentence is fine — both facts fit inside it.
    • The timer is the boss — When the timer runs, we work; when it stops, pens down. The clock is fair to everybody — also unchanged.
  3. Present the upgrade as a decision, not an order: You are a year older. I want to raise one rule. An answer has a verb — AND two facts. Who objects? Take one objection seriously, answer it, then vote.
  4. Everybody signs the A3 again — a new sheet, this year's date. The old sheet, if it survives, goes up next to it: two Septembers side by side.
  5. The four rules into the diary, first page, in English.
📺 On screen: the four rules written large on A3 in marker — paper on the wall is deliberately the medium, because it must survive the year and cannot be switched off, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: reconstruct the rules from memory, argue the upgrade, sign. Expect the recall to be fast and proud — it is their furniture. Typical problem: a student proposes a joke rule; take it, laugh, and ask the class to vote it down themselves

39–45′
Points and structures · the two-minute renewal
board → teams
Why: The score and the four structures carried last year; in year two they need naming, not teaching. Two minutes buys the whole apparatus back — and one demonstration round shows you instantly whether the summer erased the choreography.

WHAT WE DO: the three conditions of a point and the four structures — named, not re-taught

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The three conditions of a point, renewed in one breath: Points are for the TEAM, never for the person. A team gets a point when its answer is in English, has a verb and two facts in it, and every member of the team can repeat it. The third condition is the whole system: it makes the fast student teach rather than shout. The group played by these rules all last year — say only what changed: the two-facts bar.
  2. One reminder of what never scores: What does NOT earn points, still: speed, volume, being first, being right on your own. One reminder sentence is enough in year two.
  3. Then the four structures come back by name: The four structures of last year come back by name, and naming is the point — a structure the class can name is a structure that starts in ten seconds: Think-Pair-Share · Quiz-Quiz-Trade · RallyCoach · Numbered Heads Together. In year two you do not demonstrate them — you call one («Quiz-Quiz-Trade, three minutes») and watch it simply happen. If it does not, THAT is worth the two minutes of re-drilling now, in lesson 1, not in October.
  4. One demonstration round to test the choreography: Kagan RallyRobin, 60 seconds — summer words, one each, back and forth, no repeats. If it starts in ten seconds, move on. If it stumbles, re-drill it now — two minutes here saves six a week.
📺 On screen: the three conditions and the four structure names on the board, going up next to the contract — board and wall are the medium and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: listen, then run one RallyRobin round. Expect it to start almost instantly — and if it does, say so out loud: the room just proved the year of training held. Typical problem: they demand points for the demo round; let them have it, it costs nothing

WHAT WE DO: two minutes of movement and the switch from the group to the book

The map of the year · SB p.4–9 + the B1 line
pairs → class
Why: The book answers the questions instead of the teacher — a hunt through the contents pages gives them the shape of the year in a form they will remember. And this year the hunt has a destination: the exam icon is printed in their own book, and the key on p.6 names B1 Preliminary for Schools. Saying that line once, calmly, is the whole «course path» point of the syllabus.

WHAT WE DO: a six-question hunt through the contents pages — ending at the exam icon

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books open at p.4. Set the year-two hunt: Do not read the map to them — they hunted through the same map a year ago, so make the hunt harder and self-referential: Find the unit about sport — what page? · Find the unit where you learn to write a biography. · Find THREE grammar lines that you already know from last year. · How many units are about technology and science? · Find the page where the year is checked — there are five of them. · Find the little red exam icon — what exactly does the key on page 6 say it means? Six minutes, in pairs, answers written down.
  2. Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together. Then name what they found: What they should be able to say at the end of the hunt, in their own words: twenty units, four pages each; a check spread after every four units (Review 1 p.30 · Review 2 p.52 · Review 3 p.74 · Review 4 p.96 · Review 5 p.118); Extra activities p.120; the Vocabulary list p.128; Grammar reference and practice p.138; the irregular verb list p.158.
  3. Two minutes on p.8–9: SB p.8–9 names the features that come back in every unit — About you · Prepare to write · Prepare to speak · Talking points · the exam-task icon · Video. Five minutes is enough in year two: the class knows most of them; what is genuinely new to point at is the icon key on p.6.
  4. Be honest about what is not in our year: ⚠️ Two things on the map are NOT in our year, and say so now rather than let them find out in October: Culture (five spreads: Traditional clothes p.18, New York p.40, British food p.62, Film industry p.84, Special training p.106) is out — seventy-two lessons will not hold it without cutting language — and the videos live in the interactive whiteboard app, so they appear only when the room has it. Life Skills comes inside review lessons.
  5. And the destination line — once, calmly, pointing at the icon key on p.6: The line to say about the exam icon, once, calmly — it is the «course path» line the syllabus asks for: See this little icon? Page six says what it means: B1 Preliminary for Schools exam task. That is a Cambridge exam, and this book quietly trains you for it all year — every unit has one or two tasks in exactly that format. Prepare 3 aimed at A2 Key. This book aims one level higher. We are not sitting the exam this year — we are becoming the people who could.
  6. Close the stage with one pencil dot: Which unit are you most looking forward to? Dot it. Take four answers and remember them.
📺 On screen: SB p.4–9 on the screen so the whole class hunts on the same page (Backup: the map is printed in their own books on p.4–9 — pairs work from the paper page and the six hunt questions go on the board)
👦 Students do: hunt in pairs, report by number, find the icon. Expect the «three grammar lines you already know» question to produce the best moment — the map proves the year is not starting from zero. Typical problem: they read the map instead of searching it; a time limit per question stops it

Goals 2.0 · three lines that stay in the folder
solo → pairs
Why: A goal a student wrote themselves and can see in December is the only kind that survives. Year-two twist: the first line starts from what they could NOT do in May — which they remember precisely.

WHAT WE DO: three written goals in English, anchored in what May actually looked like

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand out one card each. Three frames on the board: In May I still could not ___ — by this May I will. · The mistake I most want to stop making is ___. · One English thing I will do outside this room is ___.
  2. Three minutes alone, in silence. Then Kagan Timed Pair Share: one minute each way, read your card to your partner.
  3. Make the goals concrete: «Speak better» is not a goal. «Talk for two minutes without switching to Russian» is a goal. Rewrite anything vague on the spot.
  4. Collect the cards into the folders — they are needed in December and in May.
  5. Read three out anonymously. Hearing that somebody wants the same thing is worth more than any encouragement from you.
📺 On screen: the three sentence frames on the board and blank cards on the desks — paper and chalk are the medium on purpose, because the cards must survive nine months, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write three goals alone, read them to one partner. Expect the first frame to work far better than last year's version — May is a real memory, not a guess. Typical error: goals in Russian; give the frame again and let the Russian stay underneath

69–84′
🎯 Speaking out · three true things and a lie
groups of 4
Why: The productive outcome of lesson 1 is spoken and personal: four sentences about your own summer, one of them a lie, said to people listening for a reason. The compulsory present perfect sentence turns a party game into the first grammar rep of the year.

WHAT WE DO: four summer sentences, one false, one compulsory present perfect — the group votes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Explain the mechanic in thirty seconds: Four sentences about your summer: three true, one false. The group hears all four, then votes. One point for spotting the lie, one point for every person you fool. The listeners may ask one question each before voting — and that question is the part that makes it a language task rather than a party game. Year-two upgrade: at least ONE of the four sentences must use the present perfect (I have never… / I have already…).
  2. Name the source and the promise: Mechanic taken from the official Cambridge review game «I think you're lying!» (course folder → materials → Review Games Worksheets → PREPARE_4_Review_game_Units_01-04.pdf). ⚠️ The printed grid is Units 1–4 language, which the group meets across September, so in lesson 1 we use the MECHANIC with their own summer sentences — and the printed grid itself comes back officially in lesson 8, the block 1 review. Tell them so: «this game returns with the book's own card in a month» is a promise that lands.
  3. Three minutes to write four sentences. Check the constraint on the board: All about this summer. Three true, one false. At least one sentence starts «I have never» or «I have already».
  4. Play in fours, four rounds: hear all four sentences, one question each from the listeners, then the vote. Spot the lie = 1 point; every person fooled = 1 point.
  5. Teams total their points and shout the number — first scores of the year. Point at the three conditions on the board and check they were met.
  6. Log errors silently; correct nothing aloud. The feedback slot is the first three minutes of lesson 2.
📺 On screen: the scoring line written on the board (spot the lie = 1 · fool a person = 1) — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write four sentences and play four rounds. Expect the lie to be the most fluent sentence anybody produces all lesson — say so out loud. Typical error: I have been to Turkey last July — the exact collision lesson 3 needs; it goes on the sheet, not on the air

Structure
Last summer I went to Kazan and stayed for three weeks. · I have never flown in a helicopter. · Where did you go? Who did you go with? Have you ever…? · By May I want to be able to talk for two minutes without stopping.
Form
past simple for finished July against present perfect for experience up to now (I went last summer · I have never tried); questions with did and with have you ever, and the verb forms after each.
Pron. watch
the two weak forms that hide the new grammar — have as /əv/ in I've never… and was as /wəz/. Say them naturally and let the class copy; do not teach the symbols.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I have seen that film last week.» Is last week finished? → yes. So which tense? → past simple: I saw it.
«I have never been to Moscow.» Are we talking about one finished time? → no, about my whole life up to now — that is why it is have been.
«Where you went?» Which word is missing? → did. And what happens to went? → it goes back to go.
ICQ before the line-up: How long do you speak? (thirty seconds) What happens when the timer stops? (this row moves one place) What must round three include? (a Have you ever question)
«Two facts.» Is I went to Sochi with my grandmother one fact or two? → two — where AND who with. Both fit in one sentence.

Assumptions: Syllabus lesson 1, the first lesson of the year. The group finished Prepare 3 with us in May: both past tenses, the present perfect and must/have to are all old friends, rusty but present. Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
«We did all this last year» — the year-two groanIt is a fair objection, so beat it to the punch in the frame: Same rules, higher bar. Last year an answer needed a verb. This year it needs a verb and two facts. Every returning ritual must visibly cost more than last September, and then the groan never comes.
One-word answers for the first twenty minutesExpected. Do not ask for more — give the sentence and have it repeated: Sochi. Good — now the whole thing: «I went to Sochi with my grandmother.» Say it. Three of those in the line-up and the room self-corrects.
A new student who knows nobody and understands littlePut them second in the line-up so they hear five answers before giving one, and make them the note-taker in the threes. Do not simplify out loud; lesson 2's paper will tell you what they actually have.
«Are we sitting the Cambridge exam this year?»Answer it straight — it will come up the moment the icon is explained: Not this year. This book trains you for it; whether and when to sit it is a conversation for you, your parents and me in the spring. What we do this year is become the people who could pass it.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
