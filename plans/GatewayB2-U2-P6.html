🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Work through the four Exam success formats for Units 1–2: a multiple-choice cloze (eight items), personal questions answered and peer-assessed, an eight-item three-option listening, and a guided essay of 140–190 words with three given points.
What is genuinely hard
The cloze is not a vocabulary test but a collocation test: all four options are real words and only one keeps company with the word next to it. The essay is hard for a different reason — students write their opinion and forget the given points, or write 110 words and lose marks before the content is read.
Typical error to expect
An essay of 110 words when 140–190 were asked for — in both exams the word count is a hard limit, and an answer under it is marked down before anything else is read.
An essay that answers only two of the three points, and a conclusion that introduces a new argument — both are named in the Teacher's Book and both are the commonest deductions.
Why the lesson is built this way
The lesson is deliberately not a test: each of the four formats is preceded by the strategy that makes it work, and followed by a short reflection on whether the strategy helped. The speaking stage uses peer assessment — one student plays the examiner with four written criteria and marks 1 to 4 — because the fastest way to understand a mark scheme is to apply it to somebody else. The essay is analysed before it is written (how many words? what topic? which three points?), planned in five paragraphs and begun in class; the finishing is homework and it is peer-read at the start of the next unit.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Today there is no new grammar. Today you find out what these two units are worth when the questions come in exam shape. Four formats, ninety minutes, and one essay you will actually finish.”

Why: Every student says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls the previous lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, the Unit 2 test returned quietly, one correction each, then straight into Exam success

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. One question each at the door, one full sentence back: One sentence — what did you do at the weekend? Any content counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 2 minutes — the test comes back. Hand back the Unit 2 papers face down with the score written. Two minutes to look, in silence.
  3. 1 minute — one correction each. Think-Pair-Share: one thing you got wrong, said correctly to your partner. That is the whole post-mortem.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Page thirty. Today is different: no new grammar, four exam formats, and the first real essay of the year.
📺 On screen: nothing on screen. Backup: if the papers are not marked, run a 2-minute Quiz-Quiz-Trade on the Unit 2 word cards instead and hand the tests back next lesson.

Reading · multiple-choice cloze
desks
Why: The cloze is a collocation test disguised as vocabulary, and students only see that when they are made to name the word next to the gap.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.30 ex.1–2: «Playing for the team», eight gaps, four options each

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the exam tip and turn it into one instruction: First read the whole text for the main idea. Then, at each gap, look at the word immediately before and after it.
  2. Ex.1 in pairs, ninety seconds: who is the text written for, and what is its main point? (probably new university students; the roles people play in a team).
  3. Ex.2 alone, six minutes.
  4. RallyCoach: A explains 1–4, B explains 5–8. The explanation must name the neighbouring word.
  5. Whole-class check with the reason. Key: 1 C practice (a good practice) · 2 B made (be made up of) · 3 D worth (it's worth + -ing) · 4 A up (come up with) · 5 A building (building ideas together) · 6 B tends (tends TO be) · 7 D final (final result) · 8 C part (play a part).
  6. Name the pattern out loud: Six of those eight were collocations. Not meaning — company.
📺 On screen: the text on the board app if you have it, with the gaps numbered. Backup: it is printed on p.30 — work from the page.
👦 Students do: read for gist in pairs, answer eight items alone, then explain half of them to a partner

22–30′
What kind of team player are you
desks
Why: A reading text that stays a reading text is a wasted eight minutes; this stage also prepares the personal-question speaking that follows.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.30 ex.3: the text turned into a conversation

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Elicit the four roles from the text: the contributor (ideas), the collaborator (builds with others), the communicator (keeps it running), the challenger (asks the hard questions).
  2. Think of a group project you actually did this year. Which of the four were you? What did that cost the team?
  3. Timed Pair Share, one minute each way, then three answers to the class.
  4. One extension question if the room is talking well: Which role is missing in this class?
📺 On screen: the four roles on the board. Backup: dictate them.
👦 Students do: identify their own team role and justify it with an example

30–40′
Speaking · personal questions with a peer examiner
desks in threes
Why: Applying a mark scheme to somebody else is the fastest way to internalise it, and the three-role rotation keeps every student busy in every round.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.30 ex.4–5: three question sets, and one student holds the criteria

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the speaking tip and reduce it to one rule: Never a one-word answer. Reason, then example.
  2. Groups of three: one candidate, one examiner, one observer. The examiner asks one of the three sets (people you know · things you like · places you go). The observer holds the four criteria and marks 1 to 4.
  3. The four criteria, boarded as questions: did they use correct grammar and vocabulary? did they speak clearly? did they answer the actual question? did they explain their answers?
  4. Rotate twice so everyone is a candidate once. Three minutes per round.
  5. Debrief in one question: What was the difference between the best answer you heard and the weakest? Expect: an example.
📺 On screen: the four criteria on the board for the whole stage. Backup: write them on slips, one per group of three.
👦 Students do: answer personal questions, mark a partner against four criteria, and observe
SB p.30 · board or criteria slips
40–43′
Move break · agree or disagree, fast
open floor
Why: The stirrer doubles as the brainstorm for the essay: the arguments the students will need are generated on their feet.

WHAT WE DO: three minutes standing, recycling the negotiating phrases

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two walls: AGREE · DISAGREE. Read a statement about study and work, students walk, and one student in each wall says the reason in one sentence.
  2. Five statements: everybody should go to university · a gap year is a waste of time · teamwork is better than working alone · the best learning happens at work · exams measure nothing.
  3. Keep it fast — these are the essay's own arguments and they will be needed in twenty minutes.
📺 On screen: two wall labels. Backup: hands up left / right.
👦 Students do: walk to a wall five times and give one-sentence reasons
two A4 wall labels
43–56′
Listening · eight questions, eight situations
desks
Why: Eight separate short items are exhausting unless the strategy is explicit; the pre-reading and the second-hearing rule are the whole skill.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.144 ex.7 (track ES1): three options each, heard twice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Read the listening tip and turn it into a habit: Read all three options BEFORE the recording starts. Think about what vocabulary you expect to hear.
  2. Give them ninety seconds of silent reading of the eight questions. This is the stage students skip and it is the stage that earns the marks.
  3. Play track ES1 once, straight through, no pausing.
  4. Thirty seconds in pairs — compare only, do not argue.
  5. Play again. Second time: check the two options you rejected, do not start again from nothing.
  6. Check with Numbered Heads Together. Key: 1 B · 2 B · 3 A · 4 B · 5 A · 6 C · 7 C · 8 B.
  7. For the two items the room got wrong, replay ten seconds each and let them hear the line that decides it.
📺 On screen: track ES1 in the board app, played twice. Backup: the audioscript is in the Teacher's Book — read the eight extracts aloud twice, changing your voice for each speaker.
👦 Students do: read the options first, listen twice, then answer for the team
track ES1 · SB p.144 · TB (audioscript)
56–68′
The essay task, taken apart
desks → board
Why: Almost every deduction in a guided essay is a planning failure, not a language failure, so the planning gets twelve minutes and the writing gets the rest.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.144 ex.8: three questions about the task before a word is written

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board the three questions the Teacher's Book uses and take the answers from the class: a How many words? b What is the topic? c What three points must you include?
  2. Answers: 140–190 words · whether it is important for everyone to go to university · getting a good education, learning as you work, and your own idea.
  3. Then build the five-paragraph plan together, on the board: 1 introduce the topic and state your view · 2 a good education · 3 learning as you work · 4 your own idea · 5 conclusion.
  4. Feed the linkers and drill them for sixty seconds: Firstly · In addition · On the other hand · In my opinion · To sum up.
  5. The rule about the conclusion, said once and remembered all year: A conclusion adds no new argument. It agrees with what is above it.
  6. Two minutes in pairs: decide your own third point, and say it aloud to your partner as one sentence.
📺 On screen: the five-paragraph plan on the board, left there for the writing. Backup: dictate it.
👦 Students do: answer three questions about the task, build a five-paragraph plan, choose their own third point
SB p.144 · board
68–80′
🎯 Write the introduction and two paragraphs
desks
Why: Writing under a timer with a visible plan is the only rehearsal of exam conditions that also teaches something; the peer check is limited to one criterion so it happens.

WHAT WE DO: twelve silent minutes with the plan on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Twelve minutes. Silent mode. Paragraphs one, two and three. No questions during the timer.
  2. The visible requirement: Two linkers from the board, underlined.
  3. Walk the room once and put a pencil dot where a paragraph starts without a linker. No commentary.
  4. At eleven minutes, one warning: One minute. Finish the sentence you are in.
  5. Then ninety seconds of RallyCoach: swap papers and check ONE thing — is each paragraph about one of the three points and nothing else?
📺 On screen: a visible timer and the five-paragraph plan. Backup: a phone stopwatch on the desk.
👦 Students do: write three paragraphs in silence, then check a partner's structure

EGE parallel · the same essay, one size up
desks
Why: The transfer is made explicit exactly once, at the moment when the students have just done the smaller version — which is when it costs six minutes instead of a lesson.

WHAT WE DO: six minutes: what changes when the same task is Russian

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Say the three differences and no more: Two hundred to two hundred and fifty words. The three points come as a table of numbers, not as a list. And there is one extra paragraph — the problem and its solution.
  2. Show the shape on the board next to today's plan, so they see it is the same skeleton with one box added: 1 opening · 2 two or three facts from the table · 3 a comparison · 4 a problem and a solution · 5 conclusion.
  3. One question to the room: Which of your five paragraphs today would become paragraph two?
  4. Then stop. This is a language course. The students who need the full Russian format have the trainer link in the homework; the rest have just written a good essay.
📺 On screen: the two plans side by side on the board. Backup: draw them as two columns of five and four boxes.
👦 Students do: compare the two essay plans and identify the one extra paragraph

Form

Pron. watch
linking and weak forms in the listening: «what he'd have» /wɒt id əv/ — the reason a candidate «did not hear» the answer is usually that it was unstressed
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«It's worth …» — what form comes next? → -ing. Always.
In the essay: how many words? → 140 to 190. What happens at 120? → marks are lost before anything is read.
ICQ before the listening: how many options? → three. How many times will you hear each one? → twice, and each item is separate.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students treat the lesson as a test and go silentIt is not a test and you say so in the first minute: every section is preceded by the strategy and followed by a debrief. Nothing today goes in the register.
The cloze is answered by translationKill it on item 1: all four words mean something plausible, and the answer is decided by the word next to the gap. Ask WHY for every item, and the answer must name the neighbouring word.
The peer examiner marks everything 4Give the criteria as questions, not adjectives: did they use a full sentence? did they give a reason? did they give an example? could you understand every word? A yes/no question is harder to inflate.
The essay is 100 words and finishes with a new ideaBoth are pre-empted at the analysis stage: the word count goes on the board, and the five-paragraph plan makes the conclusion a box that has to be filled with what is already above it.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 38 (the opinion essay, 200–250 words) and tasks 1–9 (listening) — this lesson is the closest match to the Russian exam in the whole term.

Say the word count out loud twice: an essay under the limit is capped in both exams.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
