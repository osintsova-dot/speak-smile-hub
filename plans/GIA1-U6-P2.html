WHAT WE DO: Quiz-Quiz-Trade on review: entry; rewind of P1 woven into the topic: I describe a room — they draw it (ex.1) and check with a neighbour

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Entry: Fist bump — one furniture word each, no repeats!
  2. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with the unit's printed cards (handout GIA1-QQT: the question on top, the answer on the back after folding; no printout — kids write their own cards in 2 min).
  3. Commands: Stand up, hand up, pair up! Quiz your partner — praise — TRADE cards — find a new partner! 3–4 exchanges, EVERYONE asks at the same time.
  4. Rewind P1 = ex.1 p.74: I read the room description aloud twice, they draw it as a sketch for 90 sec.
  5. Show your partner. Same picture? Where's the ball? Where's the guitar? Key from the text: bed between the door and a desk · laptop on the desk · chair in front of it · window above the desk · ball under the bed · school bag near the door with a guitar behind it.
  6. Bridge: You just drew a room from words. Now: three London rooms you must ESCAPE from.
📺 On screen: SB p.74 ex.1 (I read it) · sheets for drawing
👦 Students do: they draw the room by ear and check — the P1 prepositions work right away. Expected: mismatches in “behind/near” — the perfect excuse to bring back the anchor gestures. Typical error: guitar under instead of behind — I re-read the line.

Entry into the text: what an escape room is
🎯 Pre-read gate · what's actually going on
desks · crews

WHAT WE DO: a quick entry into the text: ex.2 (rooms and furniture in the guide's photos) + ex.3 (why “escape room”) + a bet on Fun facts

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ex.2 90 sec: Photos A–C. What kind of rooms and furniture can you see? Expected: a living room with a sofa and books · a museum hall with statues and a vase · a room with a lamp, a desk, papers on the wall.
  2. Ex.3 60 sec: Read ONLY the box above the title. Why do we call it an escape room? Key: escape room = you’re in a room and have to find clues and solve puzzles to get out.
  3. Bet (Fun facts): Which country invented escape rooms — Japan, the USA or Greece? Crews, write your bet. Answer: the idea is from Japan (the first real room — Kyoto, 2007); Athens has a three-hour escape room.
📺 On screen: SB pp.74–75 (photos and box) · board: crew bets
👦 Students do: they look over the photos, read only the box, make their bet. Expected: an argument Japan/USA — Fun facts settles it. Typical error: they start reading the whole text — a stop gesture: “box only”.

13–22′
Jigsaw race: three rooms, three crews
⚡ Jigsaw race · three rooms, three crews
crews → mixed

WHAT WE DO: each crew reads ONE room and becomes the expert; ex.4 photos↔texts; ex.5 a “in which room…” race in mixed groups

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. I give out roles: crew 1 — Sherlock, 2 — Wizarding School, 3 — Room 33. Read ONLY your room. Three minutes. You are the expert — nobody else reads it.
  2. Each expert writes down 3 facts: the place · the time of the game · the main problem.
  3. Ex.4 as a whole class in 60 sec: Match photos A–C with texts 1–3. Key: A – 2 (a witch’s hat on the sofa, books) · B – 3 (a vase, museum, lasers) · C – 1 (Sherlock’s hat, papers on the walls).
  4. Ex.5 in MIXED trios (one expert from each room), 4 min: 6 questions, the expert whose room it is answers. Key: 1 Room 33 · 2 The Wizarding School · 3 Sherlock: The Game Is Now (Dr Watson needs your help) · 4 The Wizarding School · 5 Sherlock: The Game Is Now · 6 Room 33.
  5. Kagan mode: Numbered Heads — I call a number, that person answers for the trio.
📺 On screen: SB p.75 (guide) · crew role cards · timer
👦 Students do: they read only their own piece, exchange facts in mixed trios — everyone speaks, because only they know their room. Expected: 6/6 in ex.5. Typical error: the expert retells in Russian — “facts in English or no point”.

22–31′
Challenge: true/false with proof
🔥 Challenge · true/false with proof
desks · crews

WHAT WE DO: subskill step by step → ex.6 (6 statements) with a compulsory proof line; Challenge TB: answer in a full sentence

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Subskill on the board, 4 steps (SB p.74): 1 read the sentence · 2 find the place in the text · 3 read it carefully · 4 same = true, different = false.
  2. Six statements. Every answer needs the LINE. No line — no point. Five minutes, crews.
  3. Check: the crew names T/F and reads the line aloud. Key: 1 True (para 1) · 2 True (para 3) · 3 False — the museum is empty (para 4) · 4 False — the game starts in a shop (para 2) · 5 False — Sherlock is 100 minutes (para 2) · 6 True — monster + lasers (para 3 and 4).
  4. Challenge (TB p.85): we correct false ones with a FULL sentence — False. Sherlock: The Game Is Now is 100 minutes, not 60.
  5. Fast finishers (TB p.86): write 2 of their own T/F — I'll take them for the revenge quiz in pair 6.
📺 On screen: SB p.74 ex.6 · board: 4 subskill steps (stay up the whole unit) · timer
👦 Students do: they hunt for proof in the text and read out the lines. Expected: 5–6 out of 6 when working step by step. Typical error: “well it says so there” with no line — 0 points, the crew searches again.

Game with words from the text
Word work game · 6 words from the text
crews · standing

WHAT WE DO: Timed RoundRobin: ex.7 definitions ↔ words (in context, not with a dictionary) + ex.8 gap-fill; ▶ Hot seat for speed

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ex.7 working rule (TB p.86): Read the WHOLE sentence with the bold word first. Then guess. 3 min. Key: 1 lost · 2 vase · 3 clues · 4 emperor · 5 monster · 6 crime.
  2. Ex.8 blitz down the crew chain: 1 crime · 2 monster · 3 emperor · 4 lost · 5 vase · 6 clues.
  3. ▶ Hot seat (Activities bank TB p.6): one person from the crew with their back to the board, the crew explains the word WITHOUT translation and without related words. 6 words, 60 sec, rotation — everyone gets a turn in the seat.
  4. I fix the pronunciation: vase /vɑːz/ · EMperor · CLUES.
  5. This is Kagan Timed RoundRobin: strictly round the circle against the timer, one turn — one person, no skipping: Clockwise, one each — keep it moving, no skips!
📺 On screen: SB p.74 ex.7–8 · word cards for Hot seat
👦 Students do: they work out the meanings from context, explain words to their own team for speed — everyone speaks in turn. Expected: 6/6 after Hot seat. Typical error: “monster — it's a monster” as an explanation — I ban related words.

Checkpoint · hunting for -ing
desks · crews

WHAT WE DO: checkpoint of lesson A: we find 6 present continuous forms in the text and formulate a hypothesis, “why is this bit of text written like this”

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hunt! Find every verb in the text that looks like IS + -ING. Ninety seconds, crews. Underline them.
  2. I collect on the board: Sherlock Holmes is travelling in another country · you're looking for something different · you're standing in the living room · someone is watching you · a monster is waiting for you.
  3. Hypothesis question (TB p.87 Warmer): Why isn't Sherlock at home in Baker Street? When is this — now or every day? → now. That tense has a name. Next lesson you get its licence.
  4. We write the hypothesis down in one line in the notebook — we'll come back to it in pair 3.
📺 On screen: SB p.75 · board: a column of found is + -ing
👦 Students do: they hunt for the form themselves and formulate “it's about now”. Expected: they find 4–6. Typical error: they underline “looking for” without is — “where's the be?” — that will be the target of pair 3.

Critical thinking · why it's popular

WHAT WE DO: Remember (what they do to escape) + Analyse: ranking 6 reasons for popularity in trios (▶ In my opinion, TB p.6)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Remember (LOT) 2 min: What do people do to escape from each room? Key TB p.86: Sherlock — solve a crime · Wizarding School — find a lost object · Room 33 — travel in time to get a vase.
  2. Analyse (HOT): 6 ideas from the book (real-life games · fantasy · the people you play with · a different world · the time limit · puzzles) — trios rank their TOP-3 in 3 min.
  3. ▶ In my opinion (Activities bank TB p.6): everyone in the trio defends their position for 30 sec, then the trio produces a SHARED number one. Frame: In my opinion, … is number one because …
  4. Round-up for the class: 3 trios name their number one (+1 for an argument with because).
📺 On screen: SB p.74 Critical thinking · board: 6 ideas as a list · the “In my opinion…” frame
👦 Students do: they rank and argue in trios — all three speak against the timer. Expected: “the people you play with” wins unexpectedly. Typical error: a top choice with no because — the “because…?” gesture

Create · designing your own room
trios

WHAT WE DO: STEP “Create” (HOT): the trio builds its own escape room using 5 questions from the SB + a frame of model sentences (TB p.86)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. I put the frame on the board: The name of our escape room is … · It's for (three/four) players. · The players are in (place) in (time). · The idea of the game is to … · The problem is that … · To escape, players … · Be careful! There's a surprise!.
  2. 5 questions from the SB: Where are the players? When is the story happening? What's the problem? What do players do to escape? Any surprises?
  3. Roles in the trio: Storyteller (plot) · Room designer (room + furniture + prepositions!) · Puzzle master (puzzle and surprise). 8 min.
  4. Requirement for the room designer — at least 3 prepositions: The key is UNDER the table, BEHIND the picture, ON the ceiling.
  5. Discussion in Russian is allowed for 60 sec, writing — only in English (TB p.86).
📺 On screen: board: a frame of 7 sentences + 5 questions · A3 sheets for room plans
👦 Students do: they build their own room with roles assigned — everyone has their own piece of the product. Expected: plots about school and dungeons. Typical error: a plan with no prepositions — that's exactly what the designer's role is for, I remind them personally.

Product: pitching the escape room to investors
🏁 PRODUCT · pitch to the investors
stage

WHAT WE DO: finale: a 60-sec escape-room pitch; they vote “would buy a ticket”; judges by the rubric; parallel rotation — everyone performs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Round 1 (everyone at once): trios pair up two by two — pitch to each other, the listening trio judges by the rubric. Rotation ×2.
  2. Round 2 for the class: 3 trios go “on air”, Sixty seconds — sell your room!
  3. Ticket voting: everyone has 2 “ticket” stickers, sticks them on the poster of the room they'd go to (can't put both on your own).
  4. Judges: fluency / accuracy / richness 1–3; a separate bonus +2 for 3+ prepositions in the room description. Gold run = +5.
📺 On screen: stopwatch · judging cards · “ticket” stickers · room posters on the wall
👦 Students do: everyone pitches in mini-rounds, then listens to the finalists and votes with tickets. Expected: 1–2 gold runs, an argument over “tickets”. Typical error: a pitch only about the plot, with no room — the judge deducts richness, the team fixes it themselves in round 2.
🏆 The bar for the product (the judges' rubric, each pair): Fluency — pauses no longer than 3 sec (1–3) · Accuracy — present continuous in full, be is NOT dropped, -ing by the rule (1–3) · Richness — prepositions of place, clothes, colours, connectors (1–3). 9/9 = “gold run”, +5 to the leaderboard.		stopwatch · stickers

WB Unit 6 (pp.44–51) · notebook · points to the leaderboard next pair
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, learners can answer true/false questions with a reason from the text (subskill) and pitch their own escape room in 60 seconds using the unit frame.
  • Subsidiary. The guide “The great escape!” is done as a jigsaw quest, not frontally; Word work (clues · crime · emperor · lost · monster · vase) is drilled with a game; critical thinking Remember→Analyse→Create; a hunt for present continuous in the text — a loaded gun for pair 3.
  • Personal. The text is long and “magazine-style” — cut it into a quest and don't let them read it whole, frontally. The main prize of the lesson isn't translation but the habit of BACKING UP an answer with a line from the text: that's half the test and the whole exam.
By the end, students can…
  • find the line that proves a true/false answer
  • use 6 word-work items in new sentences
  • compare three escape rooms and choose with a reason
  • pitch an escape room idea to the class in 60 sec

Structure
In this game, you're a time-traveller. · The players are in a museum in 1675. · The idea of the game is to find the vase. · That's false — the text says the museum is empty.
Form
true/false + REASON: we quote the line (“the text says…, line 7”); Create frame: The name of our escape room is… / It's for … players. / The problem is that…
Pron. watch
vase /vɑːz/ (not “vayz”) · clues /kluːz/ · monster /ˈmɒnstə/ · emperor /ˈempərə/ — stress on the first syllable.
Concept checks (CCQs) · Assumptions
“Is ‘The museum is empty’ true or false in the text?” → We don't guess — we find the line. (para 4)
“What are clues?” → things that help you solve a puzzle.
“Escape room — you escape FROM the room or you rent it?” → you find clues and get out.

Assumptions: Escape rooms are known to them from life/video games — the content sells itself. The gap: backing up an answer with a line and a long text without panic. Gate is diagnosis, not humiliation: the ceiling must be REAL (-ing spelling / missing ‘be’ / question word order), results are private tallies, the reveal is “so THIS is our growth zone”.

Classroom management — signals
«Eyes up in 3-2-1» — count down calmly → all eyes on you at «1». No shouting over noise, ever.
Visible timer — timer on screen = we work; timer stops = pens/hands down. The timer is the boss, not you.
Volume dial — arm up/down = louder/quieter; «radio whisper» for rehearsals.
«Boards up!» — mini-whiteboard rounds: everyone commits, all boards up together — nobody hides, nobody shouts first.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, class answers with two and freezes — instant reset mid-game.
English-first — ideas in Russian are welcome, POINTS only for the English version — recast and repeat.
