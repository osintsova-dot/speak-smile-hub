🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use the ten experiment verbs (blow, boil, cover, fill, pour, rub, shake, stir, tie, wrap) as real instructions — and read a science article for gist, detail and vocabulary the researcher's way.
What is genuinely hard
Ten short verbs that all mean «do something with your hands» blur together fast — pour vs fill vs stir is the classic knot. Three sound traps: wrap's silent w, blow with /əʊ/, stir with /ɜː/.
Typical error to expect
«fill water into the glass» — fill takes the CONTAINER (fill the glass with water), pour takes the LIQUID (pour the water into the glass). The swap is near-universal and worth a whole CCQ.
Why the lesson is built this way
The verbs arrive inside three real home experiments — every verb is a physical instruction with an object, run as a TB jigsaw so each student teaches one experiment to a new group. The recording confirms the science. The reading is the unit's showpiece — the Ig Nobel Prize, a text that sells itself — run gist → detail-with-justification → word harvest; the conditionals living in it (if a cow has a name…) stay unnamed until next lesson. The productive stage has teams invent and pitch their own Ig Nobel project with the experiment verbs — smile first, think second, exactly like the prize.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“A cow with a name gives 500 litres more milk. Toast falls butter-side down 80% of the time. Real scientists proved both — and won a prize for it. Today: ten verbs to run any experiment, the prize for research that makes you laugh, and YOUR own crazy-but-serious project at the end.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a science poll with a partner, and the bridge into the unit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — the science poll. Kagan Think-Pair-Share on About you: Are you more into science or arts — and why? What scientific experiments have you done at school? Twenty seconds to think, one minute each way, two pairs report.
  3. Stretch an answer toward the lesson: You mixed vinegar and soda in Year 4? Perfect. Today you get the English to run that experiment — and meet scientists who count cow names.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the unit: Books open, page ninety. Unit sixteen — Amazing science. Three experiments you can do in your kitchen tonight.
📺 On screen: questions by voice — the voice is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: think, tell, report. Science-or-arts splits the room nicely — keep the split, it fuels the Opinions stage

8–24′
Vocabulary · the home lab
expert groups → mixed groups
Why: Ten experiment verbs arrive inside three real experiments students can actually run at home — every verb is a physical instruction, not a list item, and the jigsaw regrouping makes everyone teach their experiment twice.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 as a TB jigsaw: three experiments, every student teaches one

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Three expert groups, one experiment each (moving a can · keeping liquids apart · egg into a bottle). Six minutes: complete your gaps a–j from the box, agree the gestures, rehearse explaining it.
  2. Key (yours): a blow · b tie · c rub · d fill · e stir · f cover · g boil · h pour · i wrap · j shake.
  3. Regroup into threes — one expert per experiment. Each teaches theirs in one minute, WITH gestures; listeners mime along.
  4. Whole-class check: gestures added to the lab wall, pronunciation drilled on the traps: blow /bləʊ/ and pour /pɔː/ — no «у» sound · rub /rʌb/ vs wrap /ræp/ with its silent w · stir /stɜː/ · tie /taɪ/.
  5. Micro-CCQ: fill the glass / pour the water — the icon goes up.
📺 On screen: SB p.90 in every book — paper is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: complete, rehearse, teach, mime. Expect the egg experiment group to hold the room — the wow factor is free attention; spend it on the verbs

Reading · the Ig Nobel Prize
solo → pairs
Why: Gist first, then detail with justification, then the word harvest — the CELTA order on a text that sells itself: cows with names, buttered toast and cheese-scented mosquito traps. The conditionals hiding in it wait for next lesson, unnamed.

WHAT WE DO: gist → detail with justification → word harvest

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hook, books closed: There is a Nobel Prize. And there is a prize for research that makes you LAUGH — first laugh, then think. Cows with names. Falling toast. All real.
  2. ex.1 warm-up: what the class knows about the real Nobel (TB: since 1901, Stockholm; winners they might name — Einstein, Curie, Obama…). One minute.
  3. ex.2 gist race, 3 minutes: title + photos → what did the four projects do? Quick check.
  4. ex.3 detail in pairs, 8 minutes, Kagan RallyCoach — prove every answer from the line. Key: 1 Nobel = a great scientific achievement, Ig Nobel = making people smile AND then think about science · 2 cows without names probably feel less loved · 3 the toast only has time to turn ONCE before the floor · 4 some mosquitoes carry malaria, which kills humans · 5 graphene — a great scientific achievement.
  5. ex.4 words, pairs: highlighted ↔ meanings. Key: 1 float · 2 aim · 3 trap(s) · 4 proof · 5 drop · 6 achievement(s). Float and proof go on the wall.
  6. Track 77 exists for listening-while-reading — optional.
📺 On screen: track 77 is the recording of the article — optional (Backup: skip the audio entirely; the stage is built on silent reading)
👦 Students do: predict, race, prove, harvest. Expect the Geim frog-and-graphene fact to trigger questions — one minute of wonder is allowed, then back to the lines

🎯 Speaking out · Ig Nobel pitches
teams of 3–4
Why: The productive outcome: teams invent their own Ig Nobel research project — a silly question investigated seriously — and pitch it with the experiment verbs. Make them smile first, then think.

WHAT WE DO: teams invent a silly-but-serious research project and pitch it with the experiment verbs

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Teams of three or four. The pitch skeleton on the board: Our research question is… · Our experiment: we will fill / shake / wrap / boil… · If we are right, people will… (the conditional sneaks in — do not name it).
  2. Eight minutes to build. Two experiment verbs minimum, one measurable result. Examples to unstick a team: do cats sleep longer on homework? does music make tea cool faster?
  3. Kagan Numbered Heads Together: you call the number, that member pitches (45 seconds max).
  4. Class votes two prizes: funniest question and best English — separate prizes, on purpose.
  5. You log instruction-slips silently — they open lesson 5.
📺 On screen: the pitch skeleton in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: invent, build, pitch, vote. Expect one team to propose testing the toast fact on the school canteen — applaud and demand the verbs

Opinions · science or arts
pairs → class
Why: The book's own About-you and Talking points run as a real exchange: sentence starters guarantee the shape, the reason rule guarantees the depth.

WHAT WE DO: About you closed + Talking points with the reason rule

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Return to the opening split: Science people — has art won a point today? Arts people — has science? One-minute pair exchange, reasons compulsory.
  2. Talking points, TB shape: What 'problem' would you like the Ig Nobel Prize to solve? What are the biggest questions of our time? — TB seeds if the room dries up: space, black holes, war and peace, climate change
  3. Collect three or four best ideas on the board in note form — they become tomorrow's conditional fuel (if we solve…, people will…, still unnamed).
  4. Class vote with thumbs on the best big question; two voters defend the thumb in a full sentence.
📺 On screen: notes in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: exchange, propose, vote, defend. Expect climate change and space to dominate — push once for a smaller, stranger question; the Ig spirit is the point

WHAT WE DO: Quiz-Quiz-Trade with verb-gesture cards + spelling round

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ten cards: verb on one side, its object phrase on the other. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade: show the gesture, partner names verb + object; coach if stuck; trade and move. Three minutes.
  2. Sit on a clap. Mini-whiteboard spelling round, the traps: Boards up — wrap! … stir! … pour!
  3. Fix with a smile: the silent w, the ir, the our. Test in two weeks. Now they're yours.
📺 On screen: printed verb cards — the cards in their hands are the medium; if the set has gone missing, mime the verbs yourself and pairs answer chorally
👦 Students do: quiz, coach, trade, spell. Expect wrap to gain a w sound and lose one on paper — both die here

Form
imperative + object (+ preposition): fill X with Y · pour Y into X · wrap X in Y · rub X on Y — the verb travels WITH its pattern.
Pron. watch
blow /bləʊ/ and pour /pɔː/ — no «у» sound · rub /rʌb/ vs wrap /ræp/ with its silent w · stir /stɜː/ · tie /taɪ/
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«fill the water into the glass» — what do we fill? → the GLASS. Fill the container, pour the liquid.
«stir» or «shake» — which needs a spoon? → stir. Shake is the whole thing moving.
«wrap» — do I hear the w? → no: /ræp/, like the music.

Assumptions: SB p.90–91, syllabus lesson 49 — the second unit of the block. Boil and blow ring bells from cooking and birthdays; cover, stir, wrap, rub are effectively new as active vocabulary. Modals of deduction (Unit 12) can resurface in the listening check — TB suggests it; take the gift if it comes.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The ten verbs blur into one gestureEvery verb gets ONE fixed gesture at the wall (stir = spoon circle, wrap = both hands fold, rub = palms). Return to the gesture, not the translation — the body disambiguates faster.
fill/pour swapThe CCQ pair is planted early and repeated at every occurrence: fill the GLASS, pour the WATER. Draw the glass-and-jug icon next to both verbs on the wall.
The reading's numbers hijack attention (13,000 vs 13,500 litres)Let them — numbers are gist hooks. The detail questions bring attention back to WHY: the justification rule (prove it from the line) is the actual skill.
Ig Nobel pitches drift into pure comedy with no EnglishThe pitch skeleton is compulsory and contains the verbs: Our question is… · We will fill/shake/wrap… · If we are right, … Fun without the frame earns applause and no points.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
