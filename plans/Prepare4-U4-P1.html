🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Own twelve be/do/have/make collocations about friends and family (have an argument, do me a favour, make friends…) and use must / mustn't / (don't) have to / had to and should to talk about rules and give advice.
What is genuinely hard
Two invisible traps. Collocations: Russian «делать» covers both do and make, so the verb is guessed, not known — only chunks survive. Modals: mustn't and don't have to BOTH translate as «не должен», but one forbids and one merely excuses — and must has no past at all (had to covers everything).
Typical error to expect
make a favour · do friends · make fun (meaning have fun) — Russian «делать» maps onto both do and make, so the pairs must be learnt as chunks [high priority, A2/B1]
You mustn't do homework tonight (meaning «не обязательно») — Russian «не должен» covers both; English splits them: mustn't = forbidden, don't have to = not necessary [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
Vocabulary comes first because the reading and the grammar are both made of it. The forum reading does double duty: careful reading for detail AND the quarry the grammar is mined from — every example in the discovery stage is a sentence the class has already met in Kaitlin's, Dylan's and Alex's stories. The mustn't/don't-have-to split gets its own concept-check minute because it is a concept problem, not a memory problem. The productive stage is a live advice show at 70′ — the forum played out loud — after two rounds of controlled practice, because fluent «You should…» needs the form to be warm. You log errors silently; they open lesson 2.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Three teenagers posted their problems on a forum — a nosy little brother, unfair parents, laughing classmates. By the end of today you can do what the forum does: name the problem properly — have an argument, fall out, it isn't my fault — and give advice that actually sounds like advice: you should, you mustn't, you don't have to. Then we run our own advice show.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts. The rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a hot verb-sorting round, and the bridge into the unit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — hot sort: be, do, have or make? Eight KNOWN chunks on the board without their verbs: ___ homework · ___ a shower · ___ breakfast · ___ a cake · ___ late · ___ sport · ___ a photo · ___ happy. Teams call the verb: Homework? — DO! A cake? — MAKE! A point per correct verb.
  3. Leave the four verbs as column headings in a corner — today the columns get twelve new tenants.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the unit: Books open, page forty-two. Unit seven — Getting on. Friends, family, arguments — and how to fix them.
📺 On screen: eight verbless chunks in chalk — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: fire verbs back in teams. Expect do homework and have breakfast to be solid and make a photo to slip from one or two — name it, don't dwell

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim and put up the two working walls of the lesson

Vocabulary · be, do, have, make
solo → pairs
Why: Twelve collocations arrive from real speakers on the recording and settle into a four-column wall. Chunks, not words: Russian «делать» covers do AND make, so only whole phrases survive contact with speech.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 (track 33 again): choose the verb in twelve lines; ex.3: sort into the four columns

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.2 solo first, pencil: choose the verb in all twelve lines — 3 minutes, guessing allowed. Then play track 33 again to check by ear. Key: 1 makes me angry · 2 having problems · 3 had an argument · 4 I'm annoyed · 5 Are you on your own · 6 do something · 7 have fun · 8 do me a favour · 9 is wrong · 10 make friends · 11 isn't my fault · 12 have lots in common.
  2. ex.3: pairs sort the twelve phrases into the four board columns. Two runners per column write them up as the class agrees. Key: be: annoyed, on your own, wrong, my fault · do: something, me a favour · have: problems, an argument, fun, lots in common · make: me angry, friends.
  3. Drill the sound traps by ear, three choral repetitions: fault /fɔːlt/ (no «у» sound!) · annoyed /əˈnɔɪd/ · argument /ˈɑːɡjumənt/ (no e after u!) · favour /ˈfeɪvə/.
  4. Chunk drill with attitude: It isn't my FAULT! · Can you do me a FAVOUR? · We have LOTS in common! — choral, then rows.
📺 On screen: track 33 in the whiteboard app (Backup: read the conversations from the audioscript, TB p.266, stressing the collocations — the check works identically by voice)
👦 Students do: choose, listen, sort, drill. Expect do/make swaps in half the class on the first pass — that is the diagnosis, not a failure. Typical error: make me a favour

35–47′
Reading · Troublespot forum
solo → pairs
Why: Gist first, then names, then advice-matching, then the highlighted words — the CELTA order. The forum is also stuffed with must/should, so the reading is the quarry the grammar is mined from.

WHAT WE DO: three problems, six replies: gist → names → advice-match → the highlighted words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hook, books closed (TB warmer): What annoys you? Where do you go for advice — a person or the internet? Two answers, then: today's text is an advice forum called TROUBLESPOT.
  2. Pre-teach on the board: credit (money on your phone) · sensible · behave yourself.
  3. Gist race, ex.1, 3 minutes: Three teenagers. Who is NOT annoyed with a family member? Key: Kaitlin (she is NOT annoyed with a family member).
  4. ex.2 in pairs — Kagan RallyCoach: partner A answers 1–3 with the text, B coaches; swap for 4–6. Key: 1 Alex · 2 Kaitlin · 3 Dylan · 4 Kaitlin · 5 Alex · 6 Dylan.
  5. ex.3: match TWO replies A–F to each problem. Key: Kaitlin A + F · Dylan B + E · Alex C + D. Fast finishers: ex.4 — what advice would YOU give (one sentence per problem).
  6. ex.5: the six highlighted words to meanings. Key: 1 password · 2 lock · 3 apologise · 4 fair · 5 behave (themselves) · 6 trust. Quick Talking point if the pace allows: Are internet forums good places to get advice? · How can you help friends with problems?
📺 On screen: track 34 is the recording of the forum — optional; the text is in every book (Backup: skip the audio entirely, the stage is built on silent reading)
👦 Students do: read for the odd one out, then argue the details from the text. Expect sympathy votes to split between Dylan and Alex — opinions mean they read it. Typical problem: «fair» known only as «светлый» — anchor it isn't fair = это нечестно

47–59′
Grammar · discovery
pairs → class
Why: Guided discovery, not presentation: the six example sentences come FROM the forum they just read. Rules extracted from sentences they have already met are rules they keep.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1 + ex.3: the modal rules, mined from the forum

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Warmer, books closed: TB warmer: three rules of MY job on the board — I must wear smart clothes · I mustn't be late · I don't have to wear a uniform — students underline the modal verbs and guess which rule is the odd one out (the not-necessary one).
  2. ex.1 — the heart of the lesson: six sentences FROM the forum, rules a–e to complete. Pairs get 3 minutes. Key: a must · b mustn't · c didn't have to · d had to — and the headline fact: must has no past form, had to covers both.
  3. Box the headline fact on the board: MUST HAS NO PAST — HAD TO does both jobs.
  4. The trap, concept-checked cold: mustn't go = forbidden. don't have to go = your choice. Run the phone CCQ from the plan header on two students by name.
  5. ex.3 fast — the should rules from the forum replies. Key: a should · b shouldn't · c should (in questions — asking for advice). Fill the ADVICE column: should = a good idea, not a rule.
  6. Signpost: Grammar reference page 144 — tonight, two minutes, if you want the rules in a table.
📺 On screen: the modal wall fills up in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: extract the rules and place the forum sentences into the columns. Expect «didn't have to» to read as a prohibition for a few — the CCQ pair fixes it. Typical error: should to apologise
📓 Interactive notebook — Prepare4_Unit7_Grammar_Rules_Advice_A5
A5 grammar card — RULES OR ADVICE?: OBLIGATION with must (the speaker says it is necessary) and have to (an outside rule) · the pair NO OBLIGATION don't have to (it isn't necessary) against PROHIBITION mustn't (don't do it) · ADVICE with should and shouldn't — every line with a model (You must say sorry. · We have to wear a uniform. · You don't have to come. · You mustn't be rude. · You should talk to her.) and the CRITICAL NOTE that none of these verbs takes to: use the base form
  • Glue the card in and highlight the don't have to / mustn't pair — that is the trap.
  • Write the meaning of both in your own words, one under the other.
  • Write five sentences about the rules of your class, one per line of the card.
  • Form check: must say, should talk — never must to say.
When: ~5 min at the start of the “Grammar · discovery” stagePrep: 1 × A5 per pupil. Scissors, glue sticks, pencils.

Grammar · practice
pairs → teams
Why: Controlled practice with immediate peer reasoning — the Corpus error hunt makes them justify the choice, which is what transfers to speaking.

WHAT WE DO: ex.2 own sentences + ex.4 advice + ex.5 Corpus error hunt

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.2: two sentences per prompt about school and home rules — solo, 3 minutes, then Kagan Showdown on mini-whiteboards: you call the prompt (mustn't at school!), teams write one sentence and show on three. Match key: match 1c · 2b · 3a · 4b · 5d · 6c; possible sentences: I don't have to stay for lunch · I mustn't run in the corridors · I must help my parents · I had to keep my toys tidy · I didn't have to make my bed.
  2. ex.4 in pairs: read problems 1–5, give should/shouldn't advice. Possible answers: possible: 1 You should always keep it in the same place · 2 You should join a club and be friendly · 3 You shouldn't get angry — try to stay calm · 4 You should try to explain your point of view · 5 You should explain why you're annoyed.
  3. ex.5 — Corpus error hunt in pairs: five real learner sentences, find and fix the modal. Say WHY, not just the fix. Key: Corpus: 1 we HAD TO go to school · 2 we had TO ask you · 3 all schools HAVE TO / SHOULD have a school uniform · 4 you SHOULDN'T sit down all day · 5 I don't HAVE TO go to bed early at weekends.
  4. Collect with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: Sentence four — number twos!
📺 On screen: prompts called aloud, answers on mini-whiteboards — voice and boards are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write rules, give advice, hunt errors. Expect ex.5 item 1 (we must go → had to) to need the boxed fact — point at it silently. Typical error: all schools has to — third person leaks

70–84′
🎯 Speaking out · the advice show
groups of 4
Why: The productive outcome: real problems, real advice, should/must/have to under time pressure — the forum from the reading, played live. Errors are logged silently; correcting now would kill fluency.

WHAT WE DO: the forum, live: real problems, real advice, modals compulsory

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the scene: Welcome to TROUBLESPOT LIVE. Every group is an advice panel.
  2. Two minutes solo: everyone writes ONE real (or invented) problem on a slip — school, family, friends. Model on the board: My little brother always takes my things.
  3. Groups of four, slips in the middle. Kagan Numbered Heads Together: draw a slip, the reader is the host; the other three each give ONE piece of advice — should / mustn't / don't have to — plus a reason. The host picks the best and says why.
  4. Rotate the host role with each slip; aim for 4–5 slips per group.
  5. You walk with a paper: log modal and collocation errors SILENTLY — they open lesson 2.
  6. Close the loop: each group reads its best problem-and-advice pair to the class.
📺 On screen: one problem slip per student, written live — paper slips are the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: post problems, give advice, judge it. Expect the invented problems to be theatrical (My cat ate my homework) — allow it: the modals ride on the theatre. Typical error: advice without a reason — the host asks Why? every time

  • say twelve friendship collocations with the RIGHT verb (do a favour, make friends)
  • tell mustn't (forbidden) from don't have to (not necessary)

Structure
It isn't my fault! · Can you do me a favour? · We have lots in common. · He knows he mustn't go in there. · I have to get home by 9.30. · I had to get home by 10. · He should apologise.
Form
collocation = fixed verb + phrase (be/do/have/make); must & have to = rule; mustn't = forbidden ≠ don't have to = not necessary; past of both = had to; should + bare infinitive = advice.
Pron. watch
fault /fɔːlt/ (no «у» sound!) · annoyed /əˈnɔɪd/ · argument /ˈɑːɡjumənt/ (no e after u!) · favour /ˈfeɪvə/
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«You mustn't bring your phone» — is a phone allowed? → no, forbidden. «You don't have to bring your phone» — allowed? → yes; just not necessary.
«Yesterday I must go home» — possible? → no: must has no past — I HAD TO go home.
«He should apologise» — is it a rule or an idea? → an idea/advice; rules take must or have to.

Must/have to existed in Prepare 3 and in the intro grammar review; the mustn't vs don't have to split and had-to-as-the-only-past are effectively new as ACTIVE knowledge. Of the twelve collocations the class usually owns three or four (have fun, make friends half-known). Block 3 marked tests have been returned and analysed.

mustn't heard as «не обязательно»Concept check, not correction: You mustn't copy in a test — is copying allowed? No — forbidden. You don't have to come on Saturday — is coming allowed? Yes, just not necessary.
«Yesterday I must go home» — must pushed into the pastThe headline fact goes on the board in a box: MUST HAS NO PAST — HAD TO does both jobs. Point at the box; don't re-explain.
One-word advice in the advice show («Apologise.»)The rule of the show: advice needs should/must/have to AND a reason. The host asks Why? every time.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
