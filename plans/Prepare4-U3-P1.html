🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name six crimes and six criminals (plus the steal/rob split) and combine past simple with past continuous in one sentence — background action vs interrupting event — while telling what happened.
What is genuinely hard
Both tenses exist separately; what is genuinely new is the MEETING point: while + continuous, when + simple, and the decision «was it in progress or did it interrupt?». Plus the word-family discipline: burglar ≠ burglary, thief ≠ theft — Russian has one word for each pair.
Typical error to expect
I read a book when a woman screamed instead of was reading — the background action pushed into the past simple; and the mirror error, we were watching TV all evening and then were going to bed [high priority, A2/B1]
The thief robbed my phone — Russian «украл» covers both verbs, English splits them: steal a thing, rob a person or place [A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
Vocabulary comes first because the reading and the grammar are both made of it. The reading about strange laws does double duty: careful reading for detail AND the quarry the grammar is mined from — every rule in ex.4 is a sentence the class has already met in the stories. The steal/rob pair gets its own short stage because it is a concept problem, not a memory problem. The productive stage is an alibi interrogation at 70′, after two rounds of controlled practice, because fluent «What were you doing at…?» needs the form to be warm. During that stage you log errors silently and open lesson 2 with them.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Somebody stole a phone in this school yesterday. By the end of today you can interrogate a suspect properly: What were you doing at 8.15? Who were you talking to? — and you'll know six crimes by name, so you can tell a burglar from a shoplifter. Useful life skills, honestly.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts. The rewind pulls the language this lesson builds on back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a hot round of irregular past forms, and the bridge into the unit

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — hot round: past forms. Eight verbs on the board: steal, catch, hear, see, run, take, find, wear. Teams fire the past forms back: Steal? — Stole! Catch? — Caught! A point per correct form, minus a point for «stealed».
  3. Keep the eight past forms in a corner of the board — half of them come back in today's stories.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the unit: Books open, page thirty-two. Unit five — Call the police! Six crimes, six criminals, and one detective story grammar.
📺 On screen: eight infinitives in chalk — the board is the medium here, so nothing can fail and no backup is needed
👦 Students do: fire past forms back in teams. Expect stole/caught to be solid and «teached/stealed» to slip from one or two — name it, don't dwell

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim and put up the two working columns of the lesson

WHAT WE DO: About you + ex.1: match the six crimes to photos A–F

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

Vocabulary · steal vs rob
pairs → class
Why: Russian «украл» covers both verbs, so this pair needs its own two minutes of concept work — otherwise «he robbed my phone» survives all year.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 rule + ex.5 practice: one Russian verb, two English ones

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.4, read the rule aloud: Thieves STEAL something (from a place or person) · thieves ROB a place or a person (of something). You steal a phone; you rob a bank.
  2. Concept check: You take a phone from a table — steal or rob? You attack a bank — steal or rob? You take money FROM a person — which verb for the person? (rob them).
  3. ex.5 solo, 2 minutes, then pair check. Key: 1 stole · 2 robbed · 3 robbing · 4 stealing.
  4. Kagan Showdown on mini-whiteboards: you say the object (a car! · a museum! · an old lady! · her purse!), teams write STEAL or ROB and show on the count of three.
📺 On screen: the steal/rob rule copied onto the board under the word wall — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: apply the rule, then commit on whiteboards. Expect 100% on things, hesitation on people (rob an old lady, steal her purse) — that hesitation is the lesson. Typical error: he robbed my phone

WHAT WE DO: three true stories about strange laws: gist → complete → true/false → the law words

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hook, books closed: In one country chewing gum is banned. Which country? Take guesses, then: Singapore — since 1992.
  2. Gist race, 3 minutes, books open: Three teenagers, three countries, three strange laws. Find all three laws — just the laws. Pre-teach on the board: pedestrian crossing, swimming cap, bell.
  3. ex.1 complete the three laws together. Key: 1 look at your phone on a pedestrian crossing · 2 wear a swimming cap in public swimming pools · 3 have a bell.
  4. ex.2 true/false in pairs — Kagan RallyCoach: partner A answers 1–3, B coaches with the text; swap for 4–6. Key: 1 false (her mum suggested it) · 2 true · 3 false (they didn't understand the lifeguards) · 4 true · 5 false (he didn't even have a bike in the UK) · 6 true.
  5. ex.3: the six highlighted law words into new sentences. Key: 1 fined · 2 illegal · 3 the law · 4 compulsory · 5 fine · 6 rule. Quick Talking point if the pace allows: Do you know any unusual laws? · What rules do you disagree with at your school?
📺 On screen: track 24 is the recording of the text — optional; the text is in every book (Backup: skip the audio entirely, the stage is built on silent reading)
👦 Students do: read for the three laws, then argue the true/false items from the text. Expect item 2 (the police officer) to split the class — make them point at the line. Typical problem: «fine» known only as an adjective — anchor fine = money you pay

Grammar · discovery
pairs → class
Why: Guided discovery, not presentation: both tenses exist separately after earlier units; the new thing is how they meet in one sentence. Rules extracted from sentences they have already read are rules they keep.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 form + ex.4: the three combination rules, mined from the stories

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Warmer, books closed: TB warmer: four verbs on the board (watch, play, buy, meet). Guess what I was doing at 7 pm yesterday. Students fire past-continuous guesses until they hit it.
  2. ex.1–2 fast: which sentence is continuous, how is it formed? Key: sentence 2 is continuous; formed with was/were + -ing; a past simple · b past continuous.
  3. ex.4 — the heart of the lesson: three sentences FROM the stories they just read, three rules to match. Pairs get 3 minutes. Key: 1 a — past continuous (in progress) · 2 b — past simple (the shorter interrupting action) · 3 c — one after the other: past simple for both.
  4. Fill the two board columns with the ex.4 sentences: WAS HAPPENING… (We were crossing the road) | …HAPPENED (when I got out my phone).
  5. Concept checks from the plan header — all three, cold-calling by name.
  6. Signpost: Grammar reference page 142 — tonight, two minutes, if you want the rules in a table.
📺 On screen: the two columns fill up in chalk — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: extract the rules and place the sentences into the columns. Expect rule c (two actions in a row → both simple) to surprise them — it breaks the «new toy» reflex. Typical error: while + past simple

70–84′
🎯 Speaking out · the alibi
pairs, then swap
Why: The productive outcome: past continuous questions and answers about a (fictional) crime, under time pressure. Errors are logged silently — correcting now would kill fluency.

WHAT WE DO: an interrogation: past continuous questions about yesterday, 8.00–8.30 pm

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the scene: Yesterday at 8.15 pm somebody stole the school's chocolate supply. Everyone is a suspect. Everyone needs an alibi.
  2. One minute solo: everyone notes what they were REALLY doing at 8.15 yesterday (three details: where, what, who with).
  3. Kagan Timed Pair Share, 2 minutes each way: A is the detective — What were you doing at 8.15? Who were you talking to? What were you wearing? B answers in full sentences; the detective asks one follow-up per answer. Swap.
  4. Swap partners once (detectives move one seat right) and repeat faster.
  5. You walk with a paper: log tense errors and «robbed my phone» slips SILENTLY — they open lesson 2.
  6. Close the loop: two detectives report the most suspicious alibi — He says he was doing homework at 8.15. On a Sunday.
📺 On screen: the three question stems in chalk (What were you doing…? · Who were you talking to…? · What were you wearing…?) — the board is the medium, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: interrogate, answer, follow up, swap. Expect fluent questions by round 2 and the continuous to stick. Typical error: What you were doing? — word order in the question; it goes on the error sheet, not into the conversation

WB Unit 5 (p.20–23)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can name the six crimes and their criminals, choose steal or rob correctly, and combine past simple and past continuous in one sentence about a past event.
  • Subsidiary. Reading three stories about strange laws for gist and detail (SB p.33); the law words (illegal, fine, compulsory…); guided discovery of the three combination rules (SB p.34 ex.4).
  • Personal. Do not pre-teach the while/when rule — let them extract it from sentences they have already read (ex.4). During the alibi stage log tense errors silently; delayed feedback opens lesson 2.
By the end, students can…
  • name six crimes and six criminals — spelled apart (burglar/burglary)
  • choose steal or rob and say why
  • say what was happening and what happened in one sentence
  • ask past continuous questions (What were you doing at…?)

Structure

Form
past simple = complete action / the interrupter; past continuous (was/were + -ing) = action in progress / the background; while + continuous, when + simple; two actions in a row → both simple.
Pron. watch
burglar /ˈbɜːɡlə/ (no «у» between g and l!) · thief /θiːf/ – thieves /θiːvz/ · vandalism /ˈvændəlɪzəm/ · queue of criminals: stress on the FIRST syllable throughout
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«I was reading when the phone rang» — which action started first? → the reading; which one interrupted? → the phone.
«While we were waiting, I heard a cry» — can I say «while we waited»? → the continuous paints the background; after WHILE the continuous is the natural choice.
«When we arrived, my brother got changed» — two backgrounds or one after the other? → one after the other: both past simple.

Assumptions: SB p.32–34 (top), syllabus lesson 15. Past simple is solid after Unit 2 and the block 1 test; past continuous existed in Prepare 3 but has not been active this year. Of the twelve crime words the class usually owns two or three (thief, steal, hacker as a loan word). The steal/rob split is effectively new.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
burglar/burglary, thief/theft mixed up in speechKeep the two-column board (CRIME | CRIMINAL) up all lesson and point at it silently every time someone says «a burglary came into the house».
«He robbed my phone» — Russian «украл» covers both verbsConcept check, not correction: Do you rob a thing or a place? A place or a person. So what do you do to a phone? Steal it.
Past continuous overused once it is «the new toy»Feed the counter-example early: When we arrived, we got changed — two actions in a row, both simple. The trap is on the board from the discovery stage.
One-word answers in the alibi interrogationThe rule of the lesson: one word is not an alibi — an answer needs a verb and a detail. The detective asks a follow-up question every time.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
