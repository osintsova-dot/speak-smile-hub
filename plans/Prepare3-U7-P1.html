🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name ten outdoor activities as whole phrases — look for fossils, kayak down a river, track wild animals — and say which of them you have and have not done.
What is genuinely hard
Not the words. The phrases: English fixes the verb to the noun in a way Russian does not, so make a cave and find fossils feel perfectly reasonable and are wrong. And the whole unit is heading for a tense that does not exist in Russian, so today's job is to build the content it will need.
Typical error to expect
swimmed, breaked, eated, flied, growed — the regular ‑ed ending put on an irregular verb. In the exam a misspelt form scores nothing [high priority, A2/B1]
Have you ever saw a whale? · I have went there — the past simple form used where the participle is needed [high priority, A2/B1]
Why the lesson is built this way
This is the first lesson of a THREE-lesson unit, and that changes what it is for. It is not a lexis-and-a-bit-of-grammar lesson; it is the lesson that builds the raw material — ten activities, a text that argues for doing them, and a recorded conversation in which two teenagers say which ones they have done. The grammar is deliberately NOT taught today. It is heard all lesson, in the website's own first line and in Susanna's answers, and it is taught tomorrow with a whole lesson to itself, because the present perfect is the heaviest grammar of the year. The vocabulary stage is longer than usual and ends with a collocation drill — you say the verb, they complete it — because the phrase is the unit, not the word. The listening carries a table rather than questions: filling a table forces detail listening without a single comprehension question. And the productive stage is their own list of ten, which is the same task the website sets and gives every student something to defend.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Ten things to do before you're sixteen. That is a real website and that is today's page. Some of you have already done six of them. By the end of the lesson you can say all ten in English — and you write your own ten, and you defend them.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, two rules of block 6 recalled and one outdoor activity from everybody

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last block rewind. Two questions on the board: How many words go in one gap when you write down what you hear? (one or two) · Which one is a person — who or which? (who).
  3. Then the switch, on mini-whiteboards: Thirty seconds — write one thing you like doing outside. Reveal on Boards up! and count how many different ones the class has between them.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the new unit: Books open, page seventy-six. New unit. And the first question is: what should you have done before you are sixteen?
📺 On screen: the two questions written on the board before the lesson — chalk only, nothing to fail and no backup needed
👦 Students do: answer the two questions, then write one outdoor activity. Expect football, walking and «nothing» — which is a genuinely useful starting point for this page. Typical problem: they write in Russian; accept it today and translate it out loud

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

8–14′
Lead-in · the outdoors
board → pairs
Why: Four words on the board and one question — what do they have in common? — open the topic in ninety seconds and from their own lives, not from the book's.

WHAT WE DO: the About you questions on p.76, from four words on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. TB p.149: write picnicking, skiing, sun-bathing, gardening on the board with books closed. «What do they have in common?» (you do them outdoors). Then «Which outdoors activities can you only do in good weather?» (sunbathing) and «Which can you do without spending a lot of money?» (running).
  2. Books open. List as many outdoor activities as you can think of. Which ones do you like doing? Which ones don't you like doing?
  3. Kagan Think-Pair-Share: twenty seconds alone, then the partner, then two pairs report to the class.
  4. Leave one question hanging, because the page answers it: Which of these can you do in Norilsk in February?
📺 On screen: the four words written on the board with books closed (Backup: none needed — the board is the only medium here and the stage is chalk and talk)
👦 Students do: find what the four words have in common, then list and compare. Expect the «only in good weather» question to produce a genuinely useful list. Typical problem: they list sports rather than outdoor activities — accept it, then narrow it

14–28′
Vocabulary 1 · outdoor activities
pairs → class
Why: Ten whole phrases, not ten words. Look for fossils is learnt as one unit because that is how it is used, how it is tested and how it is stored in the head.

WHAT WE DO: ten phrases on ten pictures: ex.1 p.76 (track 99), then a collocation drill and a mingle

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed first (TB p.149): Write down five things you would like to do before you are sixteen. One minute. Then books open and see whether anything on their list is on the website's.
  2. Explain the title in one line: the outdoors means the place where you do things outside.
  3. ex.1 — match the ten phrases to pictures A–J in pairs, 4 minutes. Key: A kayak down a river · B climb a tree · C camp under the stars · D play in the snow · E explore a cave · F look for fossils · G track wild animals · H try rock climbing · I pick wild fruit · J record birdsong
  4. Play track 99 — check, then repeat chorally. Drill the stress: KAYak, FOSsils, BIRDsong.
  5. TB p.149, three questions that turn the page from a word list into a conversation: «Is this the complete list of activities?» (no) · «Can you explore a cave before you kayak down a river?» (yes) · «When do you need to start?» (now). And after the matching, the drill that fixes the collocations: cover the answers, say a verb and let them complete it — look (for fossils) — then do the same with the nouns — wild animals (track).
  6. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with activity cards, 4 minutes: the picture on one side, the phrase on the other. Quiz your partner. Coach if he's wrong. Trade cards. Find a new partner!
📺 On screen: track 99 in the whiteboard app with the ten pictures on the screen (Backup if the board is down: read the ten phrases aloud twice yourself and use the pictures in the paper book; the Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards are printed in advance and need no technology at all)
👦 Students do: match, repeat, drill both directions, then mingle. Expect 5–6 of 10 unaided. Typical error: the preposition dropped — look fossils, camp the stars. The two-directional drill is the cure

28–42′
Reading · The Great Outdoors
solo → pairs
Why: A list with an argument in it — ten things to do before you're 16 — so reading it is motivated. It is also the source of every present perfect sentence in the next lesson.

WHAT WE DO: the website text on p.76 (track 98) — what it is, who it is for and how it works

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. SB p.76: a website called «The Great Outdoors: 10 things to do before you're 16» — download the app, tick the activities off as you do them, in any order and at any time, and when you have finished these ten, click on another section (Animals, People or Sport) and download ten more. Its own line is the hook: «Off you go! No time to lose.»
  2. Set the gist task BEFORE they read: Two minutes. Three questions. Who is this website for? What do you download? What happens when you finish the ten?
  3. Play track 98 while they follow with a finger — the reading text is recorded, and it fixes the pronunciation of the ten phrases for free.
  4. Check the three questions as a class, and add the book's own three from the vocabulary stage if they have not come up already.
  5. Then one question that is not in the book and is worth two minutes: Why does the website say «No time to lose»? You are thirteen. What is the hurry?
  6. Kagan Numbered Heads Together for the round-up: call a number, that student answers for their group.
📺 On screen: track 98 in the whiteboard app (Backup with no sound: read the website text aloud yourself while they follow in the book — it is printed on SB p.76 and the stage does not depend on the recording at all)
👦 Students do: read for gist, follow the recording, then argue about the deadline. Expect the «what's the hurry» question to produce the best English of the stage. Typical problem: they read every word from the start — set the timer visibly

Why: A stirrer that also forces one English sentence. Teenagers who have been sitting for forty minutes need to stand before the second half, or the second half dies.

WHAT WE DO: a two-minute stirrer that also forces one English sentence out of everybody

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everybody up. I say an activity — you mime it for three seconds. Kayak down a river. Climb a tree. Track wild animals. Six of them, fast.
  2. Then the sentence tax: Before you sit down — name one thing on the list you have done. One phrase each, no full sentence needed.
📺 On screen: no equipment at all — this stage is deliberately technology-free, and nothing can fail
👦 Students do: stand, mime, name one activity, sit. Expect play in the snow from everybody and explore a cave from nobody

Listening · Juan and Susanna
solo → pairs
Why: Two friends going through the same list the students have just read. The vocabulary comes straight back in somebody else's mouth, and the table forces detail listening without a single comprehension question.

WHAT WE DO: two friends going through the same list: ex.2–3 p.76 (track 100)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. SB p.76 ex.2–3, track 100: Juan has found the website and is going through it with Susanna. She has done seven of the ten already and turns out to have a reason for the three she has not: she does not like going underground. Comprehension questions from TB p.149: «Who found the website?» (Juan) · «Why hasn't Susanna explored a cave?» (she doesn't like going underground) · «Where did she go climbing?» (on a climbing wall).
  2. ex.2 first, one job only: Which activities would Juan like to do? Play track 100 once. Key: kayak down a river, look for fossils
  3. ex.3 — now the table about Susanna, three rows: new, not new, and when/where. Play track 100 again and pause it after each activity, which is what makes a long recording survivable. Key: New: explore a cave, look for fossils, track wild animals · Not new: kayak down a river, climb a tree, camp under the stars, play in the snow, try rock climbing, record birdsong, pick wild fruit · When? / Where?: kayak down a river – last year in Spain · camp under the stars – last summer in Scotland · try rock climbing – a few weeks ago at school · pick wild fruit – yesterday in grandma's garden · record birdsong – last week at school
  4. Kagan RallyCoach for the check: A gives a row, B coaches, then swap.
  5. Three comprehension questions to close, from TB p.149 — they prove the answers rather than replace them.
  6. One thing you say and do not write: Listen to how Susanna says it. «I've done that.» We learn that tomorrow.
📺 On screen: track 100 in the whiteboard app (Backup with no sound: the full audioscript is on TB p.298 — read the conversation aloud yourself in two voices, twice, pausing after each activity. The table task is identical)
👦 Students do: listen twice with pauses, fill the table, then coach. Expect the when/where row to be half empty after the first play and nearly full after the second. Typical error: they put climb a tree in the «new» row because Susanna talks about it most

58–70′
Ordering · what I'd do first
solo → pairs
Why: Ranking is thinking disguised as a task: to put the ten in order they have to understand all ten, and the pair discussion that follows has a reason to disagree.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 p.76: number the ten activities in the order you would do them, then compare

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Number the ten. One is what you would do first. Ten is what you would do last — or never. Three minutes alone, in pencil.
  2. In pairs, 5 minutes, the two questions from ex.4: Which of the activities do you like doing or would you like to do? Why? Which ones don't you like doing or wouldn't you like to do? Why not?
  3. One rule that keeps it in English and stops it collapsing into numbers: every answer needs because in it.
  4. Class round-up: which activity did most people put first, and which did most people put last? Two columns on the board.
  5. Then the question from TB p.149 that makes it local: Where near here could you actually do each of these? Take three answers.
📺 On screen: the two columns written on the board as they are collected (Backup: none needed — pencil, the book and the board)
👦 Students do: rank, then defend in pairs. Expect explore a cave to be last for most and first for one, which is the argument worth having. Typical problem: they give the number and no reason — the because rule is enforced from the first pair

70–84′
🎯 Speaking out · my ten things
fours
Why: The productive outcome: their own list of ten things to do before they are sixteen, defended out loud. Every item needs the phrase, and the defending needs a reason.

WHAT WE DO: their own «ten things to do before you're 16», written and defended

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Give the frame on the board so the writing has a shape: Our ten things to do before you're 16 · number 1 is ___ because ___ · we took ___ off the website's list because ___
  2. In fours, 7 minutes: keep five of the website's ten and invent five of their own. Every invented one must be a phrase — verb plus noun, like the book's.
  3. Circulate with a correction sheet and log errors silently. Log the present perfect attempts too, but do not correct them — tomorrow's lesson starts from that list.
  4. Each group presents its ten in 45 seconds. The class votes for the best list and has to say why in one sentence.
  5. Kagan Numbered Heads Together for the vote: number off, call a number, that student gives their group's reason.
  6. Close with the errors you logged — three sentences on the board, no names, thirty seconds to repair.
📺 On screen: the frame on the board and the website headline from 5–8′ still visible (Backup: the frame is dictated into notebooks — this stage needs no technology at all)
👦 Students do: invent five, keep five, present and vote. Expect the invented items to be better English than anything else in the lesson, because they chose them. Typical error: an invented item that is one word — send it back for a verb

WB Unit 13 (p.52–55)
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can name ten outdoor activities as whole phrases and say which they have done and when.
  • Subsidiary. Reading a website for gist (SB p.76); listening to a conversation and completing a three-row table (ex.2–3, track 100); ranking and justifying a choice.
  • Personal. Do not teach the present perfect today, however much they produce it. Accept it, use it yourself, write nothing on the board about it. It has a whole lesson tomorrow and it needs the room.
By the end, students can…
  • say ten outdoor activities as whole phrases, verb and noun together
  • complete a phrase from its verb alone, and from its noun alone
  • understand a conversation about which activities somebody has done
  • put ten activities in their own order and say why

Structure

Form
the phrase is fixed: look FOR fossils, kayak DOWN a river, camp UNDER the stars, play IN the snow. The preposition is part of the word.
Pron. watch
word stress inside the phrase: KAYak, FOSsils, BIRDsong. And cave /keɪv/, not /kav/.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
Find fossils or look for fossils? → look for. You may look and find nothing — that is why English chose that verb.
«Is this the complete list of ten?» → no. The website says you can download ten more — Animals, People or Sport.
«Can you explore a cave before you kayak down a river?» → yes. Any order, any time. That is the whole idea of the list.
«When do you need to start?» → now. That is the website's own answer, and it is what makes the page worth reading.

Assumptions: SB p.76, syllabus lesson 40. They know climb, snow and river; the phrases are new and so are fossils, birdsong, track as a verb and cave. They have heard the present perfect in reading all year and have never been asked to make it — and today they still are not.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
They translate the phrase word by word and lose the prepositionThe drill at the end of the vocabulary stage exists for this: you say the verb, they say the whole phrase. Then you say the noun and they say the verb. Sixty seconds, both directions.
Somebody produces I have never did that in the speaking stageAccept it, repeat their sentence back correctly, and move on. Do NOT stop to teach it — tomorrow's whole lesson is that sentence.
Cave and fossils mean nothing to themTwo pictures and no explanation. A cave is a hole in a mountain you can walk into; a fossil is an animal that turned into stone. Fifteen seconds each.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
