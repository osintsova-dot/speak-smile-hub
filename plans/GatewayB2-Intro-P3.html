🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Re-activate the six B1 grammar nests the B2 year builds on — narrative tenses, present perfect vs past simple, conditionals 0–2, the passive, reported speech and the modal cluster — in context and in use, not as a list of names. The productive outcome is a speaking carousel in which each student uses four of the six under time pressure.
What is genuinely hard
B1 grammar is not missing, it is unstable under load: students produce a clean second conditional in a gap-fill and a if I would have thirty seconds later in speech. The second difficulty is metalanguage — half the class can use the present perfect and could not tell you its name, and the other half knows the name and cannot use it. Today's tasks therefore judge production, never labels.
Typical error to expect
«I live here since 2019», «I know him for five years» — the Russian present covers both, English does not: a state that started in the past and is still true takes the present perfect. I have lived here since 2019. I have known him for five years. This is the single most frequent B1 leak into a B2 class.
«If it will rain, we will stay at home» — after if English uses the present for a future meaning. The Russian sentence has two futures, the English one has one.
«He said me that he is tired» — two errors in one: say takes no indirect object without to (tell me / say to me), and the reported clause shifts back (he was tired).
Why the lesson is built this way
The mistake here would be six mini-lessons. Instead: one sorting task that puts twelve real sentences into six nests (recognition, in pairs, thirteen minutes), then a grammar auction — the single best B1-revision activity there is, because teams have to decide whether a sentence is right and defend the decision with money on the table. Then movement, then the diagnostic, then a speaking carousel so that the lesson ends in production and not in a test paper.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
You know more grammar than you can use. That is not a criticism, it is just what a summer does. So today, no rules on the board and no names to memorise. You are going to buy and sell English sentences at auction, and you will find out very fast which ones you can trust. Then there is one short paper — not marked, not shown to anyone — and after that we both know exactly what this year has to build.

WHAT WE DO: one sentence at the door using yesterday's vocabulary, then straight into the topic

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. At the door: One true sentence using any collocation from last lesson. Do not accept a bare word — the sentence is the point.
  2. Inside, thirty seconds: Last lesson: your words. Today: what holds them together.
  3. Board the six nest names and leave them up: NARRATIVE TENSES · PERFECT vs PAST · CONDITIONALS · PASSIVE · REPORTED SPEECH · MODALS. You will not have to define any of these today. You will have to use them.

Guided review · twelve sentences, six nests
pairs
Why: Sorting is recognition with a decision attached, which is far more diagnostic than a gap-fill; and pointing forward to Units 6, 7 and 8 tells the class that today is a survey, not a syllabus.

WHAT WE DO: recognition in context: sort real sentences, then say what each nest is FOR

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand each pair twelve cut-up sentences — two per nest, all about school, work or travel so that the vocabulary is yesterday's. Examples to use: I was walking home when I realised I had left my keys in the classroom. · I've known him for five years. · If I had more time, I'd take a second language. · The results are published in June. · She asked me where I lived. · You don't have to come, but you should.
  2. Six minutes. Put the twelve sentences into six piles of two and write a heading for each pile — in your own words, not in grammar words.
  3. Then the harder half: RallyRobin, three minutes — for each pile, one partner says what it is for (this one is for saying what was happening when something interrupted it) and the other says one more example of their own.
  4. Take feedback nest by nest, four minutes, and write only what the class produces. Add the one distinction they will not produce themselves — Present perfect vs past simple: unfinished time and result vs finished time. I have lived here for six years (still here) vs I lived there for six years (not any more).
  5. Do not teach anything here. Anything the room cannot do goes on a list on the side of the board headed COMING BACK LATER, with the unit number next to it (the passive is Unit 8, conditionals are Unit 6, reported speech is Unit 7).
📺 On screen: the six piles' headings written up as the class produces them, and the COMING BACK LATER list. Backup: if the sentences are not cut up, dictate six of them and have pairs sort from their own notebooks.
👦 Students do: sort twelve cut-up sentences in pairs, name each pile in ordinary words, produce one new example per nest.

18–33′
🎯 Grammar auction · money on the table
teams of four
Why: An auction forces a collective judgement about accuracy and then makes the team defend it — which is exactly the metacognitive step missing from a gap-fill. It is also the loudest fifteen minutes of the week, which a September grammar lesson badly needs.

WHAT WE DO: the stage the lesson is built around: buy the correct sentences, avoid the wrong ones

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Teams of four, £100 each, a written record kept by one team member. Twenty sentences, ten correct and ten with one B1 error each. Read each one aloud twice and invite bids.
  2. Build the twenty from the errors this group actually makes. The reliable ones: I live here since 2019. · If it will rain, we'll stay in. · He said me that he is tired. · I have seen him yesterday. · This building was build in 1890. · You must to come early. Mix them with clean ones of the same shape.
  3. The rule that makes it work: Before you bid, your team has thirty seconds to agree. If you buy a wrong sentence you lose the money. If you buy a correct one you double it.
  4. After each sale, do not announce the answer immediately — ask the buying team to justify: Why is that one worth forty pounds? Then confirm or correct, and correct in writing on the board.
  5. Keep a running total on the board. It takes fifteen minutes exactly if you keep the bidding to twenty seconds per item — use a timer and be brutal.
  6. Finish with the scoreboard and one honest sentence: The team that won is the team that argued most. That is the whole trick.
📺 On screen: the twenty sentences read aloud, the running total and every correction written on the board. Backup: the auction list is on your paper, not on a screen — if you had planned to project the sentences, read them instead and give each team a printed list.
👦 Students do: confer in fours, bid, justify the purchase out loud, and watch the corrections go up.
auction list (20 sentences) · board · timer
33–43′
Find Someone Who · the six nests, on their feet
open floor
Why: It puts all six nests into genuine questions, it makes every student produce rather than recognise, and the third-person reporting stage gets reported speech out of them without a single rule being stated.

WHAT WE DO: movement plus production: every nest used in a real question to a real person

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand out a grid of eight prompts, one line each, covering all six nests: … has known their best friend for more than ten years · … was doing something interesting at eight o'clock last night · … would travel to Japan if they had the money · … was given a good piece of advice this summer · … was told by a teacher that they should study more · … doesn't have to get up early at the weekend · … had never flown before this year · … has been asked the same question twice today.
  2. The rule that makes it a language activity: You must form the question yourself. «Has known…» does not become «You know your friend ten years?» Model the first two conversions on the board and then rub them out.
  3. Mix-Pair-Share: eight minutes, one signature per person per line, and you must ask one follow-up question before you get the signature.
  4. Sit them down and take four reports in the third person: Tell us about somebody, not about yourself. That forces the reported form back out: Dasha said she'd travel to Japan if she had the money.
📺 On screen: the two modelled question conversions on the board, then rubbed out. Backup: handwritten grids work as well as printed ones; if there are no copies at all, dictate six of the eight prompts.
👦 Students do: convert eight prompts into questions, mingle, ask a follow-up before each signature, then report on somebody else in the third person.

Diagnostic · grammar, use of English, listening
desks, silent
Why: Three sections in one sitting is the most a September class should be asked for, and putting listening last means the hardest section is met when the room is already quiet and settled.

WHAT WE DO: thirty silent minutes on three sections of the official B2 Diagnostic Test

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Say the three rules once, in these words, before every diagnostic section: One — this does not go in anybody's register. Two — if you do not know, leave it and move on; a blank costs the same as a wrong answer and less time. Three — when the timer stops, pens down, and we talk about it straight away.
  2. Today the class does exercises 1, 2 and 3 (Grammar, 15 marks), exercise 7 (Use of English, 5 marks) and exercises 10 and 11 (Listening, 10 marks). Thirty marks, thirty minutes.
  3. Grammar, 14 minutes. Exercise 1: five alternatives to choose. Exercise 2: five verbs put into the right form. Exercise 3: a five-gap story ('my phone in the locker') needing one or two words per gap.
  4. Use of English, 6 minutes. Exercise 7: five rephrasings in two to four words. Give the one rule they need: Count your words. Two to four means two to four — six words is zero marks even if the English is beautiful.
  5. Listening, 10 minutes. Amy and Sophie on money and a year of spending nothing: exercise 10 (five True / False / Not stated), exercise 11 (five four-option questions). Audio B2_Diagnostic_Test_audio.mp3. Give them one minute to read the questions first — say so explicitly — then play it twice, straight through, with thirty seconds between.
  6. Cue the audio before the lesson and check the volume at the back of the room while the class is doing the grammar section.
📺 On screen: audio played from your phone or laptop through the classroom speaker, plus a visible timer. Backup: if the audio fails, print the audioscript from the publisher's pack and read the dialogue aloud yourself, twice, at a natural pace — the two parts are Amy and Sophie and you can do both. Do not skip the listening section: it is the only listening data you will have until November.
👦 Students do: read the questions first, then work silently, leaving blanks rather than stalling.

73–85′
Speaking carousel + the baseline made visible
inner and outer circle
Why: Ending a diagnostic lesson with production, not with a score, is the difference between a class that comes back next week confident and a class that comes back braced. The prompts are chosen live from the data, so the weakest nest gets its first repair in the same lesson.

WHAT WE DO: the lesson ends in production, and the class sees its own profile on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Mark the grammar and use-of-English sections together from the key, in a different colour, four minutes. The listening section you mark yourself afterwards — it takes two minutes and it protects the honesty of the data.
  2. Now put the class profile on the board as a bar for each nest: how many of the group got each grammar item right. This is not a ranking of you. It is a ranking of the six things, and it is the order I am going to teach them in.
  3. Then the carousel, six minutes, so the lesson does not end on a test. Inner circle and outer circle facing each other, ninety seconds per pair, rotate the outer circle on a clap. Three prompts, one per rotation: Tell your partner about something that happened to you this summer — one thing that was going on when something else interrupted it. · If you could change one thing about your school, what would it be and why? · What is the best advice you have ever been given, and who gave it to you?
  4. Note that the three prompts are the three nests the class was weakest on — choose them after you have seen the paper, not before. That is the whole point of running the carousel after the diagnostic and not before.
📺 On screen: the class profile drawn as six bars on the board. Backup: if you would rather not draw it, read the six numbers aloud — but drawing it is worth the ninety seconds; teenagers argue with a bar chart and ignore a list.
👦 Students do: mark their own paper, look at the class profile, then speak for ninety seconds to three different partners.

Workbook p.99, exercise 1 — the essay, 140–190 words
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will have used six B1 grammar nests productively in speech and in team decision-making, and the teacher will hold a written profile of which of the six the group can and cannot use under time pressure.
  • Subsidiary. Judging accuracy and justifying the judgement out loud; forming questions from prompts; listening for True / False / Not stated and for detail; working to a word limit in a rephrasing task.
  • Personal. In the auction, resist announcing the answer. Every second you wait for the team to justify its bid is a second of real reasoning about English.
By the end, students can…
  • tell a story with past simple, past continuous and past perfect in one sentence
  • choose between present perfect and past simple by looking at the time expression
  • form a question from a prompt without translating word for word
  • spot and correct six of the commonest B1 errors in other people's English
  • rephrase a sentence inside a two-to-four-word limit

Structure
I was walking home when I realised I had left my keys in the classroom. · I've known him for five years. · If I had more time, I'd take a second language. · The results are published in June. · She asked me where I lived. · You don't have to come, but you should.
Form
for + a length of time, since + a point in time, both with the present perfect · after if, the present for a real future and the past for an unreal one — never will and never would · reported questions take statement word order and no auxiliary · mustn't forbids, don't have to frees, and Russian «не надо» covers both.
Pron. watch
the weak forms are where B1 grammar becomes inaudible and therefore unproduced. have to → /ˈhæftə/, would have → /ˈwʊdəv/, had already → /ədɔːlˈredi/ with the h gone entirely. Drill three whole sentences at natural speed in the auction stage — students who expect three separate words hear none of them, and the diagnostic listening will punish exactly that.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
present perfect with for: «I've known him for five years» — do I know him now? — Yes. When did it start? — Five years ago.
second conditional: «If I had more time, I'd take a second language» — do I have more time? — No. Is this about the past? — No, it's about now.
don't have to vs mustn't: «You don't have to come» — am I allowed to come? — Yes. Is it necessary? — No. And «you mustn't come» — am I allowed? — No.
ICQ before the diagnostic: Three sections — which numbers? How many times do you hear the recording? What do you do in the minute before it starts?

Assumptions: Students met all six nests during a B1+ course and can use most of them in controlled practice. They will be unstable in free speech, especially with the present perfect and the second conditional, and several will not know the grammatical names at all — which is fine, because nothing today requires them.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The auction becomes a shouting match and eats twenty-five minutes.Twenty seconds of bidding per sentence, on a visible timer, and a thirty-second team conference before the first bid. If you are behind after ten sentences, raise the starting price so items sell faster — do not cut the justification step, that is the learning.
A team argues that a 'wrong' sentence is acceptable English.Sometimes they are right — I've seen him yesterday is wrong, but Have you had your hair cut? genuinely has two possible readings. If the argument is good, pay them. Being able to defend a borderline sentence is a B2 skill and rewarding it is worth more than a tidy answer key.
Thirty minutes of test is too much for this group.Cut exercise 3 (the five-gap story) and keep exercises 1, 2, 7, 10 and 11. That loses five marks of grammar data and saves six minutes. Do not cut the listening — it is the only listening baseline of the term.
The audio does not play, or the room cannot hear it.Cue it and check the back row before the lesson, not during it. If it fails, read the audioscript aloud twice yourself at natural speed. Half the value of a listening diagnostic survives a teacher-read version; none of it survives skipping the section.
Students want to know their score.Give it privately, on the paper, and give the profile publicly by nest rather than by name. I will tell you your number if you ask me. I am not putting anybody's number on that board.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 19–25 (grammar in a text) and tasks 1–9 (listening).

Task 19–25 is not a grammar quiz — it is a text with the verbs taken out, and the candidate has to put back a tense they were never asked to name. That is exactly what the diagnostic's grammar section does: a story with five gaps, a rewriting task, a choice of alternatives. Whatever the class cannot do today is the list of what the passive, the conditionals and reported speech will have to be built on later in the year, and the teacher now has that list on paper.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
