🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Use strong adjectives with the right adverbs (absolutely freezing, really enormous — never very enormous), put adjectives in the English order (a gorgeous brand new leather sofa), and talk about a photograph for one minute without stopping, using the phrases that work when the word will not come.
What is genuinely hard
Strong adjectives are already at maximum: very cannot make enormous bigger, and Russian happily says очень огромный. The order of adjectives is invisible to a Russian ear and immediately audible to an English one. And the speaking task is a stamina problem, not a language problem — a minute is much longer than teenagers think, and the silence usually arrives at forty seconds.
Typical error to expect
«very enormous», «absolutely big» — strong adjectives take absolutely and really; normal ones take very, extremely and incredibly. Mixing them is not a small mistake: it is the difference between sounding B1 and sounding B2.
«a leather brand new gorgeous sofa» — English fixes the order (opinion → age → colour → material) and a native speaker hears the wrong order instantly. Two minutes of drill buys a mark in the speaking test.
Why the lesson is built this way
The strong adjectives are taught as PAIRS with their normal version, never alone, because the pair is what makes the adverb rule obvious. Then the listening: six short conversations, three options each, done twice as the exam does it — and between the two hearings the class does the single most useful thing in exam listening, which is to underline the key word in each option. The speaking half is built backwards: first the rescue phrases (it looks like, some kind of, a bit like), then the model, then a full minute with a partner timing it, so nobody discovers at the exam that a minute is sixty seconds long.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Very cold is normal. Absolutely freezing is B2. Today: seven adjectives that are already at maximum, the order English puts them in — and one minute of talking about a photograph, timed, with the four phrases that save you when you do not know the word.”

Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a written 90-second retrieval of the comparatives with spelling marked, and the bridge into strong adjectives

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what have you listened to today? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — yesterday's comparatives, written not spoken. Four adjectives on the board: big · cosy · good · far. Everyone writes the comparative and the superlative of each on a scrap of paper. Ninety seconds.
  3. Swap with a neighbour and mark. Eight forms, eight marks, and the spelling counts — announce that before they start, because the block test does exactly this.
  4. Take the two that went wrong most often and write them correctly, large, on the board.
  5. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): open the page: Page forty-eight, the right-hand side. Some adjectives are already at maximum.

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and put up the one rule that runs all lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Two sentences on the board: It's a big house. / It's an enormous house. One question: which one is stronger, and can you say very in front of both?
  2. Write the rule that survives the whole lesson: NORMAL + very / extremely / incredibly · STRONG + absolutely. Really goes with both — it is the diplomat.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2: the table sorted, then written as twins

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1, thirty seconds: a big house or an enormous house — which is stronger? Answer: enormous.
  2. ex.2 individually, three minutes: fourteen adjectives sorted into NORMAL and STRONG. Answers: normal — old, bad, big, cold, good, new, tired; strong — ancient, terrible, enormous, freezing, spectacular, brand new, exhausted.
  3. Check with Kagan Showdown, then build the third column of the wall as TWINS, one under the other: old / ancient · bad / terrible · big / enormous · cold / freezing · good / spectacular · new / brand new · tired / exhausted.
  4. Drill exhausted and ancient chorally — /ɪɡˈzɔːstɪd/ is the hardest sound in the unit and it needs four repetitions, not one.
  5. Thirty seconds of meaning, said once: Strong adjectives are not «more polite» or «more literary». They are the same meaning at maximum.

👦 Students do: sort fourteen words alone, reveal together, then read the twins aloud as pairs

WHAT WE DO: ex.3–4: derive the rule, then the pairwork on SB p.135

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies.

WHAT WE DO: a four-minute stirrer on the adverb rule

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everyone stands. You call an adjective; the class says the adverb in chorus — very for normal, absolutely for strong. Twenty words at speed.
  2. Then the trap round: you say a WRONG collocation (very enormous) and the class sits down if it is wrong, stays standing if it is right. Six of them, half wrong.
  3. Last thirty seconds, in pairs: one true sentence about this room with a strong adjective.
  4. Freeze and sit: Page forty-nine. Six conversations, and in every one of them you will hear all three answers.
📺 On screen: the boxed rule, still on the board — nothing to fail
👦 Students do: everyone answers in chorus and everyone judges six collocations with their body

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 = B1 Listening Part 2: the example first, then six items, heard twice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.1, track 30: the example conversation with three options. Play it once and ask for the answer (C) AND the reason. This is the model for the method, so do it slowly.
  2. Before the exam task, the method on the board: 1 read the question — what does it actually ask? · 2 underline the KEY WORD in each of the three options · 3 first listening: choose · 4 second listening: check the two you rejected.
  3. Ninety seconds, silent: read the six questions and underline the key words. Walk the room and check that everyone has eighteen underlinings, not six.
  4. ex.2, track 31: play the whole thing once. Then a ninety-second pair comparison — reasons, not letters. Then play it a second time, exactly as the exam does.
  5. Check: 2 A, 3 B, 4 C, 5 B, 6 A. For every item, ask which of the other two was also mentioned and why it was wrong — that is the whole skill.

Why: Speaking · what to say when the word will not come

WHAT WE DO: ex.5: the Prepare to speak phrases, drilled until they are automatic

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. ex.5: six rescue phrases matched into the gaps — looks like, things like that, some kind of, a bit like, seems, more like.
  2. Write all six on the board in a box headed WHEN YOU DON'T KNOW THE WORD. This box is the point of the whole lesson; everything else supports it.
  3. Drill them as a physical game, three minutes: you hold up (or point at) an object nobody can name in English — a stapler, a radiator, a plug socket, a hole punch — and pairs must describe it in ten seconds using a phrase from the box. It's some kind of thing for paper. It looks like a small machine.
  4. Rule, said once and enforced: Nobody says the Russian word. If you don't know it, you go round it — that is exactly what the exam is testing.
  5. Take three of the best descriptions to the floor and let the class guess the object.
📺 On screen: the six-phrase rescue box on the board — it stays up for the final stage, so nothing can fail
👦 Students do: describe four unnameable objects in pairs, in ten seconds each, without a single Russian word

Why: 🎯 Speaking out · one minute on one photograph

WHAT WE DO: ex.6: the full exam task, timed by a partner, twice

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set it up exactly as the exam does: one photograph (SB p.136), one candidate, about one minute, no preparation notes.
  2. Round 1, pairs: A talks for sixty seconds while B times it on a phone and points silently at the rescue box when A stops. Then swap. Four minutes total.
  3. Feedback, ninety seconds, in pairs and in English: You stopped once. You used two phrases from the box. You didn't say what the people are doing.
  4. Put the four imaginary questions on the board and let them plan for thirty seconds: Where is it? Who is there? What are they doing? How do they feel?
  5. Round 2, new partners, sixty seconds each again. The instruction is one line: Same photograph, more adjectives, and answer all four questions.
  6. Record two pairs on a phone in round 2 and play thirty seconds of one back to the class, with permission. The recording teaches more than any correction you give live.
  7. Close with the honest number: Everybody in this room has now talked about a photograph for two minutes in total. In the exam it is one.
📺 On screen: the four questions and the rescue box, both on the board during both rounds — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: speak for sixty seconds twice, time a partner twice, and give feedback in English twice

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
very + strong adjective keeps coming backOne line on the board all lesson: ABSOLUTELY goes with the strong one. Every offence gets the same three-word answer — «absolutely, not very» — and the sentence is repeated.
In the listening, all three options are heardBetween the two hearings, the class underlines the key word in each option. Then the question becomes which of the three the speaker OPINES about, not which one is mentioned.
The one-minute talk dies at forty secondsThe rescue phrases go on the board before the first attempt and stay there, and the partner's job is to point at them, silently, when the speaker stops. Nobody is allowed to rescue in Russian.
Fast finishers in the vocabulary stageTwo extra items in the same shape (a normal/strong pair with a gap) written for the next round of Showdown — theirs get used, which is why they write them properly.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 1–9 (the listening section) and tasks 39–40 (the spoken part, where a photograph has to be described for a minute).

Six short conversations with three options each is the shape of the exam's listening block, and the trap is identical: all three options are said aloud, and only one answers the question that was asked. The speaking half is even closer — the Russian oral asks the candidate to talk about a photograph without stopping, and the phrases learned today (it looks like, some kind of, a bit like) are exactly what keeps a candidate talking when the word will not come.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
