🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name eight ingredients and five ways of cooking them, use make and do correctly in ten fixed phrases, and write a number or one word into a gap while listening.
What is genuinely hard
make and do. There is no rule to reason it out from — Russian uses one verb where English uses two, and the split is arbitrary. It is a list, and the only thing that works on a list is sorting it, saying it and meeting it again.
Typical error to expect
I make my homework / I do a mistake instead of do your homework and make a mistake. There is no rule to reason it out from; it is a list, and SB p.111 ex.5 is that list [high priority, A2/B1]
In a listening gap, twelve written out instead of 12. A number written in letters can be misspelt, and a misspelt answer scores nothing — write figures (TB p.218) [exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
Four short vocabulary stages rather than one long one, because eight ingredients, five cooking words, nine table words and ten collocations cannot be held in a single block of attention — each stage ends with the words being used rather than listed. The make/do slot is deliberately the longest of the four and deliberately physical: it is the highest-frequency error in the whole course and the only thing that shifts it is drilling the phrase, not the word. The listening comes after all the food vocabulary so that nothing in the recording is unknown, and its one rule — write numbers as figures — is worth more marks than any other sentence in this block. The lesson ends with a team task with a genuine decision inside it, because a team that has to agree on one recipe has to disagree first, and that is what the useful-language box is for.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“You do the shopping and you make a cake. Both of those are one word in Russian, and in English getting them the wrong way round is the mistake I hear more than any other in this room. Today we fix it — and then your team enters a cooking competition and the class votes.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, four rounds of passive retrieval, and the bridge into the vocabulary

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — passive rewind. Kagan Showdown, four rounds: you say an active sentence, the team writes the passive and the boards go up together. They sell cakes here. · We need more chairs. · People eat pasta everywhere. · Somebody cleans this room every day.
  3. One error from Thursday on the board, no names, repaired chorally: Bread eaten with butter.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): Books open, page one hundred and eleven. Today: what food is called, how it is cooked, and one pair of verbs that costs this class more marks than anything else.
📺 On screen: the four sentences read out one at a time and the correction written in chalk — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: write four passives against the clock. Expect the missing is to have almost disappeared and the third form to be the remaining wobble. Typical problem — are ate, which is the participle again

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: name the language aim of the lesson and the standard an answer has to reach

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write two sentences on the board: I do the shopping. and I make a cake.
  2. In Russian these two verbs are one verb. In English they are two, and there is no rule. What do you do with something that has no rule? (learn the phrase, not the word).
  3. Explain the aim in 40 seconds: today is the vocabulary of a kitchen — what things are called, how they are cooked, and the two verbs that go with them.
  4. Set the bar: Nobody says «make» or «do» on its own today. You say the whole phrase, every time.
📺 On screen: the two model sentences in chalk with a two-column table under them — the board is the medium here and the table fills up all lesson, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: compare the two sentences and answer the question about the rule. Expect at least one student to insist there must be a rule — say honestly that there is not, and that this is why the phrases get drilled

Vocabulary · eight ingredients
pairs → class
Why: Eight food words learnt from photographs and then immediately sorted into categories, because a word that has been put somewhere is remembered better than a word that has been translated.

WHAT WE DO: ex.1–2 p.111 (track 141): eight photos, then the four food groups

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed, elicit first: Name three vegetables. Name three fruits. Name a kind of meat. Write what comes up on the board.
  2. Books open, ex.1 in pairs, 3 minutes. Play track 141 to check and repeat chorally. Key: A salt and pepper · B carrots · C potatoes · D melon · E steak · F beans · G pears · H garlic.
  3. ex.2: the four-column table. Ask the two questions the Teacher's Book asks first — Which heading would you put apples under? (fruit) Cheese? (other).
  4. Two minutes to add as many extra words as they can to the four columns, then share. Key and possible extras: Meat: steak (+ fish, chicken, beef, ham) · Fruit: melon, pears (+ oranges, bananas) · Vegetables: beans, carrots, garlic, potatoes (+ tomatoes) · Other: salt and pepper (+ rice, egg, bread, pasta).
  5. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with picture cards, 3 minutes, five trades each.
📺 On screen: track 141 in the board app with the eight photos on the screen (Backup if the board is down: the photos are printed on SB p.111 — say the eight words yourself and have the class point at the photo in the paper book, then repeat chorally; the Quiz-Quiz-Trade cards are printed in advance and need no technology)
👦 Students do: match, check, sort, then mingle. Expect 5–6 out of 8 unaided. Typical errors: garlic unknown to nearly everybody, and pears confused with peaches

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.111: five ways of cooking matched back to the photos on p.110

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Check the meanings first, quickly, with the class: In an oven with no water? (baked or roast) In water? (boiled) In oil? (fried) Under the heat? (grilled)

  4. Kagan RallyCoach: the partner names a food and you say how it is usually cooked, in a full sentence — Potatoes are usually boiled or roast. That sentence is Thursday's grammar, and it should come out by itself.

28–36′
Vocabulary · what you eat it with
pairs
Why: Knife, mug, bowl — the words are known and the distinctions are not. Defining them to a partner is what shows whether dish and plate are actually separate in their heads.

WHAT WE DO: ex.4 p.111: knife, fork, spoon · mug, glass, cup · bowl, plate, dish

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed, 90 seconds: write down every word you know for something you eat or drink with. Books open and check against the exercise.
  2. The exercise in pairs, 4 minutes: say what each thing is for. Key: 1 You use a spoon to drink soup with, or for dessert, for example ice cream. · 2 A mug is a kind of cup, usually big and heavy, for coffee or hot chocolate; a glass is for water or juice; a cup is usually for hot drinks like tea. · 3 A bowl is a round deep dish for soup or cereal or for serving vegetables; you eat your main course from a plate; a dish is bigger and is used for serving — and a dish can also mean a particular food served at a meal..
  3. Check by naming a food and asking what you need: Ice cream? (a spoon and a bowl) Roast chicken? (a knife, a fork and a plate) Hot chocolate? (a mug).
  4. One extra meaning worth thirty seconds: a dish is also a particular food served at a meal — my favourite dish — and that is the sense the last stage of the lesson needs.
📺 On screen: the nine words on the screen in three groups (Backup: they are printed on SB p.111 — work from the page and write the three groups on the board)
👦 Students do: define nine words to a partner. Expect mug and cup to be treated as the same word and dish to be new in both meanings. Typical error: plate used for soup

36–46′
Vocabulary · make and do
class → pairs
Why: There is no rule here, only a list, so the stage is built as sorting and drilling rather than as explanation. This is one of the highest-frequency errors in the whole language.

WHAT WE DO: ex.5–6 p.111: ten collocations sorted, drilled and then made personal

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Start with the question that produces the rule of the day: All those knives and plates are dirty. What do you do? (wash them up) Do you DO or MAKE the washing-up? (do).
  2. ex.5 in pairs, 3 minutes: sort the ten phrases into the two columns. Key: make: a cup of tea, the bed, a cake, a mess, a mistake · do: the cleaning, the dishes, your homework, the shopping, the washing.
  3. Drill every phrase chorally, and drill the WHOLE phrase — never the verb on its own. Three rounds, faster each time.
  4. ex.6 in pairs: which of these do you sometimes, often or never do at home? Kagan Think-Pair-Share, one round.
  5. Physical round to finish, 90 seconds: Left hand up for MAKE, right hand up for DO. You call the noun: the bed · the dishes · a mistake · your homework · a mess · the shopping · a cup of tea · the cleaning.
📺 On screen: the two-column table on the board, filled in live as the class sorts — the board is the medium here and the table stays up for the rest of the block, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: sort, drill, then say what they actually do at home. Expect make a mistake and do your homework to be the two that go wrong. Typical error: I make my homework — which is the single most common error in the whole course and the reason this stage is ten minutes long

48–62′
Listening · the school chef competition
solo → pairs
Why: Note completion with three numeric answers out of five — the densest listening of the block and the one that most rewards knowing what KIND of answer each gap needs.

WHAT WE DO: the Listening on p.111 (track 142): five notes to complete, one word or a number in each

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The format in two lines: A woman gives information about a cooking competition. Five notes to complete under the heading SCHOOL CHEF COMPETITION: the last date to enter, the number of teams, where the competition will be, the name of the chef, and the third prize.
  2. Before anything plays, 90 seconds on the notes themselves: Look at the five gaps. Which of them wants a number? Which wants a name? Three of the five are numbers, and knowing that is most of the work.
  3. Say the one rule that saves marks: TB p.218, the one rule that saves marks: write numbers as numbers — 12, not twelve — so that a spelling mistake cannot cost you the item. And read every gap before the recording starts: three of these five want a number or a date, and knowing that is most of the work.
  4. Play track 142. First time you answer. Thirty seconds of silence with pens down. Then play it again: Second time you check.
  5. Pairs compare only after the second play, then Kagan Showdown. Key: 1 2nd / 2 / second · 2 8 / eight · 3 university · 4 Wright (not Right) · 5 money.
  6. Name the trap after the check: Where marks go: gap 4. The name is Wright and it is spelt out in the recording; students who are listening for a word rather than for letters write Right and lose the mark on a homophone.
📺 On screen: track 142 in the board app with the notes on the screen (Backup: the notes are printed on SB p.111 and the audioscript is on TB p.304 — read it out yourself, twice, at natural speed, and spell the name in gap 4 exactly as the script does)
👦 Students do: predict, listen twice, then compare. Expect 3–4 out of 5. Typical error: gap 4 written as Right — the homophone, and the reason for the warning before the recording

WHAT WE DO: the Talking points box p.110 (video 15) — three questions and one comparison

70–86′
🎯 Speaking out · the Junior Chef competition
teams of 3–4
Why: Freer practice with a real decision inside it: a team that has to agree on one recipe has to disagree first, and the useful-language box is the tool for disagreeing politely.

WHAT WE DO: the Speaking task p.111: decide on a recipe, share out the jobs, and present it to the class

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the task: In teams of three or four: you are entering the Junior Chef cooking competition. Decide on a recipe — the ingredients, how you will cook it, who does which job (shopping, cutting, cooking on the day, washing up) and three reasons why it is a good meal to serve in a school café. Then present it, and the class votes.
  2. Put the useful language on the board and teach the grammar inside it — two minutes, and it saves the whole stage: TB p.218, the grammar hidden inside the useful-language box, and it is worth two minutes: Why don't we …? and Let's … take the base form · What about …? takes the ‑ing form · I'd prefer … takes the to-infinitive.
  3. The phrases themselves: Can you cut the vegetables? · I'd prefer not to do the dishes. · I'll buy the ingredients. · I'm not sure about that. · Let's … · That's a great idea. · That sounds good! · What about …? · Why don't we …?
  4. Planning, 7 minutes, in teams. Insist on the jobs being shared out — the book asks for it and it is what stops one student writing everything.
  5. Presentations, 60 seconds per team. The listeners have a job: each team writes down one question to ask, and asks it.
  6. Vote for the dish that goes into the school café, with one sentence of justification from three students.
  7. Log errors silently on your correction sheet; do not correct aloud — the feedback slot is the start of the next lesson.
📺 On screen: the useful-language box and the three grammar patterns written on the board as a checklist — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed
👦 Students do: plan a recipe as a team, then present it. Expect the disagreement phrases to be used for real, which is the point of the stage. Typical error: What about make a pizza? — the ‑ing form, which is exactly why the three patterns went up first

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can name and classify eight ingredients and five ways of cooking, and use ten make/do collocations without stopping to think.
  • Subsidiary. Knife, fork, spoon, mug, glass, cup, bowl, plate, dish; completing five notes from a recording; agreeing and disagreeing politely in a team.

By the end, students can…
  • name eight ingredients and sort them into food groups
  • say how a dish was cooked — baked, boiled, fried, grilled or roast
  • use ten make and do phrases correctly
  • write a number or one word into a gap while listening

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
«do the shopping» or «make the shopping»? → do. And there is no reason; it is a list.
«make a mistake» or «do a mistake»? → make. In Russian both of these are the same verb, which is exactly why they have to be learnt as phrases.
A steak cooked under the heat from above — baked, grilled or roast? → grilled.
You are eating soup. Bowl or plate? → bowl, because it is round and deep.
In the listening, gap 2 asks for the number of teams. Do you write eight or 8? → 8. A word can be misspelt; a figure cannot.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
make and do are still swapped at the end of the lessonThey will be, in about a third of the class, and that is normal on day one of a list. Leave the two columns on the board for the rest of the block and add to them whenever a new phrase appears. The test asks about four of them, and the workbook asks about twenty.
The team task turns into one confident student writing a recipeGive the roles out before the task, not during it: shopping, cutting, cooking on the day, washing up. The book itself asks who does which job — use it as the structure, not as an afterthought.
Nobody can hear the name in gap 4 of the listeningIt is spelt out in the recording and it is a homophone: Wright, not Right. Warn them that a name will be spelt, and that when a name is spelt they write letters, not a word.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
