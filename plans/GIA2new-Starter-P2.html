🏠HomeworkA description of your own family with each person's appearance, from a model (Teacher's Book) + cards
🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Why it is built this way (for the teacher): The second part of the card carries the heaviest vocabulary load in the whole of Starter: 14 family words plus 17 appearance words plus two grammar points. So the pair is built in steps and with permanent supports: the family tree stays on the board the whole lesson, the cards lie on the desks, and the description table is built together, not handed down. The three reader profiles (p.8) are cut into pieces — one per crew, then put back together, so the amount of text doesn't frighten anyone. The product is gentle and exciting at the same time: you describe a person, the crew guesses — the motivation to be precise appears without any bar being set.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Part two of your card. Today: your family, and what people look like. Look at the board — this is MY family tree. Grandmother, mother, aunt, cousin… By the end of today you'll have your own tree, and you'll describe one person so well that your crew guesses who it is. Everything you need is on the desk: cards, the tree, the word bank. Nobody works from memory today.”
In simpler words, if the class needs support: Part two of the card: my family and what I look like. We build our own family tree and learn to describe a person well enough for the crew to guess who it is. The cards, the tree on the board and the word bank stay in place the whole lesson.

WHAT WE DO: I name the topic openly → ex.6 table from a model → ex.7 in writing → ex.8 the apostrophe via the two questions → mini-test with self-check

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. I announce it plainly: Grammar today: my, your, his, her… and the apostrophe.
  2. THE MODEL before the task, as the Teacher's Book says: I point at objects and say — This is MY book. That is YOUR pencil. This is HER chair. This is OUR classroom. Then I leave a gap and they finish it.
  3. We fill in ex.6 together: key 1 your · 2 his · 3 its · 4 our · 5 their. Separately: his = whose, he's = he is. its = whose, it's = it is.
  4. ex.7 in writing off the table: key 1 His · 2 their · 3 our · 4 her · 5 your.
  5. ex.8 the apostrophe — two questions go on the board and stay there until the end of the pair: “how many owners?” and “does the word already end in -s?” Key: 1 dad’s · 2 grandparents’ · 3 uncle’s · 4 cousins’.
  6. A 6-line mini-test → self-check against the key → sticker.
  7. 🟢🟡🔴 Self-check stickers: after every mini-test the student sticks a sticker in the margin themselves: 🟢 got it · 🟡 so-so · 🔴 didn't get it. This is NOT a mark — it is my map of who to go to during the next task. I say this out loud every single time.

board → solo → pairs

WHAT WE DO: I name the topic openly → ex.4–6 three tables from a model → ex.7 questions in writing → pair survey AFTER the writing

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. I announce it: Grammar: have got. It means 'I have / I own'.
  2. THE MODEL: I describe myself — I've got brown eyes. I haven't got glasses. My sister has got long hair.
  3. ex.4 key: 1 haven’t · 2 has · 3 hasn’t. ex.5 key: 1 ’s got · 2 haven’t got · 3 ’ve got · 4 haven’t got · 5 hasn’t got.
  4. Two rules on their own line on the board: 3rd person = HAS got. and Negative + plural = any: 'They haven't got ANY children'.
  5. ex.6 short answers: key 1 haven’t · 2 Has · 3 hasn’t · 4 have. Short answer: 'Yes, he has' — NOT 'Yes, he has got'.
  6. ex.7 five questions IN WRITING, then ex.8 — the pair survey off what they wrote.
  7. Kagan RallyRobin: question — short answer — swap, three rounds.
📺 On screen: SB p.9 ex.4–8 · the have got tables on the board (they stay) · the question frame
👦 Students do: they fill the tables in from the model, write the questions, then ask them. Expect: the written fixing removes the fear again — everybody speaks. Problem: “Yes, he has got” — the crossed-out version stays up on the board

PRODUCT · my card: family and appearance
solo → crews

WHAT WE DO: the product: 5 lines (3 family + 2 appearance) on the frame → the “guess who I described” game in the crew

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 7 minutes of writing on the frame: Three lines about your family: 'I've got …' / 'My … is …' / 'My …'s name is …'. Two lines about a person: 'He/She has got …'. A character is allowed — the rule was announced.
  2. A checklist on the desk, three points: has got with he/she? · apostrophe checked against the two questions? · is the description kind?
  3. Kagan Timed Pair Share: 40 seconds reading your five lines to a partner → swap. With the sheet.
  4. The game in the crew: Read your two description lines. No name. Your crew guesses WHO it is — someone in this room, or someone we all know.
  5. The speaker takes the one best riddle out to the class.
  6. I praise specifically: Great detail! We guessed straight away! — I don't go over errors at the board.
📺 On screen: the card frame (5 lines) · the 3-point checklist · word bank · the description table on the board
👦 Students do: they write on the frame and play the guessing game. Expect: the game makes precision interesting all by itself — they start adding details without being reminded. Problem: a description with one feature in it (“She's tall”) — the crew doesn't guess, and that is the best lesson of all

🏠
Homework

Core (the task from the Teacher's Book, p. 16): write a description of your family — 1–2 sentences about each of them: who it is and what they look like (My uncle Sasha has got short grey hair and glasses.). At least 4 people. A character's family is allowed. A worked example of the task is on the slide.

🌱 Optional: draw your own family tree and label it in English — it becomes a support in P4.

🔁 Words: the Starter cards — the “family” (14) and “appearance” (17) sets, 5–7 minutes.

⚠️ There is NO Starter section in the Workbook — the homework comes from the Teacher's Book + the cards · the tree will be useful in pair 4
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, learners can write 5 lines about their family and describe a person's appearance using possessive adjectives, possessive ’s/s’ and have got, well enough for the crew to identify the person.
  • Subsidiary. 14 family words sorted into Male/Female/Both with support (SB p.8 ex.1–2); the three reader profiles read in pieces (ex.3–5); the description table built together (p.9 ex.1–2) and checked against audio 5 with the table as a support; the have got tables (ex.4–6) filled in from a model.
  • Personal. I state the compliments-only rule BEFORE the first description, not after an incident. And I give a choice: your own family OR a character's family — I announce it straight away, so that nobody has to opt out in public.
By the end, students can…
  • sort and use 14 family words
  • use my/your/his/her/its/our/their correctly
  • put ’s and s’ in the right place
  • describe a person with have got so the crew identifies them

Structure
I/You/We/They have got black hair. / haven't got blue eyes. · He/She/It has got short hair. / hasn't got glasses. · My nephew's name is Jia. (singular + 's) · Their sons' names are Caleb and Damien. (plural in -s + ')
Form
I → my · you → your · he → his · she → her · it → its · we → our · they → their · in the negative with have got, a plural noun takes any: They haven't got any children. · 3rd person: has got / hasn't got (not have) · the short answer never repeats got: Yes, he has (not «Yes, he has got»)
Pron. watch
from the Wordlist: aunt /ɑːnt/ — “ahnt”, not “aynt” · nephew /ˈnefjuː/ · niece /niːs/ · cousin /ˈkʌz(ə)n/ — “KUZ-n” · moustache /məˈstɑːʃ/ · glasses /ˈɡlɑːsɪz/
Concept checks (CCQs) · Assumptions
«his» or «he's»? → his = whose (his name), he's = he is (he's tall).
«its» or «it's»? → its = possessive, it's = it is.
«my parents' car» — why is the apostrophe after the s? → parents is already plural in -s, so we add only '.
«She have got a cat» — what's wrong? → she has got.
«Yes, I have got» — is that the short answer? → no, the short one is: Yes, I have.

Assumptions: SB pp.8–9, KTP pair 2 (lessons 3–4). Almost everyone knows the basic family words (mother, father, sister, brother); new for this track are nephew, niece, parent, and the apostrophe in the plural (s’). The pace is slower than in the “ours” track — that is normal.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The amount of vocabulary is frightening: 14 + 17 words in one pairIn steps and with supports: the family tree stays on the board the whole lesson, the cards lie on the desks, the description table gets built together. Nothing is learnt “out of your head”.
Three reader profiles — too much text at onceCut it up: one profile per crew. The crew finds its own facts and tells the others. The whole text comes together on the board, not inside each head.
Appearance descriptions turn into digs at each otherThe rule before the task: compliments only. Break it and the crew loses the round. I say this exactly once, but before, not after.
They put the apostrophe in at randomTwo test questions stay on the board the whole lesson: “how many owners?” and “does the word already end in -s?” One → ’s. Many and already with -s → just ’.
Classroom management — signals
«Eyes up in 3-2-1» — count down calmly → all eyes on you at «1». No shouting over noise, ever.
Visible timer — timer on screen = we work; timer stops = pens/hands down. The timer is the boss, not you.
Volume dial — arm up/down = louder/quieter; «radio whisper» for rehearsals.
«Boards up!» — mini-whiteboard rounds: everyone commits, all boards up together — nobody hides, nobody shouts first.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, class answers with two and freezes — instant reset mid-game.
English-first — ideas in Russian are welcome, POINTS only for the English version — recast and repeat.
