🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Why it is built this way (for the teacher): The weather vocabulary comes in not as a list but as a working tool of a weather service: pp.48–49 already have the map 'The United States of extreme weather', and the crews have a job — to prove that their region deserves a place on that map next to Death Valley and the Great Lakes. The words colder/wetter appear as 'the bureau's language of comparison', not as the topic 'comparative degree' — that is the work of P3. We watch the video 'Four seasons in a week' as fellow meteorologists.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Agents, the map is on the screen. Death Valley: six centimetres of rain a year. Seattle: cloudy 220 days. New Hampshire: fog 300 days a year. This is the map of extreme weather — and our region is not on it. Your crew has ninety minutes to prove it should be. Words, numbers, one comparison with the USA, and a sixty-second bulletin at the end. 'It's cold here' is not evidence. Start the clock!”
In simpler words, if the class needs support: On the screen there is a map of extreme weather in the USA; our region is not on it. The crews have to prove it belongs there: words, numbers, one comparison with the USA and a 60-second weather bulletin at the end.

WHAT WE DO: a level for our students: the language of a weather bureau — averages, days per year, comparison (with no grammar announced)

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:

WHAT WE DO: a 60-second bulletin: the weather of our region across the four seasons + three numbers + one comparison with the US map + the verdict 'we deserve the map or we don't'

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 7 minutes to prepare: Keywords only, no scripts. Everyone owns one season — plus one person for the numbers and the verdict.

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, crews can use 27 weather and season words (nouns + -y adjectives) and deliver a 60-second weather bulletin about their own region with numbers and one comparison with the US map.
  • Subsidiary. The gate exposes the real vocabulary ceiling; ex.1 and ex.3 p.48 are closed by a race; the ex.4 table (noun → adjective) is worked out as a rule by the students themselves; track 24 (five sounds) runs as a challenge; Video skills p.49 is watched with a task; the first comparatives come in context, with no topic announced.
  • Personal. I do NOT announce the comparative as the topic: it turns up as 'the bureau's language'. Form errors (more hot, hotest) I log silently — that is the material for the clinic in P3. This is the first pair after the holidays: I set the pace from the doorway and leave no gaps.
By the end, students can…
  • write 15+ weather and season words in 90 seconds
  • sort words into seasons / temperature / weather
  • build the adjective from the noun (sun → sunny, ice → icy)
  • give a 60-second weather bulletin with numbers and one comparison

Structure
In winter it's freezing and there's snow and ice. · It's foggy here about thirty days a year. · Seattle is cloudy for 220 days — we are cloudier than that. · 🚀 bonus: The average temperature in January is …
Form
noun → adjective most often through -y: cloud → cloudy, wind → windy; in fog and sun the consonant doubles (foggy, sunny), in ice the -e goes (icy).
Pron. watch
from the Wordlist: thunderstorms /ˈθʌndə(r)ˌstɔː(r)mz/ · freezing /ˈfriːzɪŋ/ · showers /ˈʃaʊə(r)z/ · autumn /ˈɔːtəm/ (the n in autumn is silent) · icy /ˈaɪsi/
Concept checks (CCQs) · Assumptions
'It's a rainy' — can we say that? → no: It's rainy / There's rain.
'freezing' — is that just 'cold'? → no, it is 'very, very cold', below zero.
'You have been talking about the weather for four years. Can you write 15 words in 90 seconds?' → gate.

Assumptions: SB pp.48–49, KTP pair 30. Half the words are familiar from A1+ (hot, cold, rain, snow, sunny); the new ones are freezing, showers, thunderstorms, frost/frosty, fog/foggy, wet, dry, cool. The first pair after the midterm and the holidays.

Classroom management — signals
«Eyes up in 3-2-1» — count down calmly → all eyes on you at «1». No shouting over noise, ever.
Visible timer — timer on screen = we work; timer stops = pens/hands down. The timer is the boss, not you.
Volume dial — arm up/down = louder/quieter; «radio whisper» for rehearsals.
«Boards up!» — mini-whiteboard rounds: everyone commits, all boards up together — nobody hides, nobody shouts first.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, class answers with two and freezes — instant reset mid-game.
English-first — ideas in Russian are welcome, POINTS only for the English version — recast and repeat.
