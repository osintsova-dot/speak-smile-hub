🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Revise the year from the group's own error logs rather than from a generic list, and rehearse all four parts of the spoken exam in threes, with everybody taking the examiner's chair.
What is genuinely hard
A revision lesson in the last week of May fails in one of two ways: it is a list of everything, which nobody can hold, or it is a game with no content, which nobody remembers. The cure is that the content is theirs — the three-line logs written yesterday decide what gets revised. The spoken half has its own difficulty: after two written lessons the room is quiet, and quiet has to be broken deliberately rather than hoped away.
Typical error to expect
Revising what you already know because it is comfortable. The logs exist to prevent exactly that, which is why the stations are built from them and not from a syllabus.
Treating the examiner's chair as a rest. It is the hardest and most useful of the three roles: reading the rubric accurately, timing, and using the back-up prompts at the right moment.
Why the lesson is built this way
The first half is built overnight out of the error cards collected yesterday — four stations, four themes, chosen by frequency. That takes the teacher twenty minutes of preparation and it is the difference between a revision lesson and a filler. The second half is the full spoken rehearsal from the publisher's Speaking bank, Parts 1 to 4, in threes with rotating roles, exactly as in lesson 61 but with all four parts joined up. The lesson is also the syllabus's reserve slot, so the plan says explicitly what to cut and what to substitute if a lesson has been lost to a cancelled day.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Today's revision was written by you. I read your twenty-odd cards last night and the four things on the board are the four that came up most often. Then the whole spoken exam, all four parts, in threes — and everyone gets the examiner's chair.”

Why: Two written lessons leave a room silent, and the speaking half of today needs it loud. The mingle is not a warm-up; it is a state change and it is worth two of the ninety minutes.

WHAT WE DO: the rewritten reviews collected and the room made loud again

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — collect the second drafts with the first version stapled behind. Look at each for five seconds: is the first version there? That is what you are checking, not the English.
  2. 2 minutes — Kagan Mix-Pair-Share, standing, and it is here for a reason: after two written lessons this room is quiet, and quiet has to be broken on purpose. Mix, pair, and tell somebody one thing you are actually going to do in the first week of the holidays. Two partners.
  3. Bridge (30 seconds): I read all your cards last night. The four things on the board are yours, not mine.
📺 On screen: no technology in the opening — paper and voices only, and nothing can fail
👦 Students do: hand in a second draft with the first behind it, then speak to two partners standing

Why: Showing the arithmetic — how many cards, how many lines, which four themes — proves the lesson was built from their work, and that proof is what buys the effort.

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and reveal the four station themes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the four station themes on the board, and say where they came from: Twenty-one cards. Sixty-three lines. These four came up most.
  2. Say the shape in 30 seconds: forty minutes on those four, then the whole spoken exam in threes with everybody taking the examiner's chair.
  3. Set the rule that makes the stations work: At each station, somebody who got it RIGHT explains it. I am not explaining anything for the next forty minutes.
  4. Say the choice out loud, because it matters more than the rotation: anybody whose own three lines are all at one station may stay there for two rounds and skip another. It is their revision, not yours.

👦 Students do: find their own three lines among the four themes and decide their own route

The year in four stations · rounds one and two
four groups, rotating
Why: The year in four stations · rounds one and two

WHAT WE DO: twelve minutes, two stations each, taught by students

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Four tables, one theme each, with the material you prepared last night: a short explanation card, six practice items and an answer slip face down.
  2. Groups of four to five. Six minutes per station. At each one, the student who got that theme right on the mock is the EXPLAINER for the first minute; then everybody does the six items; then the answer slip is turned over.
  3. Your job for these twelve minutes is to walk and say nothing except Ask the person who got it right. Hold the line — the moment you explain something, four stations become one lecture.
  4. Rotate on a signal, not on completion. Two stations in this block.
  5. At the end of each station everybody ticks the line in their own log if it now feels fixed. A tick is not a claim to mastery; it is a note to themselves in August.
📺 On screen: the four station tables and the rotation signal — chalk, paper and prepared cards only, and nothing can fail
👦 Students do: explain one point to four people, practise six items at two stations, and tick their own log

The year in four stations · rounds three and four
four groups, rotating
Why: The year in four stations · rounds three and four

WHAT WE DO: twelve more minutes, the other two stations

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Same structure, the remaining two stations. Six minutes each.
  2. Change one thing for these two rounds and announce it: at each station, the LAST two minutes are a two-item test written by the group for the next group. Writing an item is harder than doing one and it is the best diagnostic there is.
  3. Collect the student-written items as you go and keep the good ones — they are next year's warmers.
  4. Two minutes at the end, whole class, with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: one named student per station says the rule of that station in one sentence. Four sentences, and they go on the board.
  5. Those four sentences are the year's revision, on one board, written by the class. Photograph it and send it to them — it is the most useful thing they will get this week.
📺 On screen: the four one-sentence rules written up by students — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: complete the rotation, write two test items for the next group, and state one rule for the whole room

32–36′
Move break · the state change
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies — and in an examination week that is truer, not less true.

WHAT WE DO: four minutes that turn a written lesson into a spoken one

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everybody stands and the desks move if the room allows it — into threes, facing each other.
  2. Sixty seconds of pronunciation, and it is the only drill of the lesson: What do YOU think? Say it flat, then say it as a real invitation. The room repeats it four times until the difference is audible.
  3. Then the same with Could you repeat that, please? — said politely, not apologetically.
  4. Two minutes of Kagan RallyRobin in the new threes: name one thing you would do on a summer activity course and one you would refuse. It is the topic of Part 3 in twenty minutes, so nothing here is wasted.
  5. Sit down in the threes: Nobody writes anything for the rest of this lesson.
📺 On screen: no technology in this stage — voices and furniture only, and nothing can fail
👦 Students do: stand, move the desks, drill two phrases in chorus and speak to two partners

WHAT WE DO: twelve minutes of the publisher's own overview, watched with a job

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand out a four-box slip before you press play: how long the whole exam is · how many parts the speaking has · what the examiner is listening for · one thing that surprised me.
  2. Play the overview film. It is new to them — they saw the paired speaking video in lesson 61, but not this one.
  3. Three minutes, threes, then floor: compare the four boxes. Take the surprises first, because they are the ones that stick.
  4. Then two minutes of the examiner-feedback PDF, read aloud, choosing the lines about listening to each other and about keeping going. Two lines, not ten.
  5. Close the stage with the sentence that frames the next forty minutes: Every part you have just watched, you are about to do. Three times, in three roles.

Speaking Parts 1 and 2 · in threes
groups of three
Why: Speaking Parts 1 and 2 · in threes

WHAT WE DO: the personal questions and the photograph, with rotating roles

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Part 1 first, and they prepared it two lessons ago — the four questions with one extra fact attached to each answer. Threes, roles named: examiner · Candidate A · Candidate B.
  2. Kagan Three-Step Interview, six minutes: the examiner asks, the candidates answer, roles rotate twice so everybody examines once.
  3. The examiner's job is not passive: read the questions exactly, and use a back-up prompt the moment an answer arrives without its extra fact.
  4. Then Part 2, six minutes: each candidate talks alone about one photograph from the Speaking bank for about a minute while the other two listen. Rotate so everybody describes once.
  5. The key language box for describing a photo is on the page and should be open — In the foreground… · It looks as if… · I think they might be…
  6. Two minutes, floor: what is harder, talking for a minute alone or answering questions? The answers split the room and both sides are right, which is a useful thing for them to hear before the next stage.
📺 On screen: the Speaking bank pages, open at the key language boxes. Backup: the model audio files 01–06 are in the same folder if a group needs to hear a model first — the rehearsal runs unchanged without them
👦 Students do: answer four personal questions with an extra fact, describe a photograph alone for a minute, and examine once

groups of three → class
Why: 🎯 Speaking Parts 3 and 4 · the full paired exam

WHAT WE DO: the discussion task and the follow-up questions, joined up

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 🎯 Part 3, the Speaking bank discussion task, run exactly as in lesson 61: the examiner reads the situation, the two candidates talk together for about two minutes, discuss ALL the options and then reach an agreement.
  2. The ninety-second rule, called out loud by you so nobody watches their own clock: no decision before ninety seconds.
  3. Three rotations, nine minutes, so everybody discusses twice and examines once. The language of suggesting and agreeing is in the key language box on the page and the examiner ticks the phrases used.
  4. Then Part 4 joined straight on, six minutes: the general questions on the same topic, answered in two parts — the answer, then a reason or an example. The examiner uses the back-up prompts.
  5. Three minutes of feedback and it comes from the examiners: each one reports ONE phrase their pair used well, word for word. Collect them on the board.
  6. Say the count out loud, because it is genuinely the point: You have now sat the paired speaking test four times this term and been the examiner four times. Whatever happens in June, it will not be the first time.
📺 On screen: the collected phrases on the board and the key language boxes open on the page — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: discuss twice, examine once, answer the follow-up questions in two parts, and give evidence-based feedback
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains the EGE oral section (tasks 1–4 of the speaking paper). Answer, then reason, then example — the two-part answer rehearsed here is the difference between a two-mark and a four-mark response on the Russian speaking card.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: after the Part 4 rotation, give each three one Russian-format monologue plan: ninety seconds of silent preparation, then two minutes of delivery to the other two. Take the time from the reserve stage.

WHAT WE DO: six minutes of buffer, and the written instruction for a lost lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. If everything has run to time, use these six minutes on the thing the class asks for. It is the last free time of the year and it belongs to them — usually it becomes questions about the final test, and answering them plainly is worth more than another activity.
  2. Say exactly what is on Monday's paper, without hedging: six blocks, the same shape as the mock, the whole year instead of the last six units, and a letter about cooking instead of a film review. A named paper is not a scary one.
  3. If a lesson has been lost earlier in this block and this one is the replacement, the instruction is in the plan rather than in your head: The syllabus calls lesson 71 a reserve. If nothing has been lost to a cancelled day, it runs as written — the year reviewed out of the group's own error slips, plus the full spoken rehearsal. If a lesson HAS been lost, the two stages that go first are the year quiz and the carousel, and the replacement is one TOR_B1 plus worksheet, which is a self-contained forty-minute lesson with its own key.
  4. Second reserve option, entirely self-contained: SB p.116–117, the Life Skills spread on managing stress, is the one page of the Student's Book the syllabus never allocates. It is a legitimate reserve for this lesson and it is more appropriate in the last week of May than it would be at any other point in the year: five tips on handling exam stress, a listening with four teenagers and their problems, and a project. It has a key in the Teacher's Book like everything else.
  5. Whatever happens, protect the last four minutes. The wrap-up of this lesson is the last ordinary lesson-ending of the year and it should not be squeezed.

Wrap-up + homework
desks → door
Why: Self-assessment against the aim they heard at the start, so the lesson closes the loop it opened — and in these four lessons the loop is the year, not the hour.

WHAT WE DO: self-check against today's aim, then the homework said once and written on the board

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Back to the aim: Thumbs up if at least two of your three lines have a tick against them now. Thumbs up if you got your partner to say something they would not have said alone.
  2. Exit at the door: each student says one sentence using the language point from their FIRST log line. It is the neatest possible check that the stations worked.
  3. Homework said once and written up: thirty minutes of your own revision, chosen by you. Take the two sections your log names, read the Grammar reference page for each and do the practice items under it. Bring the notebook — I will look at what you chose, not at how many you got right.
  4. Say what is next, plainly and without drama: Next lesson is the final paper. Same shape as the mock, the whole year instead of six units. Bring a pen that works and get some sleep.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
