🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Fix the four things the group's own error logs name at four stations taught by the students themselves, then sit the complete official Speaking test — Parts 1 to 4 — in threes, with everybody taking the interlocutor's chair.
What is genuinely hard
A revision lesson in the last week of May fails in one of two ways: it is a list of everything, which nobody can hold, or it is a game with no content, which nobody remembers. The cure is that the content is theirs — yesterday's three-line logs decide what gets revised. The speaking half has its own difficulty: after two written lessons the room is quiet, and quiet has to be broken deliberately rather than hoped away.
Typical error to expect
Revising what you already know because it is comfortable. The logs exist to prevent exactly that, which is why the stations are built from them and not from a syllabus.
Treating the interlocutor's chair as a rest. It is the hardest and most useful of the three roles: reading the script exactly as printed, timing the minute, and using the back-up prompt at the moment an answer dies rather than after it.
Why the lesson is built this way
The first half is built overnight out of the error cards — four stations, four themes, chosen by frequency, twenty minutes of preparation, and that is the whole difference between a revision lesson and a filler. The second half is the part of the examination that could not fit into lesson 69 and cannot be practised alone at home: the official Speaking paper, run from the interlocutor's own printed script, in threes with rotating roles. Everybody speaks for roughly the same number of minutes; nobody watches. This lesson is also the syllabus's reserve slot, so the plan says explicitly what to cut and what to substitute if a lesson has been lost.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“The first forty minutes were written by you — I read your cards last night and the four things on the board are the four that came up most. Then the part of the exam we could not fit into Monday: the whole speaking test, from the examiner's own script, and every one of you sits in the examiner's chair.”

WHAT WE DO: the rewritten emails collected and two written lessons shaken off

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — collect the second drafts with the first version stapled behind. Look at each for five seconds: is the first version there? That is what you are checking, not the English.
  2. 2 minutes — Kagan Mix-Pair-Share, standing, and it is here for a reason: after two written lessons this room is quiet, and the second half of today needs it loud. Mix, pair, and tell somebody one thing you are actually going to do in the first week of the holidays. Two partners.
  3. Bridge (30 seconds): I read all your cards last night. The four things on the board are yours, not mine. And in forty minutes you are sitting the speaking test.

WHAT WE DO: name the aim and reveal the four station themes

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Write the four station themes on the board and say where they came from: Twenty-one cards. Sixty-three lines. These four came up most.
  2. Say the shape in thirty seconds: twenty-four minutes on those four, then the whole speaking test in threes with everybody taking the examiner's chair.
  3. Set the rule that makes the stations work: At each station, somebody who got it RIGHT explains it. I am not explaining anything for the next twenty-four minutes.
  4. Say the choice out loud, because it matters more than the rotation: anybody whose own three lines are all at one station may stay there for two rounds and skip another. It is their revision, not yours.

Why: Stations · rounds one and two

WHAT WE DO: twelve minutes, two stations each, taught by students

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Four tables, one theme each, with the material prepared last night: a short explanation card, six practice items and an answer slip face down.
  2. Groups of four to five. Six minutes per station. At each one the student who got that theme right on the mock is the EXPLAINER for the first minute; then everybody does the six items; then the answer slip is turned over.
  3. Your job for these twelve minutes is to walk and say nothing except Ask the person who got it right. Hold the line — the moment you explain something, four stations become one lecture.
  4. Rotate on a signal, not on completion. Two stations in this block.
  5. At the end of each station everybody ticks the line in their own log if it now feels fixed. A tick is not a claim to mastery; it is a note to themselves in August.
📺 On screen: the four station tables and the rotation signal — chalk, paper and prepared cards only, and nothing can fail
👦 Students do: explain one point to four people, practise six items at two stations and tick their own log

WHAT WE DO: twelve more minutes, the other two stations

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Same structure, the remaining two stations, six minutes each.
  2. Change one thing for these two rounds and announce it: the LAST two minutes at each station are a two-item test written by this group for the next group. Writing an item is harder than doing one and it is the best diagnostic there is.
  3. Collect the student-written items as you go and keep the good ones — they are next year's warmers.
  4. Two minutes at the end, whole class, Kagan Numbered Heads Together: one named student per station says the rule of that station in one sentence. Four sentences, and they go on the board.
  5. Those four sentences are the year's revision, on one board, written by the class. Photograph it and send it to them — it is the most useful thing they will get this week.

👦 Students do: complete the rotation, write two test items for the next group and state one rule for the whole room

Move break · desks into threes
standing, whole class
Why: A stirrer that also recycles language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies — and in an examination week that is truer, not less true.

WHAT WE DO: four minutes that turn a written lesson into a spoken one

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Everybody stands and the desks move into threes, facing each other. Do it now, not while the scripts are being handed out.
  2. Sixty seconds of pronunciation, the only drill of the lesson: What do YOU think? Say it flat, then say it as a real invitation. The room repeats it four times until the difference is audible.
  3. Then the same with Could you repeat that, please? — polite, not apologetic.
  4. Two minutes of Kagan RallyRobin in the new threes: name one thing you would do on the last day of school before the summer holidays, and one you would refuse. That is the topic of Part 3 in fifteen minutes, so nothing here is wasted.
  5. Sit down in the threes: Nobody writes anything for the rest of this lesson.
📺 On screen: no technology in this stage — voices and furniture only, and nothing can fail
👦 Students do: stand, move the desks, drill two phrases in chorus and speak to two partners

WHAT WE DO: eight minutes so that a fifteen-year-old can run the exam accurately at the first attempt

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. One printed set per three. Show what is in it: the script pages, the two photographs on page 6, the Part 3 options on page 8.
  2. Say the shape and the clock, and write it on the board: Part 1 2–3 minutes, personal questions · Part 2 2–3 minutes, one photograph each, about a minute alone · Parts 3 and 4 6 minutes, discuss the options and agree, then follow-up questions. Twelve to seventeen minutes for a pair, and everybody does all of it.
  3. Teach the interlocutor's job in four sentences, because it is the role that makes or breaks the rehearsal: read the words as printed · give the photograph to the right candidate · time the minute · use the printed back-up prompt the moment an answer stops, not thirty seconds later.

Name the four things the examiner marks, once, and then leave them alone: The Cambridge speaking scale has four criteria and it is worth naming them once, because they explain why a long silence costs more than a wrong tense: Grammar and Vocabulary · Discourse Management · Pronunciation · Interactive Communication.

  6. Close the stage with the sentence that frames the next thirty-four minutes: Every part you have just seen, you are about to do. Three times, in three roles.

WHAT WE DO: the personal questions and the photograph, with rotating roles

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Roles named out loud: interlocutor · Candidate A · Candidate B. Everybody will hold each role once before the lesson ends.
  2. Part 1, seven minutes, Kagan Three-Step Interview: the interlocutor works through Phase 1 (name, age, where you live, who you live with) and then two or three questions from the printed list for each candidate. Rotate twice so everybody examines once.
  3. The one instruction for candidates, and it is the whole of Part 1: every answer has two parts — the answer, then a reason or an example. The interlocutor uses the printed back-up prompt the moment a bare answer arrives.
  4. Part 2, six minutes: photograph 1A to Candidate A (a family spending time together), 1B to Candidate B (people studying together in class). About a minute each, alone, while the other two listen without interrupting.
  5. The key language for a photograph goes on the board and stays there: In the foreground… · It looks as if… · I think they might be… The printed back-up prompts do the rest — talk about the people, the place, the other things.
  6. One minute, floor: what is harder, a minute alone or answering questions? The answers split the room and both sides are right, which is useful to hear before Part 3.
📺 On screen: the photograph language on the board and the script open in every group — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: answer personal questions in two parts, speak alone for a minute about a photograph and run the examiner's script once

WHAT WE DO: the discussion task and the follow-up questions, joined up, three times

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 🎯 Part 3, from the paper: a teacher wants to organise a fun activity for the class on the last day of school before the summer holidays. The interlocutor reads the situation, places the options page in front of the pair and says Now, talk together.
  2. The ninety-second rule, called by you so that nobody watches their own clock: no decision before ninety seconds. Talk about ALL the options first, then agree.
  3. Part 4 joined straight on, without a break: the five printed follow-up questions on days out and holidays, with the prompts — How about you? · Do you agree? · What do you think?
  4. Three rotations of five minutes, so everybody discusses twice and examines once. While a three works, you sit with one group at a time and write down two phrases you actually hear.
  5. Three minutes of feedback, and it comes from the interlocutors, not from you: each one reports ONE phrase their pair used well, word for word. Collect them on the board — it is the best phrase bank of the term because they said it themselves.
  6. Say the count out loud, because it is genuinely the point: You have now sat the whole speaking test and been the examiner as well. Whatever happens next year, it will not be the first time.
📺 On screen: the collected phrases written up as the groups report them, and the options page open in every three — chalk and paper only, nothing to fail
👦 Students do: discuss twice, examine once, answer the follow-up questions in two parts and report one phrase their pair used well
🇷🇺 EGE parallel — optional 5–7 minutes, teacher's choice: this stage already trains the EGE oral section. Answer, then reason, then example — the two-part answer rehearsed in Part 4 is the difference between a two-mark and a four-mark response on the Russian speaking card, and the Cambridge back-up prompts are exactly the questions a Russian examiner will not ask you.
How to run it without losing the 90 minutes: after the third rotation, give each three one Russian-format monologue plan: ninety seconds of silent preparation, then two minutes of delivery to the other two. Take the time from the reserve stage.

Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

The station half of this lesson is built from the error cards, so its EGE content is whatever the group actually got wrong. The fixed element is the speaking test: the official Cambridge interlocutor script, run in threes, which trains the two habits the Russian oral rewards most — answering in two parts (answer, then reason or example) and keeping going when the first idea runs out.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
