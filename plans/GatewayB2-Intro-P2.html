🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Retrieve and re-activate the five B1 lexical fields the whole Gateway B2 year is built on — study, work, travel, technology and health — as collocations rather than as single words: not exam but take, pass and fail an exam; not job but apply for a part-time job; not phone but charge it, plug it in, log in. Then measure what is actually there with the publisher's diagnostic.
What is genuinely hard
After ten weeks of no English the problem is not that words are gone — they are passive. A student recognises apply instantly and cannot produce it under time pressure. The second difficulty is that B1 vocabulary is stored as translation pairs, so the collocation is missing: the student knows «сдать экзамен» and produces give an exam or hand over an exam.
Typical error to expect
«make homework», «do a mistake» — you do homework, exercises, the washing-up, and you make mistakes, decisions, progress, friends. Pure collocation, no rule, and it costs marks in tasks 32–36 every year.
«many informations», «much people», «advices» — information, advice, news, furniture, money and progress are uncountable in English and plural or countable in Russian. The error survives all the way to C1 if it is not named early.
Why the lesson is built this way
Recognition before production, movement before sitting, and the test last. The five fields are met at stations — five tables, five minutes each in rotation — because a rotation forces retrieval five times in twenty minutes with no dead time. Then Quiz-Quiz-Trade converts recognition into production. Only then comes the diagnostic paper, and it comes with its rules said out loud, so that it reads as a map and not as a verdict.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
Here is the thing nobody tells you about vocabulary: you have not forgotten it. It is all still there, it has just gone quiet. Today we make it loud again — five topics, five tables, five minutes each. And at the end there is a short paper from the publishers so that both of us can see exactly what is solid and what needs work. It is not marked, it is not shown to anybody, and I need it more than you do.

Lead-in · the brainstorm race
pairs, against the clock
Why: Competitive retrieval wakes a September class faster than anything else, and the cross-out makes the lesson's central point (collocation, not translation) land as a discovery rather than as an announcement.

WHAT WE DO: eight minutes of pure retrieval, competitive, before any input at all

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Pairs, one sheet, five columns headed with the five fields. Four minutes. As many English words as you can under each heading. Spelling counts. Go.
  2. Stop them dead at four minutes. Count your total. Hands up if you have more than thirty. Forty? Fifty?
  3. Now the twist that makes it a language stage rather than a memory game: Cross out every word that is only a noun on its own. Count again. The numbers collapse, and that is the teaching point of the morning: You have the words. What you are missing is what goes with them.
  4. Take three examples from the room and turn them into collocations on the board in front of the class: exam → take an exam, pass an exam, revise for an exam.
📺 On screen: the five columns modelled on the board, then the three examples. Backup: board-and-paper only; nothing to fail.
👦 Students do: brainstorm against the clock in pairs, count, cross out, count again.
paper · board
13–33′
Station carousel · five fields, five tables
five stations, rotating
Why: Five short exposures beat one long one for retrieval, the rotation removes dead time completely, and the poster means each group's output becomes the next group's input.

WHAT WE DO: twenty minutes, four minutes per station, everybody moving and everybody producing

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set five tables before the lesson. Each has a card with the field's collocation list and one task. The lists are: Study — subject · timetable · revise for a test · take / pass / fail an exam · get good marks · hand in an essay · homework (uncountable) · make progress · do a course · a degree. Work — apply for a job · a part-time job · earn money · a salary · staff · get on with your colleagues · be in charge of · an interview · experience (uncountable) · a career.
  2. Travel — book a flight · check in · a return ticket · get on / off · miss a train · a journey vs a trip vs travel · accommodation · abroad · pack · go sightseeing. Technology — download an app · charge your phone · a screen · plug in · log in to an account · a password · post a photo · scroll · a device · switch it off and on again. Health — keep fit · put on weight · have a sore throat · take medicine · an appointment · feel dizzy · get over an illness · a balanced diet · exhausted · give up sugar.
  3. The task at every station is the same three steps, printed on the card so you never have to explain it twice: (1) read the ten items and tick the ones you could have produced yourself; (2) in your pair, use four of them in four true sentences about your own life, out loud; (3) write ONE of those sentences on the station's poster, so the next group reads it.
  4. Four minutes per station, then a clap and rotate clockwise. Be strict with the clock — the pressure is what makes it work.
  5. Circulate with a notebook. You are collecting errors for the feedback stage, not correcting on the spot.
  6. After the fifth rotation, thirty seconds: Look at the poster at your table. Read the best sentence on it out loud.
📺 On screen: nothing on screen; five printed station cards and five posters. Backup: if the printer failed, write the five lists on five sheets of A4 by hand before the lesson — the stage depends on paper, not on technology.
👦 Students do: rotate through five tables in pairs, tick what they knew, say four true sentences at each, and leave one written sentence behind.

33–43′
Quiz-Quiz-Trade · production under pressure
open floor
Why: Quiz-Quiz-Trade gives every student ten to fourteen retrievals in six minutes with 100% simultaneous participation — no queue, no spectators — and the coaching step means the weakest student is helped by a peer six times rather than by the teacher once.

WHAT WE DO: recognition converted into production, with every student holding a card and moving

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Hand each student two cards from the fifty collocations. On the front: a definition or a gapped sentence (to ______ for an exam = to study for it before it happens). On the back: the answer.
  2. Quiz-Quiz-Trade: find a partner, quiz them with your card, be quizzed with theirs, coach if they are stuck, praise, trade cards, find a new partner. Six minutes, non-stop.
  3. Model one exchange with a strong student first, including the coaching move: Not quite — it begins with R. … Yes! Revise for an exam.
  4. Stop and sit them down for a fast whole-class round: read out eight of the gapped sentences and take choral answers. Anything the class hesitates on goes on the board.
  5. Those five on the board are the ones that are still asleep. They are on tonight's list.
📺 On screen: the eight hesitation items written on the board at the end. Backup: no technology at any point; the cards are the only requirement, and a handwritten set works exactly as well as a printed one.
👦 Students do: quiz and be quizzed by six or seven different partners, coach when their partner is stuck, then answer chorally.
50 quiz cards · board
43–68′
Diagnostic · vocabulary and reading, from the publisher's paper
desks, silent
Why: A diagnostic is only useful if it is done under real conditions, and it is only bearable if it is short and explained. Two sections, twenty-five minutes, and the class knows exactly why.

WHAT WE DO: twenty-five silent minutes on two sections of the official B2 Diagnostic Test

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Say the three rules once, in these words, before every diagnostic section: One — this does not go in anybody's register. Two — if you do not know, leave it and move on; a blank costs the same as a wrong answer and less time. Three — when the timer stops, pens down, and we talk about it straight away.
  2. Hand out the paper. Today the class does exercises 4, 5 and 6 (Vocabulary, 15 marks) and exercises 8 and 9 (Reading, 10 marks). Nothing else — say so, and have them fold the sheet.
  3. Vocabulary, 12 minutes. Exercise 4: five words re-formed from a stem (employ, renew, fascinate, psychology, embarrass). Exercise 5: five three-option items. Exercise 6: a text about names — five of eight words fit, three are extra.
  4. Reading, 13 minutes. The article on 'textese'. Exercise 8: six removed sentences A–F, one extra. Exercise 9: five True / False / Not stated statements.
  5. Give the one strategy line they need before the reading, and only one: For sentence-insertion, read the sentence BEFORE the gap and the sentence AFTER it. The answer almost always links backwards.
  6. Timer visible. Circulate but do not help; note who finishes early and who stalls, and where.
📺 On screen: a visible timer — a phone stopwatch on the desk is enough. Backup: if there is no timer, write the finishing time on the board and read out the halfway point aloud.
👦 Students do: work in silence on two sections of a real paper, leaving blanks rather than stopping.

68–80′
Feedback + word maps
pairs → whole class
Why: Immediate self-marking turns a test into feedback, and turning the weakest field into a map means the student leaves with something to revise from rather than with a score.

WHAT WE DO: the paper marked together immediately, then turned into a revision artefact

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Mark it in the room, now, together — do not take it home. Read the answers; students mark their own paper in a different colour. It takes four minutes and it is worth more than a week of waiting.
  2. For the three items the class got most wrong, do not give the answer — build it: Why is it «renewable» and not «renewed»? What kind of word does the gap need — a thing, a person or a describing word?
  3. Then word maps, eight minutes, in pairs: take the ONE field from the board where your pair lost most marks and draw it — the field in the middle, the verbs that go with it on one branch, the adjectives on another, the collocations on a third, and three sentences of your own around the outside.
  4. Two pairs show their map to two other pairs and steal anything they missed. The maps go in the back of their notebooks and come out again in December.
📺 On screen: the answer key read aloud, and the three problem items built on the board. Backup: the printed answer key (B2_Diagnostic_Test_Teacher_s_Guide_answer_key.pdf) is the only thing you need in your hand.
👦 Students do: mark their own paper in a second colour, then build a word map of their own weakest field and swap it with another pair.

EGE parallel · why collocation is worth five marks
whole class
Why: The exam link is made once, briefly, and only where it is genuinely the same skill — which here it is. It also retroactively justifies the whole collocation design of the lesson.

WHAT WE DO: a five-minute honest aside for the students who will sit the Russian exam

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Only for those who are sitting it — say so first: This next bit is for the people taking the EGE in May. Everyone else, listen if you're curious.
  2. Write one real item on the board in the exam's own shape: She knew the company needed her, and the results depended ______ her work. (1) for (2) on (3) from (4) of)
  3. All four are real English words. Only one goes with «depend». That is tasks 32 to 36 of the Russian paper — five marks, every year, and they are not testing whether you know the word.
  4. Elicit three more from today's stations that would work the same way: apply FOR a job · get ON WITH your colleagues · suffer FROM an illness.
  5. Close it in one sentence and move on: That is why we learned them in pairs today and not in a list.
📺 On screen: the four-option item written on the board. Backup: dictate it; five minutes, no equipment.
👦 Students do: choose the preposition, then supply three more collocations from the stations.

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will have retrieved and used, in true sentences about themselves, thirty to fifty B1 collocations across five lexical fields, and will know from a real paper which of the five is their weakest.
  • Subsidiary. Reading a short article for sentence-level cohesion (insertion) and for True / False / Not stated; self-marking against a key; building a word map as a revision artefact.
  • Personal. Do not correct anything during the carousel. Write it down, wait thirty-five minutes, and deal with all of it in one place.
By the end, students can…
  • produce, not just recognise, ten collocations in each of five fields
  • say why «do homework» and «make progress» are not interchangeable
  • insert a removed sentence into a text by reading backwards from the gap
  • distinguish False from Not stated in a reading task
  • name their own weakest lexical field and show a map of it

Structure
I'm revising for three exams at the moment. · He applied for a part-time job and got it. · It depends on the weather. · I've never been very good at keeping fit.
Form
collocation is the whole lesson: verb + noun (take an exam, make progress, book a flight), verb + preposition (apply for, depend on, suffer from, get over), adjective + preposition (good at, keen on, afraid of). Also the uncountables that Russian makes plural: homework, information, advice, news, money, progress — no -s, no a, and much not many.
Pron. watch
the collocation is one rhythmic unit, not two words: ˈtake an exˈam, apˈply for a ˈjob — the preposition is unstressed and reduced to a schwa, which is exactly why students never hear it and therefore never produce it. Drill the whole chunk at speed, then slowly, then at speed again. Also watch two words that are mis-stressed all year: reˈvise (not ˈrevise) and ˈcolleague /ˈkɒliːɡ/.
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
revise for an exam: Is the exam finished? — No, it's coming. Am I studying something new or something I met before? — Something I met before.
apply for: Have I got the job? — Not yet. What did I send? — A letter or a form.
get over an illness: Am I ill now? — No, not any more. Was I ill? — Yes.
ICQ before the diagnostic: How many exercises? Which numbers? What do you do if you don't know — sit and think, or leave it and move on?

Assumptions: Students finished a B1+ course in May and have used little or no English since. They will recognise most of the fifty collocations and produce perhaps half. They have met True / False / Not stated before but will still treat 'Not stated' as 'False'.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
The stations turn into a copying exercise.Step 2 on every card is spoken, not written, and step 3 allows exactly one sentence on the poster. If a table is silently copying, stand next to it and ask two students to say their sentence to you instead.
Quiz-Quiz-Trade collapses because nobody moves.Model it once with a strong student and then stand in the middle of the floor rather than at the board. If the room is small, run it in two concentric circles that rotate in opposite directions on a clap.
Students treat the diagnostic as a real exam and panic.The three rules are said out loud before the paper is turned over, every time, and the paper is marked in the room in the same lesson. Panic survives waiting; it does not survive immediate feedback.
'Not stated' is answered as 'False' throughout exercise 9.Predict it. When you mark, take one statement and ask: Show me the line in the text that proves it is wrong. You can't? Then it is not False — it is Not stated. One worked example fixes most of the group.
Fast finishers have eight spare minutes in the middle of the diagnostic.On the board before you start: Finished? Turn the sheet over and write five sentences using five collocations from the stations — one from each field. No talking, and it feeds the word-map stage.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
Where this goes next
🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Tasks 32–36 (lexical choice) and tasks 10–18 (reading) — the two places where a thin B1 vocabulary stops being invisible.

The Russian paper does not test whether a candidate knows a word; it tests whether they know which of four real words collocates. That is a B2 skill built on a B1 base, and the base is what today measures. The diagnostic sections used today are the vocabulary block (word formation, three-option choice, a gapped text with three extra words) and the reading block — precisely the two halves of the Russian written paper that a weak lexical base ruins first.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
