🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Name ten animals and hear the meaning, not the familiar word: match five speakers to a list of options over two listenings. Plus a three-paragraph plan for an article about an animal.
What is genuinely hard
The matching trap: several words from the option list are said in the recording and only one of them fits. And students don't use the second listening — they start guessing again from scratch instead of checking what they already have.
Typical error to expect
A word from the option was said in the recording → they tick it. A matching word is not a correct answer; meaning decides [exam report]
The speaker only brushed past a topic → they choose that heading. You are looking for the speaker's main idea, not a mention [exam report]
Why the lesson is built this way
The strategy is named before the listening, not after it, because a strategy explained during feedback is a strategy nobody used. The panda text and the vocabulary matching come first so that by the time the recording is played the ten animal words are already warm — the listening then tests listening, not vocabulary. The 90-second move break sits immediately before the listening on purpose: silent solo work only works if the class has just been allowed to be loud. The productive stage at 40′ is a listening outcome, so it comes in the middle of the lesson while attention is at its best, and the writing after it is deliberately quiet work that lets the class come down. Nothing is corrected between the two listenings — the pause is the whole point of the strategy.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
“Five questions, eight answers — three of them are extra. You hear the recording twice. Rule one: the first time you LISTEN, the second time you CHECK. Rule two: a word you hear is not an answer — the meaning is.”

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, so the first word out of their mouth is English. The rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — new input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting, a moving review of geography and stative verbs, and the bridge into the lesson

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One word — how are you today? … Good. Sit down and open your book. Any answer is accepted as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — last lesson rewind. Kagan Stand Up–Hand Up–Pair Up: stand up, find a partner — Say one geographical feature and one verb that never takes -ing. Swap partners. Go! Three partners in 3 minutes.
  3. Sit them down with a clap: One clap! — the class answers with two and sits down.
  4. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): The planet has people, mountains — and animals. Today: pandas — and a listening task with a trap in it.
📺 On screen: instruction by voice; the nine geography words are still up on the board from last lesson as a safety net — the board is the medium here, so no backup is needed, and if the board has been wiped the words are in the book on p.14
👦 Students do: walk around swapping words. Expect: the geography comes back easily, the stative verbs less so — that is normal after one lesson

Lesson frame
board
Why: Students hear the one aim and the standard their answers must reach. Naming the bar now means you can hold them to it later without it feeling like a punishment.

WHAT WE DO: announce the mechanics of the listening task and the strategy checklist

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. One line on the board: Five questions · eight answers · you hear it TWICE.
  2. The mechanics in words: Students listen to a dialogue and match five items to a list of options. The recording is played TWICE.
  3. The trap: Five questions, eight options — two are always extra. Several words from the list may come up in the recording and only one of them is right: listen for meaning, not for a matching word (Exam tip, SB p.17).
  4. The strategy checklist goes on the board, three lines: 1 Read A–H before you listen. 2 First time — listen. Second time — check. 3 No letter twice.
  5. Set the bar: By the end you can say WHY you chose each answer — not just which one.
📺 On screen: a slide with the three-line checklist (Backup: the same three lines in chalk on the board — they stay up until the end of the lesson and are needed again at 40–56′)
👦 Students do: listen; one student says the checklist back in their own words. Expect surprise that there are more options than questions. Typical problem — they assume there are no extra options at all

8–18′
Reading · giant panda
pairs → solo
Why: A model text for their own article later: they read it for facts now, and reuse its shape when they write.

WHAT WE DO: the article on p.16: three facts before reading, then seven right/wrong statements with a correction

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books closed: Which animal is black and white and lives in China? In pairs they write three facts about pandas (technique from TB p.29).
  2. Pre-teach three words: bamboo — a tall grass pandas eat, enclosure — an area with fences, the wild — not a zoo.
  3. Books open, 3 minutes — check your own facts against the text.
  4. ex.2: seven statements, and the wrong ones have to be corrected. Kagan RallyCoach to check.
  5. Key (TB p.29): 1 ✓ · 2 ✗ — Pandas are only found in the mountains of central China. · 3 ✗ — They sometimes eat other plants or small animals. · 4 ✓ · 5 ✗ — They start eating bamboo when they are about four months old. · 6 ✗ — They think there are about 1,500 to 2,000. · 7 ✓
📺 On screen: the text on the screen so evidence lines can be marked for everybody (Backup: read from the book on p.16 and keep two columns ✓ / ✗ with the numbers on the board)
👦 Students do: write predictions, read, then repair the wrong statements. Expect item 5 (the age they start eating bamboo) to be missed most often. Typical error: they mark ✗ but write no correct version — without the correction it does not count

18–30′
Vocabulary · animals
class → pairs
Why: Ten words in one matching task, then straight into a survey — the words are used within two minutes of being met.

WHAT WE DO: ten animals: ex.1 p.16 (photos A–J, track 14) + the ex.2 questions + Quiz-Quiz-Trade

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Before the matching: Which has a long neck? Which can fly? Which can climb very well? (technique from TB p.29).
  2. ex.1 — match the words to photos A–J. Track 14 — check and choral repetition. Key: A monkey · B elephant · C snake · D polar bear · E whale · F giraffe · G tiger · H parrot · I penguin · J dolphin
  3. Cover the words with your hand and name them from the photos — a second pass with no support.
  4. Drill the stress separately: giraffe /dʒəˈrɑːf/ — second syllable, not the first.
  5. Kagan Quiz-Quiz-Trade with photo ↔ word cards, 4 minutes.
📺 On screen: the animal photos A–J on the screen (Backup: the same photos in the book on p.16 with the word list written on the board; the cards are printed in advance)
👦 Students do: match, repeat, then trade cards. Expect dolphin and whale to be confused from the photos. Typical written error: giraf, penguine — put the correct spelling on the board straight away

30–38′
Animal survey
class
Why: Whole-class movement with a fixed question frame: maximum repetitions of the target structure per minute, and everyone speaks.

WHAT WE DO: the Talking points on p.16 run as a class survey: favourite animals and zoos

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. The question of the day on the board: What are your five favourite animals? Why? How often do you go to zoos? Do you like them?
  2. Survey format (technique from TB p.29): each student interviews as many classmates as possible in 4 minutes and keeps the score.
  3. Collect it with Kagan Numbered Heads Together: The most popular animal in this class — number twos, report!
  4. Extension: build a pictogram on the board — one animal symbol per vote.
📺 On screen: four questions on the board and the pictogram drawn there and then — the board is the medium, no backup needed
👦 Students do: interview each other and count. Expect an argument about zoos — a good way into discussion. Typical error: I like dolphin with no -s — fix it as they report

🎯 Listening out · matching
solo, silent mode
Why: The productive listening outcome: they handle extra options and check on the second play. The strategy, not the answers, is the point of the stage.

WHAT WE DO: the matching task: five animal photos → eight countries, track 15, two listenings

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set it up (TB p.30): TB p.30, before you play it — Which two people are speaking? (Gina and her uncle) · Who took the photos? (her uncle) · What are the photos of? (animals) · Did Gina's uncle visit Europe? (yes – England and Scotland).
  2. Give them 1 minute to read the lists — straight off the checklist on the board: Read A to H before you listen.
  3. Silent mode — palm down: no talking, everyone works alone. First listening.
  4. A 30-second pause with no discussion. Second listening — checking only.
  5. Check against: 1 C · 2 B · 3 H · 4 G · 5 E (example 0 = D)
  6. Error analysis: Where marks go: students tick the option on the first word they recognise and never use the second listening to check. Ask about every wrong answer: Which word made you choose that one? — that is what makes the trap visible.
  7. Kagan Think-Pair-Share to close: one sentence on what you will do differently next time.
📺 On screen: the audio of track 15 (Backup if the board is down: take the audioscript from TB p.286 and read the dialogue yourself in two voices, exactly twice, at an even pace, with no special stress on the answers; the photo list and the country list stay open in the book on p.17)
👦 Students do: listen twice in silence and match. Expect 3–4 out of 5 on the first play. Typical error: they choose the country on the first name they recognise rather than on what the speaker means

Info-gap · kakapo vs sand cat
pairs
Why: A genuine information gap: neither partner can finish without asking. It rehearses the question forms they need for the written article.

WHAT WE DO: ex.3 p.17: each partner has their own animal and their own text — build the questions and get the information

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Set the pairs: Student A goes to p.136 (the kakapo), Student B to p.138 (the sand cat).
  2. Build the questions from the nine prompts first, don't let them just start asking: What kind of animal is it? · Where is it from? · How much does it weigh? · How many are left in the wild?
  3. Kagan RallyCoach: the partner checks the form of the question BEFORE answering it — a broken question is asked again.
  4. Exchange the information and write the answers in their notebooks.
  5. Key for checking (TB p.30): SB p.17 ex.3: Student A reads about the kakapo (SB p.136), Student B about the sand cat (SB p.138); they build questions from prompts 1–9. Key, TB p.30: the kakapo is a parrot from New Zealand, lives on two islands, eats plants, fruit and nuts, weighs 2–4 kg, about 127 are left in the wild, the babies are chicks, 2–3 every two years, they stay with the mother for 10 weeks. The sand cat comes from Africa and Asia, lives in deserts, eats insects, birds and small animals, weighs 2–3 kg, nobody knows how many are left in the wild, the babies are kittens, 1–8 a year, they stay with the mother for about six months.
  6. Students who need support (Mixed ability, TB p.30): work through all nine questions chorally before the pair work starts.
📺 On screen: the nine prompts on the screen (Backup: the prompts are open in the book on p.17 and the kakapo and sand cat texts are read straight from the book on p.136 and p.138; the four model questions go on the board)
👦 Students do: build questions and mine each other for facts. Expect How much and How many to be mixed up. Typical error: How much it weighs? — the partner has to correct it, that is their job
SB p.17 ex.3 · SB p.136 and p.138 · notebooks
68–84′
Writing · article about an animal
solo → pairs
Why: Writing built on a model and a paragraph plan, started in class so nobody takes an empty page home.

WHAT WE DO: Prepare to write p.17: the Get ready prepositions, the three-paragraph plan, a draft of paragraph one

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Get ready: underline between, about, around and including in the panda article. Which two mean the same? (about and around).
  2. Five sentences to complete. Key (TB p.30): 1 between · 2 about/around · 3 including · 4 between · 5 including
  3. PLAN: choose your animal — from ex.1 or any other. The plan goes on the board: Paragraph 1 — what the animal is, where it lives, what it eats; Paragraph 2 — how much it weighs, what we know about its babies; Paragraph 3 — how many are left in the wild and how many live in zoos.
  4. WRITE: write paragraph one in class, 8 minutes. The other two are homework.
  5. IMPROVE: Kagan RallyCoach — swap drafts, and the partner looks for exactly two things: is there a preposition from Get ready, and is there any is living / is weighing.
  6. The bar: Three paragraphs, sixty to eighty words, at least two prepositions from Get ready.
📺 On screen: the three-paragraph plan on the screen (Backup: the plan written out on the board in three lines, where it stays until the end of the lesson; the panda article is open in the book on p.16 as the model)
👦 Students do: complete the prepositions, choose an animal, write paragraph one and correct each other. Expect half the class to take a cat or a dog — allow it, the criterion is not how exotic the animal is. Typical error: My animal is living in Africa — the partner has to catch it

📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end, students can complete a matching listening task using the two-listening strategy, and name ten animals with the right pronunciation.
  • Subsidiary. Reading the panda article for detail (SB p.16 ex.2, seven statements); building questions about animals from prompts (the kakapo / sand cat info-gap); a three-paragraph article plan and the prepositions between / about / around / including.
  • Personal. During the listening say nothing and give nothing away between the two plays. Errors are analysed after the second play — otherwise what gets trained is guessing, not strategy.
By the end, students can…
  • name ten animals and say where they live
  • decide if a statement about a text is right or wrong and correct it
  • do a matching listening task using both listenings
  • plan a three-paragraph article about an animal

Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
The task has five questions and eight options. How many are extra? → three (the example counts). If you have used all eight letters, there is already a mistake.
You hear the word monkey and there is an option with the word monkey — do you tick it? → no: meaning decides, and the word may have been said by the other speaker.
What is the second listening for? → for checking, not for starting again.

Assumptions: SB p.16–17, syllabus lesson 7. The animal words are partly familiar, but the spelling (giraffe, penguin) is shaky. This is the first matching task they meet this year — which is why the mechanics are explained before the task, not after.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
They want to talk it over out loud after the first listeningThe silent-mode rule: between the two plays nobody speaks. Discussion happens after the second play only.
They use the same option twiceSay it before the task: every letter is used once. Checking «have I repeated a letter?» is part of the strategy and gets its own line on the board checklist.
The writing turns into a retelling of the panda articleThe bar: three paragraphs on the SB p.17 plan and at least two prepositions from Get ready. Your own animal, not the panda.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.
