🎯 Why this lesson looks like this — the design in one card
Language aim
Give a structured two-minute presentation about a person who changed something — opening, signposted middle, conclusion — using the Speaking bank (I'd like to begin by saying …, First of all, Furthermore, The point I'm trying to make is …, In short); and write an article that involves the reader (direct questions, you/we, «Just imagine …», strong adjectives with the right intensifier).
What is genuinely hard
Both halves fail in the same place: structure is invisible to the student and audible to the listener. Teenagers produce a puddle of facts with no opening and no end, and read their notes with flat intonation. In writing, the problem is register: an article is not an essay and not a chat — it addresses a reader directly and still has paragraphs.
Typical error to expect
«He is very brilliant», «absolutely interesting» — extreme adjectives (brilliant, fantastic, awful) do not take very; they take absolutely, totally, completely. Normal adjectives (good, interesting, bad) take very, rather, extremely. Russian intensifies everything with «очень», so this has to be taught as two lists.
Reading notes aloud, eyes down — an exam mark lost in silence, and the easiest one to recover.
Why the lesson is built this way
Model first, then strip the model for parts, then rehearse, then perform — CELTA's presentation-practice cycle applied to a speaking genre. The writing half deliberately mirrors the speaking half: the same person, the same three facts, a different genre — so the second half costs the class no new content, only new form. Everyone performs; nobody watches eleven presentations in a row, because the first round runs simultaneously in groups of four.
How to announce it (say this, 30–40 seconds):
Two minutes on your feet about one person who made a difference. That is the task, and it is exactly what an examiner asks you to do. You will hear one student do it well, steal his sentence-openers, rehearse once, and then do it. After the break we turn the same person into a written article — same facts, different clothes.

Opening · check-in + rewind
door → desks
Why: Every teenager says one English sentence before the lesson proper starts, and the rewind pulls last lesson's language back into working memory — today's input lands on something.

WHAT WE DO: greeting at the door, then a one-sentence recycling round on last lesson's grammar

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. 2 minutes — welcome check-in. Meet them at the door, one question each, one short answer: Hi! One sentence — what is the best thing that happened to you this week? Any answer counts as long as it is in English.
  2. 3 minutes — rewind, ability and articles. On the board: Name one thing Leonardo was able to do that nobody else could. RallyRobin, then three answers aloud — you correct only the ability form and any missing article.
  3. Bridge into the lesson (30 seconds): You've been talking about people who did extraordinary things. Today you stand up and do it properly — two minutes, no notes to read from. Page 52.
📺 On screen: the prompt on the board. Backup: say it aloud twice; the stage needs nothing else.
👦 Students do: one sentence each in pairs, then three students report.

5–13′
Lead-in · four faces, four reasons to be remembered
desks → groups of three
Why: The content of the presentation is generated before the language of it, so nobody spends rehearsal time deciding who to talk about.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.52 exercises 1–2 — who these people are and what makes someone worth remembering

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Books at p.52. In threes, ninety seconds: Who are these four? Where were they from? When did they live? (Rosa Parks, Alexander Fleming, Amelia Earhart, William Shakespeare.)
  2. Fill the gaps yourself in one line each — Parks refused to give up a bus seat and became a symbol of the civil rights movement; Fleming discovered penicillin; Earhart flew solo across the Atlantic; Shakespeare wrote thirty-nine plays.
  3. Exercise 2: What makes a person worth remembering? Two minutes in threes, then one criterion per group on the board.
  4. Keep the criteria up — students will use them as the argument in their own presentation.
📺 On screen: the class's criteria written up as a list as groups call them out. Backup: the photos are in the book, so nothing depends on a screen.
👦 Students do: identify the four people in threes, then agree one criterion per group and give it to the board.

13–28′
The model · one student's presentation, taken apart
desks
Why: A genre is learned from a model that is then dismantled. The intonation drill is not decoration: flat delivery is the commonest reason a well-planned talk scores badly.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.52 exercises 3–4, recording 21 — listen for the choice and the reason, then harvest the signposts

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. First listening — exercise 3: Who does he choose, and why? Play track 21 once. Answer: track 21 — the student chose Rosa Parks because she brought about a positive change in society by standing up for the rights of black people.
  2. Second listening — exercise 4: tick the expressions you hear. Play again. The harvest: the signposts in the recording: I'd like to begin by saying … · I'm going to talk about … · First of all · Furthermore · It's important to remember that … · Finally · The point I'm trying to make is … · In short.
  3. Ask the question that makes the point: why do we need these expressions at all? What happens without them? (the audience gets lost — you cannot hear paragraphs).
  4. Intonation, three minutes, and do not skip it. Say I'd like to begin by saying … twice: once alive, once in a dead monotone. Which one would you listen to for two minutes? Then choral drill of four expressions, then pairs practise five.
  5. Board the three-part skeleton they will use: SHORT INTRODUCTION — who and why · LONG MIDDLE — what they did and how · SHORT CONCLUSION — why it mattered.
📺 On screen: recording — track 21 in the board app; the skeleton and the harvested expressions written on the board as they are found. Backup: the audio script is in the Teacher's Book (TB p.123) — read the model presentation aloud yourself; it is under two minutes and, honestly, models the intonation better.
👦 Students do: listen twice with different jobs, tick expressions, then drill them aloud in chorus and in pairs.

28–34′
Stirrer · sixty-second rehearsal on the feet
open floor
Why: A stirrer that also recycles the new language. Teenagers who have been sitting for half an hour need to stand before the next block, or the next block dies. It also gets the hardest ten seconds of any presentation — the beginning — out of the way three times before it counts.

WHAT WE DO: everyone stands and delivers only the OPENING of their presentation, to three different people

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Ninety seconds of silent planning: write your first two sentences only. Who and why.
  2. Mix-Pair-Share: mingle, freeze, deliver your opening to the nearest person with a straight back and eye contact, get one word of feedback (alive or flat?), mingle again. Three rounds.
  3. Nobody may read from a page in this stage — two sentences fit in anyone's head.
  4. Stop it on time. This is a rehearsal, not the performance.
📺 On screen: the two-sentence rule on the board. Backup: no equipment.
👦 Students do: stand, deliver an opening three times to three people, and give a one-word verdict each time.

34–48′
🎯 Presentations · everybody speaks, nobody watches eleven in a row
groups of four
Why: Parallel groups of four mean every student speaks for two minutes and listens to three peers, instead of one student speaking while eleven wait. That is the difference between a speaking lesson and a speaking-observation lesson.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.52 exercises 5–6 — prepare, then deliver a two-minute presentation and take a question

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Six minutes of preparation, individually and in silence: notes in bullet points, never sentences. Remind them of the skeleton on the board and of the criteria from the lead-in.
  2. Notes are for glancing at, not for reading. If your eyes are down, your mark is down.
  3. Groups of four. Each student presents for two minutes; the other three listen and must ask one question each at the end. Eight minutes covers a full round of four.
  4. You circulate with a notebook, not a red pen — write down two good phrases and three errors. Do not interrupt anybody.
  5. If the group is small (six or fewer), run it in front of the whole class instead — Teacher's Book advice, and it makes the examiner situation feel normal.
📺 On screen: a visible two-minute timer per group — the group's own phone works. Backup: you time all groups centrally and call «change» aloud.
👦 Students do: present standing, with notes in hand but eyes up; listen to three others and ask a question each time.
notes · timers
48–54′
Feedback · what to keep, what to fix
whole class
Why: Feedback given by peers is remembered; feedback given by the teacher to eleven people in a row is not heard by nine of them.

WHAT WE DO: constructive peer feedback plus two minutes of delayed correction

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. In the group of four: each person gets one thing they did well and one specific thing to change. Specific means «look up more», not «be better». Two minutes.
  2. Board the three errors you collected and let the class fix them — anonymously, no names.
  3. Board two good phrases you heard, with the speaker's name. That is the currency teenagers actually spend.
  4. One-line exam note, said once and lightly: this is Cambridge Speaking Part 2 and it's the monologue in the Russian oral. Same skeleton, both of them.
📺 On screen: the errors and the two good phrases written on the board. Backup: no equipment.
👦 Students do: give and receive one specific improvement each, then fix three sentences as a class.

54–68′
Developing writing · the article that keeps a reader
desks → pairs
Why: An article is a genre with visible moves. Naming the four moves and finding them in a real text is what makes the students' own articles more than a biography.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.53 exercises 1–4 — the Jürgen Klopp model, then the four techniques named and found

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Exercise 1 in pairs, two minutes: who would you invite to your school, and why? Encourage the opinion phrases from Unit 3 (p.41).
  2. Exercise 2: read the model article about Jürgen Klopp fast — who is he and why did the writer choose him? Check the idiom in passing: he's a live wire — from Lesson 1, and they should recognise it.
  3. Exercise 3 (matching paragraph functions), three minutes. Answers: 1 D · 2 A · 3 B · 4 C.
  4. Exercise 4 — the four techniques. Read the Writing bank list first, then hunt for examples in the model. Findings: the Klopp article uses ALL four techniques: you/your/we/our («Whether you like football or not …»), a direct question («Have you ever heard of the football manager Jürgen Klopp?»), a connection with the reader («Just imagine how somebody like that could inspire all of us …») and colourful language «he's absolutely brilliant»; opinion is marked with «Let me explain why».
  5. Extreme adjectives, four minutes, on the board. Two columns: normal (good, interesting, boring, bad) and extreme (brilliant, fantastic, awesome, fascinating, awful, terrible). Then the intensifiers: very/rather/extremely + normal; totally/absolutely/completely + extreme; really goes with both. Drill six pairs orally: very good — absolutely brilliant.
  6. Mixed ability: mixed pairs list all the reasons the writer gives for inviting Klopp and rank them best to worst.
📺 On screen: the two-column adjective table on the board — it stays up for the writing stage. Backup: the same distinction is in the Writing bank on SB p.53 if the board is full.
👦 Students do: read the model for gist, match functions, hunt for four techniques, then drill intensifier pairs aloud.

68–80′
Writing · plan and first paragraphs, in class
desks
Why: Writing started in class gets the hardest part — the first paragraph — done where a teacher is standing. What goes home is continuation, not invention.

WHAT WE DO: SB p.53 exercise 5a — the plan and the opening, written here where help is available

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. Board the paragraph plan and make them copy it: 1 introduction · 2 character and what you like · 3 what they did · 4 conclusion with your opinion.
  2. Three minutes: plan only — the topic of each paragraph and two ideas beside it. No sentences yet. Circulate and check plans, not English.
  3. Nine minutes of silent writing: paragraph 1 and paragraph 2 only. Your homework is the rest. Two requirements on the board: one direct question and one «Just imagine …».
  4. Circulate with two questions only: where is your direct question? which adjective would be stronger?
  5. Two minutes before the end: Rally Coach swap — partner reads only for the two required techniques and ticks them in the margin.
📺 On screen: the paragraph plan and the two requirements on the board. Backup: the same plan is printed in the Workbook on p.37, which they take home.
👦 Students do: plan in note form, write two paragraphs in silence, then check a partner's for the two required techniques.

80–86′
Adjective upgrade · the last six minutes are the loudest
pairs → whole class
Why: A six-minute editing stage teaches more about adjectives than a six-minute presentation of them, because the text being fixed is their own.

WHAT WE DO: every student replaces two weak adjectives in their own opening paragraph with a strong one and its correct intensifier

👩‍🏫 Teacher does — step by step:
  1. On the board, two columns from the earlier stage stay visible: normal adjectives with very / rather / extremely, extreme adjectives with absolutely / totally / completely.
  2. Instruction: find two adjectives in your own paragraph. Cross them out. Write something stronger — and check the intensifier goes with it. Three minutes, in silence.
  3. RallyRobin: read your two new phrases to your partner. The partner's only job is to say yes or wrong intensifier.
  4. Take five upgrades for the whole class and write them up: very good → absolutely brilliant · quite interesting → completely fascinating.
  5. One warning worth thirty seconds: do not upgrade everything. If every adjective is «absolutely amazing», none of them is.
📺 On screen: the two adjective columns and the five class upgrades on the board. Backup: the same lists are in the Writing bank on SB p.53.
👦 Students do: edit their own text, then check a partner's two phrases for the intensifier.

Workbook p.35–36
📋 Preparation reference — read BEFORE the lesson, not in class
  • Main aim. By the end of the lesson students will be better able to give a structured two-minute presentation about a person and to write an article that involves the reader, in the context of people who made a difference.
  • Subsidiary. Listening to a model for structure; intonation and stress in signposting phrases; normal versus extreme adjectives and their intensifiers.
  • Personal. Do not interrupt a presentation. Write the error down, keep your face neutral, and deal with it in the two-minute correction slot.
By the end, students can…
  • structure a two-minute talk with an opening, signposts and a conclusion
  • deliver it with eye contact and live intonation instead of reading
  • ask a follow-up question after a peer's presentation
  • name the four techniques that involve a reader in an article
  • use absolutely/totally with extreme adjectives and very/rather with normal ones

Structure
I'd like to begin by saying … · First of all … Furthermore … · The point I'm trying to make is … · In short, … · Have you ever heard of …? Just imagine how somebody like that could inspire all of us.
Form
signposting phrases in fixed position (opening / middle / closing) · direct questions to the reader (Have you ever …?) · you, your, we, our for reader involvement · very/rather/extremely + normal adjective; totally/absolutely/completely + extreme adjective.
Pron. watch
presentation intonation: the Speaking bank phrases must rise and fall, or the whole talk flatlines. Model «I'd like to begin by saying …» twice — once alive, once dead — and let the class hear the difference. Then drill the stress: First of ALL · FURtherMORE · the POINT I'm trying to make
Concept checks (CCQs) & ICQs · Assumptions
signposts: If I say «Furthermore», is the next idea new or the same? — New, and it supports the last one.
extreme adjectives: Can something be «very brilliant»? — No. Why not? — Brilliant already means very good.
reader involvement: Who is «you» in an article? — The reader. Does it make the text closer or further away? — Closer.
ICQ before the presentations: How long? Who asks questions? Do you read your notes or glance at them?

Assumptions: Students have given short prepared talks before and know basic linkers (first, then, finally). They have not been taught presentation intonation, and the article as a genre is new — they will default to an essay.

Anticipated problems & solutions
Likely problemWhat you do
Students read their notes word for word and speak to the desk.The rule is set before preparation, not after: bullet points only, and the stirrer has already made them deliver an opening three times without paper. If someone still reads, stand behind their group so they have to look up.
A round of eleven presentations kills the room.It never happens: the first round runs in parallel groups of four. Only invite two volunteers to repeat theirs for the whole class if time allows.
The two-minute limit is ignored in both directions.Each group times itself, and the instruction «speak for exactly the right amount of time» is a Cambridge criterion — say that once, in one sentence, and then hold the timer.
The article turns into a Wikipedia biography.The two requirements on the board are non-negotiable: one direct question and one «Just imagine …». The Rally Coach check finds them before the piece goes home.
Weaker students have nothing to say about anyone famous.The Writing bank task allows «someone close to you» — a grandmother, a coach, a neighbour. Say that option aloud at the start of preparation, not when someone is already stuck.
Classroom management — signals
“Eyes up in 3-2-1” — a calm countdown → on “1” every pair of eyes is on you. Never talk over the class.
Visible timer — timer running = we work; timer stops = pens down. The timer is the boss, not you.
“Boards up!” — a mini-whiteboard round: everyone answers at the same moment — nobody hides, nobody shouts out first.
Silent mode — palm down = we work alone and in silence: listening or writing. Questions afterwards, not during.
One clap — two claps — you clap once, the class claps twice and freezes — an instant reset in the middle of a game.
English-first — an idea may be started in Russian, but the POINT is only given for the English version: reformulate and repeat it.

🇷🇺 Where this lands in the EGE (Russian state exam, grades 10–11) — teacher's note, never taken into class

The same skill is tested by: Task 37 (the personal email) and tasks 39–40 (the oral monologue).

The presentation frame the class builds today — an opening line, signposts, a closing line — is the same skeleton the Russian oral monologue is marked on: content answering every prompt, logical organisation and a conclusion. The article half feeds task 37: involving the reader, giving an opinion and justifying it are the K2 and K3 criteria of the written paper.

Who needs this: not every student in the group will sit the EGE. Those who will get the trainer link in the homework as an optional extra. The rest are told nothing — the lesson works as language teaching without it.
